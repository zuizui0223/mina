Dear Editor-in-Chief,

Please consider our manuscript, **“Breeding-space contraction recurs across spatial scales in Antarctic penguins,”** for publication as an **Article** in *Ecology*.

Population decline is usually summarized by how many individuals remain, yet abundance does not uniquely determine the spatial organization of a structured population. Our manuscript asks a stricter ecological question: **after conditioning on the observed abundance trajectory, does reproduction become redistributed toward fewer effective breeding components, and does that direction persist when the spatial component is defined at a larger hierarchical scale?**

We address the first part with fixed-composition null models that preserve the empirical population-total trajectory while holding expected relative breeding-component shares constant. This design incorporates the mechanical tendency for low-count components to disappear as populations shrink. In three Adélie penguin populations near Palmer Station, effective breeding-component number declined by 19–83% beyond the complete frozen count-error null family.

The key replication evidence comes from Signy Island. Before computing the relevant concentration effects, we froze the same effective-component endpoint in an independent Adélie panel and then in a separately specified chinstrap penguin panel. Both prospective tests supported breeding-space contraction under the full prespecified error family, including the most severe 20% multiplicative-CV sensitivity (Adélie p = 0.000010; chinstrap p = 0.000020).

We then tested whether the **direction**, rather than a universal effect size, persisted one level higher in the spatial hierarchy using an already-pinned Antarctic Penguin Biogeography Project data snapshot. Species × published APBP regions were treated as monitored networks and repeatedly monitored breeding sites as components under a coverage-only roster rule frozen before regional concentration outcomes were computed. Four eligible regional networks were declining; all four had positive observation-error-calibrated abundance–concentration effects. The South Shetland Adélie and chinstrap networks were individually supported under both frozen regional nulls (p = 0.0148 and 0.0152). We do not treat these panel-level probabilities as a family-wide regional significance test, and we do not describe the MAPPPD extension as an additional independent geographic replication of Palmer. Signy provides independent geographic replication; MAPPPD provides the cross-scale transfer test.

The contribution is therefore not the established observation that abundance and occupancy can covary. It is an abundance-conditioned demonstration that **relative reproductive allocation can reorganize beyond proportional thinning**, followed by prospective geographic and cross-species replication and a bounded test of whether the same direction persists across spatial levels. The result also places a useful boundary on generalization: the direction transfers more consistently than the magnitude, and Palmer and Signy reach concentration through different component-level routes.

We believe the Article format is appropriate because the manuscript now combines distinct but linked inferential components—within-system discovery, prospective replication, and a regional scale-transfer analysis—while retaining one ecological question. We deliberately do not claim a universal scaling exponent, a seabird-wide law, regional hysteresis, individual movement, or a single habitat or behavioral mechanism.

The manuscript is approximately 4,400 words before the full reference list, has a 256-word abstract, and contains three main figures plus one supplementary descriptive figure. All primary data sources are public. Frozen analysis contracts, source checksums, result receipts, reviewer-facing claim boundaries, and reproducible figure workflows are available in the accompanying public repository.

Thank you for considering the manuscript.
