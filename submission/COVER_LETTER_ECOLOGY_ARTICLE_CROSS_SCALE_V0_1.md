Dear Editor-in-Chief,

Please consider our manuscript, **“Breeding-space concentration recurs across spatial scales in Antarctic penguins,”** for publication as an **Article** in *Ecology*.

Population change is usually summarized by how many individuals are gained or lost, yet abundance does not uniquely determine the spatial organization of a structured population. Our manuscript asks a stricter ecological question: **after conditioning on the observed abundance trajectory, does reproduction become redistributed toward fewer effective breeding components, and is that concentration specific to population decline?**

We address the first part with fixed-composition null models that preserve the empirical population-total trajectory while holding expected relative breeding-component shares constant. This design incorporates the mechanical tendency for low-count components to disappear as populations shrink. In three Adélie penguin populations near Palmer Station, effective breeding-component number declined by 19–83% beyond the complete frozen count-error null family.

The key replication evidence comes from Signy Island. Before computing the relevant concentration effects, we froze the same effective-component endpoint in an independent Adélie panel and then in a separately specified chinstrap penguin panel. Both prospective tests supported breeding-space contraction under the full prespecified error family, including the most severe 20% multiplicative-CV sensitivity (Adélie p = 0.000010; chinstrap p = 0.000020).

We then moved one level higher in the spatial hierarchy using an already-pinned Antarctic Penguin Biogeography Project snapshot. Among four declining species × region networks, all four had positive observation-error-calibrated abundance–concentration effects, with individual support in South Shetland Adélie and chinstrap networks (p = 0.0148 and 0.0152). Crucially, however, all three eligible **increasing** regional networks also ended with lower effective breeding-site number. In Victoria Land Adélie and Central-west Antarctic Peninsula gentoo, abundance increased while effective site number fell, reversing the sign of the abundance–space elasticity.

This result changes the interpretation of the scale-transfer analysis. MAPPPD does not provide a simple second-scale replication of a decline-specific contraction mechanism. Instead, it shows that while abundance-linked concentration recurs within the declining regional subset, regional breeding-space concentration itself is not uniquely associated with decline. The increasing panels are sparse and no formal trend-asymmetry test was frozen, so we do not claim hysteresis or a trend-independent law.

The contribution is therefore not the established observation that abundance and occupancy can covary. It is an abundance-conditioned demonstration that **relative reproductive allocation can reorganize beyond proportional thinning**, followed by prospective geographic and cross-species replication, plus a broader-scale boundary showing that concentration cannot be interpreted as a simple consequence of decline. The result separates population magnitude from breeding-space organization rather than proposing a universal abundance–space exponent.

We believe the Article format is appropriate because the manuscript combines distinct but linked inferential components—within-system discovery, prospective replication, and a regional scale-transfer analysis—while retaining one ecological question. We deliberately do not claim a universal scaling exponent, a seabird-wide law, regional hysteresis, individual movement, or a single habitat or behavioral mechanism.

**1. Contribution to the broad field of ecology.** The manuscript addresses a general population-ecology problem: whether population abundance is sufficient to describe spatial population state. By conditioning explicitly on empirical abundance trajectories, the analyses distinguish non-proportional spatial reorganization from the mechanical consequences of having fewer individuals. Prospective geographic and cross-species replication, followed by a deliberately bounded scale-transfer test, connects density-dependent distribution, habitat selection, population monitoring, and the spatial dynamics of decline. The central inference is therefore about how structured populations change in space, rather than about penguins as a taxonomic special case.
