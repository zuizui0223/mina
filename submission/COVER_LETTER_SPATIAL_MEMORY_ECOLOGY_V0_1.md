# Cover letter draft — Ecology Article v0.1

Dear Editor,

Please consider our manuscript, **“Population change can suppress, erode or redirect breeding-space organization,”** for publication as an Article in *Ecology*.

Ecological recovery is often evaluated from aggregate abundance, yet spatially structured populations can retain or lose organization independently of that total. Existing work has emphasized one side of this problem: aggregate recovery can hide persistent compositional or patch-level collapse. We use long-term penguin breeding datasets to expose the converse risk and to ask what kinds of population change preserve or rewrite spatial organization.

A documented mega-iceberg disturbance on Ross Island reduced observed Adélie penguin breeding abundance by 54.3%. At the next census, 96.97% of the aggregate loss had returned, and the rebound closely retraced the six-component spatial footprint of the preceding loss (cosine similarity 0.997; 9.41% inverse-path allocation mismatch). Thus a severe census collapse did not correspond to equivalent loss of the spatial allocation that could subsequently be re-expressed.

We contrast this natural experiment with two other process classes using a common proportional spatial counterfactual. Persistent Adélie declines at Palmer and Signy departed from proportional thinning and concentrated breeding abundance among fewer effective units. At Beaufort Island, increased nesting capacity was accompanied by growth of a small/new breeding unit 3.15 times greater than proportional expectation and by independent evidence of reduced movement toward nearby Ross Island colonies. Additional prospectively frozen boundary cases show that the direction of aggregate abundance change alone does not determine the direction of spatial reallocation.

We do not claim a universal causal law or a common effect size across these heterogeneous natural experiments. Instead, the manuscript establishes a distinction relevant to recovery ecology, metapopulation ecology, and monitoring: **what disappears from a breeding census is not always what disappears from the breeding network.** The biological process underlying abundance change determines which spatial counterfactual is appropriate and whether prior spatial organization can plausibly be re-expressed.

The manuscript is submitted as an Article because its ecological conclusion depends on several linked empirical components rather than a single concise result. All source datasets are public or publicly archived, the inferential status of prospective and post-result analyses is explicitly reported, and no overlapping Palmer or Ross manuscript is being submitted elsewhere while this integrated manuscript is active.

Thank you for your consideration.
