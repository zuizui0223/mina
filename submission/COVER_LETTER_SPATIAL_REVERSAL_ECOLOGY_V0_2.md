# Cover letter — Ecology Report spatial reversibility paper v0.2

Dear Editors,

Please consider our manuscript, **“Breeding rebound largely retraces spatial loss after a mega-iceberg disturbance,”** for publication as a **Report** in *Ecology*.

Population recovery is usually summarized by whether abundance returns. We ask a spatially explicit version of that question: after a shared disturbance, how closely does breeding abundance return to the places from which it was lost?

Ross Island provides a documented natural experiment. A mega-iceberg disturbance reduced breeding abundance across six monitored Adélie penguin breeding components from 207,411 pairs in 1999 to 94,798 in 2001. By 2002, 96.97% of the aggregate loss had returned. Spatial recovery was also strikingly strong: the loss and rebound vectors were nearly collinear (cosine = 0.997), and only 9.41% of rebound abundance would have to be reallocated among components to reproduce exact proportional reversal. The remaining excess was concentrated at Cape Crozier West.

We calibrate this result conservatively. The 9.41% mismatch is moderate and comparable to other Ross down–up episodes, so we do not present the mega-iceberg event as an exceptional compositional anomaly. Its ecological value is that a severe, independently documented disturbance produced a rapid rebound whose spatial pattern could be compared directly with the preceding loss.

The broader inference is tested prospectively rather than inferred from Ross alone. In a separately frozen six-unit Gentoo penguin comparison at Bird Island, aggregate breeding abundance increased 34.2% while effective breeding-unit number increased 15.7%. In a prospectively frozen 50-colony emperor penguin comparison, aggregate abundance declined 12.4% while effective colony number declined 12.8%, but prespecified regions occupied all four combinations of aggregate and spatial directional change. These tests show that the high reversibility observed at Ross cannot be inferred from the sign of aggregate change alone.

We also report clear inferential boundaries. The focal 1999–2002 Ross decomposition was developed after an earlier frozen recovery prediction failed, and the manuscript labels it explicitly as a post-result refinement. Prospectively specified mechanism tests using reproductive output and mapped subcolony configuration did not provide a confirmed general predictor, so the paper remains a state/allocation study rather than a mechanism claim.

We believe the Report format is appropriate because the manuscript makes one concise ecological contribution: **a severe breeding disturbance was followed by rapid, largely reversible spatial recovery, while prospective external tests show that aggregate change alone does not determine spatial reallocation.** The analyses use public datasets and version-controlled code, and the final submission will provide permanent archival access to the exact derived data products and novel code needed for reproduction.
