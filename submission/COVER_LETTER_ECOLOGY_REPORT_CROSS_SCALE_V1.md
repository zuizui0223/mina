Dear Editor-in-Chief,

Please consider our manuscript, **“Breeding-space contraction recurs across spatial scales in Antarctic penguins,”** for publication as a **Report** in *Ecology*.

Population decline is commonly accompanied by loss of occupied sites, but that relationship alone cannot distinguish ecological redistribution from the mechanical disappearance of low-count sites as abundance falls. Our manuscript makes one ecological statement: **after conditioning on the observed abundance trajectory, declining colonial populations can still become disproportionately concentrated into fewer effective breeding components, and that direction can persist across spatial levels.**

We test this with a fixed-composition null in which every observed annual population total is retained while expected relative shares among monitored breeding components are held constant. The null therefore already includes abundance-dependent loss of small components. In three Adélie penguin populations near Palmer Station, effective breeding-component number declined by 19–83% beyond the complete frozen count-error family.

Palmer is explicitly treated as the discovery system. The stronger evidence comes from Signy Island, where the same endpoint was frozen before effect computation. The independent Adélie test showed a 37% decline in effective monitored components and the separately frozen chinstrap test a 51% decline; both remained more extreme than the severe 20%-CV sensitivity (p = 0.000010 and 0.000020).

We then asked a bounded cross-scale question using an already-pinned APBP/MAPPPD archive. We redefined components as monitored breeding sites within published regional networks, froze a coverage-only eligibility rule before regional concentration outcomes, and reused previously frozen observation handling. All four estimable declining regional networks had positive observation-error-calibrated abundance–concentration effects; South Shetland Adélie and chinstrap panels were individually supported. We do not present these four panels as four independent geographic replications or as a family-wide rejection test. Signy supplies independent geographic replication; MAPPPD tests whether the **direction** survives a change in spatial level.

The contribution is not a new abundance–occupancy relationship, a universal scaling exponent, or a penguin-specific claim that large colonies survive. It is a decomposition of population decline into **numerical loss versus change in relative spatial composition** under an abundance-conditioned null. That distinction is relevant wherever populations are monitored as repeated spatial components but later summarized only by aggregate totals.

We believe the manuscript fits *Ecology*’s Report format because it is organized around one concise ecological result, separates discovery from prospective replication and bounded scale transfer, and deliberately moves post-hoc scaling and mechanism descriptions to Supporting Information.
