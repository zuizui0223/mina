Dear Editor-in-Chief,

Please consider our manuscript, **“Breeding-space contraction recurs across declining Adélie and chinstrap penguins,”** for publication as a **Report** in *Ecology*.

Population decline is usually summarized by how many individuals are lost. Our study asks a distinct spatial question: when abundance declines, does a colonial population simply thin in fixed proportion across established breeding components, or does reproduction become concentrated into fewer effective components?

We developed a proportional-thinning null that preserves each observed population-abundance trajectory while holding the latent relative distribution among monitored breeding components constant. In three Adélie penguin populations near Palmer Station, effective breeding-component number declined by 19–83% more rapidly than expected under this null and frozen count-error sensitivities. Because Palmer was the discovery system and its public colony-code archive does not provide a versioned spatial-boundary history for every code, we treat those units conservatively as monitored census colonies rather than fixed GIS polygons.

The confirmatory evidence comes from Signy Island. Before computing the relevant effects, we froze the same endpoint in an independent Adélie panel and then in a separate chinstrap penguin panel. Both prospectively frozen tests supported breeding-space contraction under the complete prespecified error family, including a 20% multiplicative-CV sensitivity (Adélie p = 0.000010; chinstrap p = 0.000020).

The ecological contribution is therefore not the already-known fact that penguin colonies can decline or disappear. It is that **abundance loss and spatial contraction are empirically distinct population-state changes**: across two Antarctic systems and two *Pygoscelis* species, reproduction became concentrated into fewer effective monitored components even after the observed abundance trajectory was held fixed. We frame this as a within-population analogue of the broader ecological problem of abundance decline versus range contraction.

We believe this fits *Ecology*’s Report format because the manuscript makes one concise ecological statement, is supported by a discovery system followed by prospective geographic and cross-species replications, and is explicit about the limits of inference. We do not identify a single behavioural or habitat mechanism, infer individual movement, or equate census codes with physical polygons.

The manuscript is approximately 2,000 words before the full reference list, has a 149-word abstract, and contains two main figures plus one supplementary descriptive figure. All primary source data are public. Frozen analysis contracts, provenance records, result receipts and reproducible figure scripts are available in the accompanying public repository. The older integrated manuscript line has been formally superseded to avoid duplicate publication of the Palmer result.
