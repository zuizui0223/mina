Dear Editor,

Please consider our manuscript, **“Population recovery can retrace local losses while spatial structure remains altered,”** for publication as an Article in *Ecology*.

When an island breeding population rebounds, does its total abundance—and even a close reversal of where losses occurred—mean that the distribution of breeding effort among local sites has been restored? This distinction matters for colonial animals whose island totals may conceal persistent changes in spatial dominance and the balance of breeding activity among colonies. It is already recognized in ecological theory; our contribution is a quantitative field demonstration from a documented Antarctic disturbance and a set of independent ecological contrasts.

Ross Island Adélie penguin breeding abundance fell 54.3% between 1999 and 2001 during a major iceberg disturbance; the 2002 census restored **96.97%** of that aggregate loss. Yet restoration of the preceding local losses varied from **10.8% to 109.8%** across six breeding components (median **51.8%**), and the dominant Cape Crozier West component accounted for **80.6%** of the rebound. Although the local gain vector substantially retraced the geography of loss (**90.59%** inverse-path fidelity), only **2.4%** of the baseline-to-trough displacement in relative breeding composition was erased at the six-component grain. At the three-colony grain, the corresponding path-fidelity and state-restoration values were **93.27%** and **28.3%**. The effect is scale dependent, but the qualitative discrepancy is not: the observed endpoint remained **4.97%** and **3.54%** total-variation distance from baseline at the two grains, whereas exact proportional reversal at the observed aggregate rebound would have placed it approximately **0.07%** from baseline.

Importantly, **all six focal breeding components remained occupied throughout the three census years**. The Ross finding is not a newly observed local extinction or recolonization event, nor evidence of individual dispersal. Instead, nearly complete aggregate recovery emerged from **unequal recovery among already occupied local breeding units**, with strong geographic alignment but incomplete restoration of their previous relative importance.

We place this natural experiment beside two distinct biological contrasts rather than treating them as independent replications of the same shock. Persistent Adélie declines at Palmer and Signy were accompanied by breeding concentration beyond proportional thinning under the prespecified count-error models. At Beaufort Island, a smaller/new unit grew disproportionately after nesting habitat became more available; prior band-resighting research also documented changes in inter-island sightings. Neither contrast identifies the causes of Ross recovery or proves a general island-specific mechanism.

The broader message for disturbance and spatial population ecology is that **island-wide recovery can mask a redistribution of breeding activity among local units, even if loss and rebound occur in much the same places**. Recovery assessments should therefore report both population totals and changes in the relative composition of persistently monitored sites. Our path-versus-state reinterpretation and grain comparison are transparently identified as post-result, without a confirmatory significance claim or a new resilience-theory claim.

Original monitoring datasets are publicly identified by persistent DOIs, and the novel analytical source code and frozen results are available for peer review in the public version-controlled repository: https://github.com/zuizui0223/mina/tree/analysis/ross-sea-expansion-intensification-support-v1.
