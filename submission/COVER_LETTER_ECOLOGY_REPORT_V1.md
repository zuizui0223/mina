Dear Editor-in-Chief,

Please consider our manuscript, **“Breeding-space contraction recurs across declining Adélie and chinstrap penguins,”** for publication as a **Report** in *Ecology*.

Population decline is usually summarized by how many individuals are lost. Our study asks a different but general ecological question: when abundance declines, does the spatial organization of a population simply thin in proportion, or does reproduction become concentrated into fewer occupied components?

We test this question in long-term colonial penguin populations using a proportional-thinning null that preserves each observed abundance trajectory while fixing the latent relative distribution among breeding components. In three Adélie penguin island populations near Palmer Station, effective breeding-component number declined by 19–83% more rapidly than expected under proportional thinning plus frozen count-error models. We then froze the same endpoint before effect computation in an independent Signy Island Adélie data set, where the result replicated, and in a separately frozen chinstrap penguin test, where it replicated across species. The most severe prespecified 20% multiplicative-CV error model remained strongly inconsistent with both Signy trends.

The result is broader than colony loss itself, which is already documented in these systems. It shows that total abundance and spatial contraction are distinct dimensions of population decline. A post-hoc descriptive decomposition also sets an informative mechanistic boundary: contraction occurred through dominance turnover in the Palmer populations but through retention of the initially dominant breeding component in both Signy species. Thus the population-level spatial endpoint converged while the component-level route did not.

We believe the manuscript fits *Ecology*’s emphasis on concise papers documenting important ecological phenomena and producing generalizations applicable beyond a focal species or system. The study connects colonial population dynamics to the broader ecological problem of abundance loss versus spatial contraction, while remaining explicit about its inferential limits. It does not claim that a single behavioural or habitat mechanism causes the pattern.

The manuscript is approximately 2,100 words before the full reference list, has a 173-word abstract, and contains three main figures. All primary source data are public. Frozen analysis contracts, provenance records, result receipts and reproducible figure scripts are available in the accompanying repository. The work is not under consideration elsewhere, and the older integrated manuscript line in the repository has been formally superseded to avoid duplicate publication of the Palmer result.

Thank you for considering the manuscript.
