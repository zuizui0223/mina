# Environmental exposure and spatial reorganization in Antarctic colonial marine vertebrates
**Antarctic integrated working draft v0.6 — 26 November 2026; author review only.** Alternative to the narrower seabird-network manuscript, not an approved replacement for it or PR214/189. No author order assigned. No submission, merge, or new causal estimate.

## Abstract
The environmental conditions of breeding locations and the distribution of breeding counts jointly determine count-weighted physical exposure. Using fixed Adélie penguin breeding-site rosters, we separated changes in environmental levels from changes in exposure to historical and projected physical anomalies. Between 1993–2000 and 2011–2020, redistribution contributed +2.25 percentage points to weighted sea-ice levels across 23 sites (+5.58 in a nested 17-site subset with complete hydrography). However, the same fixed early and recent allocations produced opposite historical and late-century SSP5-8.5 mean sea-ice change-field contrasts in all four overlapping rosters. For the long 23-site roster, the projected between-region contribution (+0.44 points) was offset by the within-region contribution (−0.58); six sites lacking historical hydrographic coverage accounted for 49.4–60.1% of the recorded site-median sums. This reveals sensitivity to geographic composition rather than demonstrating adaptive redistribution. Separate observation systems showed a negative association of winter coastal polynya with the subsequent optical nitrogen-isotope proxy, heterogeneous reproductive associations, and differing changes in penguin breeding and seal haul-out concentration. Seal detections at Palmer overlapped mapped vegetation more than terrain-matched availability, and mapped vegetation at Shirley overlapped part of the historical relic breeding footprint. Direct longitudinal joins of optical and nest observations, and of contemporary marine, nest and vegetation data, failed support criteria. Together, these results distinguish physical exposure, spatial allocation and ecological associations, without establishing a causal marine-to-land pathway or a population forecast.


## Introduction
Colonial marine vertebrates link spatially distributed marine resource environments to discrete breeding or haul-out locations on land. Climate-driven changes in sea ice, open-water access, water masses and food availability can modify the marine conditions surrounding these locations. Yet regional changes in observed breeding abundance can also modify the distribution of animals across those conditions. The environmental change at a location and the change in an abundance-weighted environmental summary are therefore different quantities. In the terrestrial part of the system, changes in where animals are counted need not correspond to changes in total population size, and different taxa can contribute different disturbance and nutrient pathways.

Historical niche tracking is already a developed research question. Cohen and Jetz (2025) evaluated incomplete climatic exposure mitigation through redistribution across North American birds, and Avidad et al. (2025) distinguished environmental and distributional contributions to changes in realized niches. For Antarctic penguins, Cimino et al. (2016) projected spatially heterogeneous breeding-habitat change, while Iles et al. (2020) showed that prevailing sea ice explains long-term population trends more readily than annual fluctuations. Youngflesh et al. (2026) already linked sea ice, satellite-derived dietary variation and abundance trends using the optical archive reanalyzed here. Marine-to-land nutrient transfer and biodiversity footprints are also established (Bokhorst et al., 2019). We therefore do not claim discovery of climate tracking, future penguin vulnerability, optical dietary monitoring or marine–terrestrial coupling. Our narrower question is whether the same observed colony allocations have comparable consequences for exposure to different marine variables and to historical versus projected physical changes, holding each physical field fixed in turn.

We distinguish four questions that can be addressed with different original observation systems: (1) How does geographic reweighting of observed breeding counts alter environmental exposure summaries? (2) Do winter marine conditions have associations with different biological response variables? (3) Are numerical and spatial configuration changes interchangeable within and among colonial taxa? (4) What direct evidence connects the locations of animals and terrestrial photosynthetic cover? The primary contribution is the same-roster historical and projected exposure comparison (Figs. 1–3). The other observation systems (Figs. 4–6) show what can and cannot be inferred about biological and terrestrial associations. The questions are linked by spatial allocation, not an assumed chain of causal coefficients. We explicitly evaluate whether the same nominal site and season support a direct longitudinal bridge. We do not claim novel mathematical indices, new field collection, or a verified mechanism common to all taxa.

## Methods and evidence design
### Shared accounting framework
For nonnegative count summaries n_it on a fixed set of sites, define N_t=sum_i n_it and p_it=n_it/N_t. Concentration H_t=sum_i p_it², effective component number 1/H_t, and total variation TV=0.5 sum_i |p_i1−p_i0| quantify different features of the composition. For a location-specific physical exposure x_it, E_t=sum_i p_it x_it. Following the algebraic form of the existing rate/composition decomposition of Kitagawa (1955), the exact symmetric decomposition is
ΔE = Σ_i [(p_i0+p_i1)/2](x_i1−x_i0) + Σ_i [(x_i0+x_i1)/2](p_i1−p_i0).
The first term is the fixed-location physical-change component and the second is the reweighting component. For a specified projected physical anomaly f_i, the difference between fixed recent and early allocation summaries is Σ_i (p_i1−p_i0) f_i. The historical reweighting term and this fixed future-field contrast are different estimands. To align the two change-field estimands explicitly, we additionally calculated A = Σ_i (p_i1−p_i0)(x_i1−x_i0), the difference between recent-weight and early-weight summaries of the identical historical physical change field. A equals R1−R0, where R_t = Σ_i(p_i1−p_i0)x_it, whereas historical midpoint-level reweighting is (R0+R1)/2. Thus the sign of the midpoint-level term need not match the sign of A or F. We evaluated all 16 originally available exposure rows without changing the site rosters, and calculated leave-one-reporting-region-out values of A when both remaining totals were positive and at least two sites remained. Historical and projected anomaly intervals and products differ, so the historical-versus-future contrast is descriptive, not a calibrated transfer test. We compare SIC and salinity only where the original historical field exists; historical SST is not projected air temperature, and future polynya was not modeled. For each fixed historical change or projected anomaly field f, we further separated the allocation contrast into between-region and within-region terms, using the same symmetric composition identity. With regional share P_tg and conditional mean field μ_tg, the terms are Σ_g (P_1g−P_0g)(μ_0g+μ_1g)/2 and Σ_g (P_0g+P_1g)(μ_1g−μ_0g)/2. The total is exactly Δp·f. We repeated this identity for the two groups defined by historical hydrographic coverage, allowing the parent-minus-complete contrast to be separated into retained-weight renormalization, excluded-group internal reweighting, and changing shares of the two groups. This retrospective arithmetic audit used the unchanged 16 historical exposure rows and all 144 model-specific future contrasts; the primary illustrative scenario was fixed as SSP5-8.5 late-century SIC before computing the new components. These are identities, not estimated effects of climate on animal abundance. The long-period nest weights are site-specific noncontemporaneous medians (not simultaneous population totals), so integer count-noise correction is not appropriate. In separate actual-count panels we use the existing Bray–Curtis replacement component C=2 min(G,L)/(N0+N1) (Legendre 2014), where G and L are summed absolute local gains and losses. We never pool these metrics as equivalent independent samples across incompatible grains.

### Source systems and fixed supports
The marine atlas contains 595 known breeding locations. A 588-site, 1993–2020 physical panel provides Aug–Nov sea-ice concentration, surface salinity and temperature (378 GLORYS wet cells), and the DEEP-AA daily-polynya product (Lin et al., 2024; version 1.1.0-2023) provides June–Oct native coastal-polynya fractions at 25 and 100 km for 15 complete winters (2004–10, 2013–20). Missing winters are not filled. These are nominal near-colony exposure measures, not tracked foraging paths. The long Adélie comparison uses the archived 23-site ice roster and nested 17-site complete hydrographic roster; the shorter comparison uses 15 sites and nine complete sites. All source selection, counts, seasons and archive hashes are inherited from the prior audited analysis, rather than selected anew for direction. The long comparison uses 1993–2000 and 2011–2020, and the shorter comparison 2004–10 and 2013–20.

The separate food-web module reuses the original Youngflesh et al. (2026) archive (not independent biological observations) and uses 439 summer optical isotope-proxy records at 85 sites with antecedent winter polynya exposure. The primary conditional model includes site and region-season effects, summer sea ice, summer SST and image date; salinity adjustment, source calibration ensemble, region deletion, temporal comparison and Mertz event diagnostics follow the existing frozen specification. This is an optical proxy analysis, not a direct assay of prey composition. Reproductive output and juvenile mass are separate archived response series from BAS, BCO-DMO and NOAA-derived source datasets, whose denominator, season and spatial support were audited independently. We summarize the original fitted results and their failed transfer or adjustment checks without multiplying coefficients across systems.

The terrestrial allocation module uses fixed breeding-count panels at Palmer, Signy and Ross, and fixed matched-summer count contrasts between fur seals and penguins at Signy. Haul-out zone counts and penguin nest counts are distinct units. The JNCC paired-census comparison of 17 taxa supplies an external taxonomic benchmark, not a climate or Antarctic geographic replication. Plantarctica (Walshaw et al., 2024) is a static 2017–2023 10-m composite; mapped vegetation and lichen are not annual measured plant cover, and mapped non-detection is not verified absence. Palmer uses the original actual 2020 fur-seal detections from Larsen et al. (2025) and matched terrain availability within survey areas; Shirley compares original 2005/06 colony-status polygons against the later vegetation composite. These separate spatial contrasts do not supply temporal response to animal pressure.

For a direct modern bridge we fixed all four seasons 2017–2020, with a secondary exact 2017/2020 endpoint design, on the same site × species and same known count method. We require complete sea ice, salinity, temperature and both polynya radii, and compare the physical roster and its nested positive-map subset. A species-level descriptive accounting requires at least three sites and two source regions; no ecological regression was planned. We also enumerate same-site-season same-method two-species support. After inspecting the primary support, the alternative-method v5 sensitivity was fixed: reconstruct every original 2,456-row key/count/method and retain all 2,724 agreed single-known-method groups in the original v5 source; no changes in time window or geography.

The additional optical–nest bridge used the exact original 439 site-season keys, the source-selected ADPE census records and nominal atlas locations, with same survey method on both endpoints. We evaluated all six adjacent seasons 2004–2010 and the 2004–2010 endpoint; each accounting required at least three shared sites and two reporting regions. For the future physical comparison, the four historical rosters and their original count medians were fixed, including their previously identified coverage differences. For each of three CMIP6 models, two SSPs, two future periods and three physical variables (August–November SIC, model surface salinity and air temperature), early and recent count weights were applied separately to the same model anomaly field relative to 1995–2014. The difference is an allocation contrast conditional on fixed future physics, not a modeled future redistribution. We retained the existing all-six-marine-grid-centres-within-50-km sensitivity and reporting-region omissions.

## Results
### 1. Marine conditions and breeding allocation are distinct (Figs. 1–2)
In the 588-site physical panel, the temperature/ice axis accounted for 60.37% and the predominantly salinity axis 33.38% of standardized site-year variance. Orthogonality of PCA scores is not evidence of distinct causal processes. Across 595 known sites, the equal-site winter coastal-polynya area fraction within 100 km increased by 0.634 points between the two shorter periods (408/595 increasing), while equal weighting of 43 250-km blocks yielded −0.036 points. Therefore the site-weighted physical result is not a uniform geographic trend.

The long parent and complete cohorts both have positive ice reweighting, but the concentration directions differ. Earlier exact permutation and source-year diagnostics did not identify special active climate tracking. Reweighting is an accounting result, not adaptive relocation.

### 2. The same allocations have different historical and future change-field contrasts (Fig. 3)
Under SSP5-8.5 in 2081–2100, applying early and recent weights to the same three-model sea-ice change fields produced −5.155 and −5.296 percentage points in the 23-site long parent cohort (difference −0.141), versus −2.260 and −0.794 in its 17-site complete ocean-data subset (+1.466). The shorter parent 15 and complete nine cohorts gave differences of −0.163 and +0.470 points, respectively. The 17-site subset had positive differences in every model and all four scenarios, but region omission could reverse the direction. The 13-site marine-grid-distance subset retained a positive direction under all model and region omissions in the late-century high-emissions case only; the 23-site parent model-specific signs disagreed in all four scenarios. The corresponding surface salinity differences were −0.00483, −0.00313, +0.00015 and −0.00212 in the four full rosters. The sign was not consistently shared by all models. The historical nest redistribution can therefore change physical exposure summaries without guaranteeing reduced future sea-ice or salinity change. These comparisons hold two observed allocations fixed rather than predicting animals, and the nested supports are not independent.

The previously reported historical level-reweighting term is not directly comparable to the projected change-field contrast. ### 3. Marine associations depend on biological outcome and support (Fig. 4)
The winter 100-km polynya coefficient for next-season optical δ15N was −1.003 per 10 percentage points (CV3 interval −1.675 to −0.331); salinity adjustment retained a negative point estimate. Separate salinity association was +0.834 per unit, with its region-level interval including zero. The polynya result was exploratory, forward prediction did not improve, the future/past contrast was uncertain, and the Mertz event did not provide the expected biological response. An independently measured stomach-content polynya validation at Signy did not support the association as diet switching.

A separate 15-season Pointe Géologie productivity series gave a trend-adjusted +0.0806 offspring per nesting female per P100 percentage point, and +0.0913 with physical adjustment; individual-year deletions retained positive direction. The original productivity and regional nest denominators did not coincide, and the positive direction did not transfer universally to other Adélie monitoring locations. The matched productivity-to-spatial bridge was unstable. In the 595-location source, secondary 1–5-year nest-change associations had opposite signs for Adélie (−0.0358) and gentoo (+0.0598) per polynya point, with different geographic supports. Annual primary models and forward tests did not support generalization. At Signy the observed gentoo-minus-Adélie pre-fledging chick-per-pair contrast was positive across nine fixed summers, but the fully adjusted design lacked degrees of freedom. The Cape Shirreff relative juvenile-mass association was negative in a trend-adjusted 12-year contrast and unstable after full physical adjustment. The different outcomes and sampling footprints preclude a common stage trade-off or mediator.

The exact optical–nest join retained 46 of 439 colony-seasons at 38 sites, eight reporting regions and 19 250-km blocks. Neither the seven-season panel nor any of the seven fixed endpoint/adjacent-year comparisons met the joint support gate: four adjacent pairs had only Pointe Géologie and three comparisons had no shared site. We therefore did not calculate a temporal count-weighted optical proxy or fit a mediator.

### 4. Numerical and spatial changes are not interchangeable (Fig. 5)
The fixed Palmer/Signy cases show concentration of observed nest counts during declines. The Ross 1999–2001–2002 sequence regained 96.97% of aggregate observed breeding-count loss while restoring only 2.4% of the original six-component configuration displacement (28.3% at the three-colony resolution). This is one observational disturbance and rebound, not individual demographic recovery.

The selected matched Signy summers show that fur seals and chinstrap penguins both declined (−52.8% and −27.8%) but had opposite concentration changes (−0.0394 and +0.0120), robust to one-summer deletion; the Adélie result depends on which documented colony roster is used. A distinct Bird Island gentoo series increased from 3,549 to 5,119 observed pairs while effective component number rose from 4.069 to 4.981, with an uncertain temporal concentration slope. These cases do not support a universal seal–penguin difference. In Signy's three monitored seal taxa, the combined mean count fell 56.48%, and increases in the other two taxa offset only 1.07% of the numerical fur-seal decline. This does not measure equivalent ecological pressure or compensation of nutrient flux. Same-place Adélie–gentoo opposite observed trends and local species-composition shifts are not a demonstrated competition mechanism; the strict cross-site compensation test lacked support.

The independent JNCC data system illustrates the broader problem: 256/310 growing matched network comparisons contained local losses, and 140/310 had at least one prior-positive component recorded as zero. These are recorded count changes, not verified extirpation. The nested 663 exact-year panels and conservative two-taxon warning exclusion retain the central concentration and countervailing-change results; they are not new independent datasets. We reserve the full estimates, observation-model references and detailed caveats for the companion narrow manuscript/evidence supplement.

### 5. Direct terrestrial spatial evidence and the failed temporal bridge (Fig. 6)
At Palmer, 3,684 eligible detected seal positions across ten positive-detection sites overlapped mapped terrestrial vegetation and lichen at an equal-site/day mean of 10.925%, compared with 7.343% under a terrain-matched availability reference (+3.582 points; seven of ten sites positive; one-site-deletion mean 2.623–4.116 points). The 20-m tile comparison found +3.040 points of mapped green vegetation in high- versus low-detected-use tiles after terrain matching at nine sites; the broad intermediate-density optimum prediction was not supported. These are repeated detections, not distinct animals or verified animal-days. The plant map overlaps the seal survey period and is static, so neither plant facilitation nor damage is identified.

At Shirley, the union of polygons labeled extant in the 2005/06 archive had no mapped vegetation overlap in the 2017–2023 composite, while relic-minus-extant polygons had 230.378 m² of overlap within 7,249.682 m² (3.178%). Three positive polygons belonged to one 50-m-connected group. Historical plant cover, current penguin occupation and true absence of plants are not established. The existing three-species regional analysis of mapped vegetation area and breeding-count change did not establish a common within-region relationship. Published Signy impacted/protected plant functional-cover contrasts and Kerguelen scat chemistry are mechanistic context rather than independent new estimated effects.

The directly matched 2017–2020 cohort failed before estimation: no four-year complete count site in any of the three focal species, and only 1/1/5 Adélie/chinstrap/gentoo sites in the exact endpoint comparison, all in one central-west Antarctic Peninsula 250-km block. Alternative-method original v5 records did not improve this fixed support. The two-species same-season common modern map/physical support comprised 5, 9 and 20 site-seasons for Adélie–chinstrap, Adélie–gentoo and chinstrap–gentoo in the v5 sensitivity. None constitutes a replicated longitudinal plant–animal or interaction study.

## Discussion
The strongest direct bridge is the exact change in count-weighted physical exposure, not an estimated effect of physical conditions on concentration. The closest literature already separates redistribution from environmental change (Cohen & Jetz, 2025; Avidad et al., 2025); the mathematical partition is established (Kitagawa, 1955). We contribute a conditional Antarctic application: the same observed site weights are evaluated against physical levels, historical changes and projected changes across marine variables and spatial supports. The aligned contrast demonstrates that even a positive historical level-reweighting component does not establish a positive historical change-field contrast. Historical-to-future change-field signs also differ, but the comparison is geographically and model dependent. Because sea-ice effects on growth are nonlinear (Iles et al., 2020), a more positive SIC contrast should not automatically be termed buffering, benefit or adaptation. This is narrower than the habitat-suitability and population projections of Cimino et al. (2016), and cannot be interpreted as a better forecast. The novelty candidate is the empirical matched-roster multivariable and cross-period comparison, not a first discovery or new mathematical method. Our geographic accounting makes the coverage problem explicit: the increase in the share of the six omitted Victoria Land components changes both within-group and between-group contributions; a region-wide positive reweighting term can be canceled by the spatial allocation within that region. Hence regional distribution, local breeding configuration, and environmental exposure cannot be substituted for one another even in a fixed set of nominal colonies. This is an explanation of the observed accounting result, not a causal explanation of why animals redistributed. Changes in observed breeding allocation can offset or reinforce changes at locations, and the contribution can differ among physical variables and radii. Concentration, total variation and exposure reweighting answer separate questions. Geographic coverage matters enough to reverse the direction of concentration in the long nested samples, so the arithmetic should always be accompanied by roster sensitivity.

The biological modules constrain interpretation rather than establish successive mediators. The optical isotope association is consistent with marine food-web variation but neither uniquely identifies prey choice nor transfers reliably to future seasons. Reproductive-stage comparisons are local, with non-equivalent denominators and geographic support. The vertebrate allocation results illustrate non-equivalent local and aggregate changes; they cannot measure individual movements or interactions. Direct nitrogen-mediated Antarctic marine–land effects are documented by Bokhorst et al. (2019), and repeated Signy plant surveys by Cannone et al. (2022). Our original-source seal/plant overlaps are a different, weaker spatial estimand, not independent confirmation of those effects. The terrestrial overlaps make it reasonable to investigate how spatial animal use and plant composition are related, while allowing reverse causation, shared terrain and observation/classification effects. They do not identify the direction of a vegetation response. A genuine terrestrial footprint budget would require spatially and temporally resolved animal-days for each taxon and independently measured nutrient/deposition or disturbance rates, not arbitrary weights assigned to breeding or haul-out counts.

Future CMIP6 physical projections from three selected models agree on surface freshening at 464/595 sites and combined freshening/ice decline at 384/595 sites under SSP5-8.5 in 2081–2100. These provide physical exposure scenarios, not predictions of counts, diet or vegetation. A biological forecast is not justified by the failed temporal transfer. The fixed historical allocation comparison further shows why the historical exposure offset cannot be extrapolated to future physical anomalies. Neither the original 439 optical records nor the static mapped land cover provides sufficient matched longitudinal support for the missing demographic or terrestrial mediation. The current evidence cannot support the complete marine-to-land causal chain; a single integrated empirical submission would require a narrower claim or genuinely new aligned temporal records. The alternative narrower JNCC-centered manuscript remains self-contained.
