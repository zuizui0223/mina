# Spatial reorganization of colonial seabird breeding networks beyond population decline
**Integrated working manuscript v0.1 — author review only, 25 November 2026.** Not submitted. No author list or affiliation assigned. This candidate synthesis overlaps earlier frozen Ecology drafts (PRs 173 and 189; main Report); choose one route and disclose overlap before submission. No prior manuscript modified. Primary empirical JNCC analysis; Antarctic cases supply bounded temporal and recovery contrasts. Marine climate and food-web results are context, not a fitted causal pathway.

## Abstract
Population totals aggregate changes across places, potentially concealing a loss of breeding components even where abundance increases. We compared two national seabird censuses in Britain and Ireland using 1,321 matched species-by-monitoring-network-by-count-unit comparisons across 17 taxa and 604 named networks. We separated total change, concentration of relative breeding counts, and countervailing absolute local changes using established dissimilarity partitioning. Among 310 growing comparisons, 256 (82.6%) contained at least one declining component and 140 (45.2%) contained at least one previously positive component recorded as zero in the later census. Across ten taxa represented in all three aggregate-change classes, the species-equal mean replacement component of Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was 0.098 in declining, 0.235 in near-stable and 0.161 in growing networks. These values exceeded simulations under fixed pooled site shares, including an assumed 20% multiplicative-error sensitivity. Concentration also increased on average in all three classes. Restricting comparisons to identical survey years across components within each census preserved these directions (663 comparisons; seven common taxa), although bootstrap interval support was insufficient. In six previously studied Antarctic breeding-count panels, concentration accumulated over matched longer intervals, often without simultaneous absolute local gains and losses. A Ross Island disturbance–rebound case further illustrates a mismatch between aggregate recovery and spatial restoration. These observations concern matched monitoring components, not confirmed colony extinction, migration, or calibrated detection. They show why aggregate breeding abundance and spatial configuration should be evaluated separately.

**Keywords:** coloniality; breeding networks; temporal beta diversity; Bray–Curtis; population change; seabird monitoring; spatial concentration.

## Introduction
The number of breeding animals in a monitoring network and their allocation among breeding components convey different information. A rising aggregate count can coexist with falling counts at particular sites; equally, a declining aggregate can conceal local increases. Spatial population ecology already recognizes heterogeneous local dynamics and synchrony (Liebhold et al., 2004), while abundance–occupancy studies and proportional-density/basin models compare changes in numbers with the amount of space occupied (Gaston et al., 2000; Thorson et al., 2016). However, occupancy or effective area alone does not describe whether previously important breeding components retain their relative contribution.

Colonial breeding offers a discrete observational framework for examining this distinction. Seabirds acquire marine resources but are counted at breeding sites; heterogeneous access, recruitment, survival, breeding participation and site fidelity can produce different site-level trajectories. Regional heterogeneity in Magellanic penguin growth is documented (Pozzi et al., 2015). Long-term work on cliff swallows also illustrates variable local colony sizes without a strong directional shift in overall colony-size distributions (Brown et al., 2013). We therefore do not present spatial rearrangement or site-dependent demography as new phenomena. The comparative question is whether substantial change in the allocation of monitored breeding counts, and specifically countervailing changes in local absolute counts, is restricted to declining aggregates or is evident across the full direction of aggregate change.

We adapt the established Podani-family percentage-difference partition (Legendre, 2014, Appendix S1) and temporal gain–loss accounting (Legendre, 2019), treating monitored breeding components, rather than species within a community, as coordinates of an abundance vector. We distinguish relative concentration from absolute countervailing change. A fixed-composition, fixed-total model makes explicit which part of the pattern is compatible with an idealized count-noise model; it is not a model of true census detectability. We ask (1) whether concentration and composition change recur in growing and near-stable as well as declining networks; (2) whether opposite absolute changes at different components exceed this model reference; and (3) whether selected annual Antarctic series and a disturbance–rebound case illustrate distinct accounting routes to similar concentration outcomes. The third question is descriptive because the local panels were selected in earlier research, largely for decline. We do not test a universal duration effect or identify movement and climate drivers.

## Methods
### Matched breeding networks
The main data are the original revised 2024 comparative workbook for Seabird 2000 and Seabirds Count (Burnell et al., 2023; JNCC official resource below), representing the 1998–2002 and 2015–2021 national census campaigns. We use the actual source year, adjusted count and source identity fields rather than assuming every record falls within the nominal survey window. The original XLSX SHA256 was fixed before analyses. The previous structural audit identified 14,112 eligible matched source site-species records and 1,485 candidate parent panels. A conservative guard removed ambiguous child rows named identically to the parent network, leaving 1,483 structural parent panels. A panel is a species × country × Master Site × count unit, containing at least three matched original Site.ID components. Both epochs require nonnegative exact integer adjusted counts and totals of at least two; 162 structural panels lacked adequate totals, leaving 1,321 panels of 17 taxa in 604 country–Master Site networks. The unit is a monitoring network rather than a demonstrated population or physical island. Count units differ between panels; raw counts are never pooled across incompatible units. Only matched recorded components are represented, and no missing survey is imputed as zero. Complete extinction of the matched network is outside the conditional composition estimand.

The exact-year sensitivity retains panels in which all child components have the same recorded earlier census year and the same recorded later census year. It contains 663 eligible panels (389 country–Master Site networks), nested in the full sample. Even in this subset, census intervals vary between networks (median 19, 18 and 18 years in the three aggregate classes) and identical recorded years do not establish constant surveyed area, effort or site boundaries. The official source cautions about comparability of certain national species estimates; matched-site filters cannot automatically remove these biases.

### Separate measures of amount, relative composition and absolute change
For count vectors a and b on the same roster, let N0 and N1 be their sums and p and q their relative shares. The aggregate direction was classified using N1/N0: decline below 1/1.10, increase above 1.10, near-stable otherwise. This is a whole-interval classification, not an annual growth rate or an estimate of long-term stability.

Relative composition was quantified by TV = 0.5 sum |q_i−p_i|, and concentration by H = sum p_i² (effective component number = 1/H). For integer counts, the finite-count-corrected Simpson estimate is U = sum n_i(n_i−1)/[N(N−1)]. We use signed ΔU = U1−U0 (positive indicates greater concentration) and signed D² = U0+U1−2 sum p_i q_i, whose expectation under two independent multinomial samples with the same composition is zero. The correction is conditional on that sampling model; negative D² estimates are retained. A unique dominant component changes identity only when the maximum is unique at both times.

For the absolute difference d_i=b_i−a_i, define gains G=sum max(d_i,0) and losses L=sum max(−d_i,0). The net change is G−L. Let A=sum min(a_i,b_i). Bray–Curtis dissimilarity on the original count vectors equals (G+L)/(N0+N1), and N0+N1=2A+G+L. Following the replacement/abundance-difference partition of Legendre (2014, equations 12–14 in Appendix S1), C=2 min(G,L)/(N0+N1) is the replacement component and |G−L|/(N0+N1) the abundance-difference component. C measures countervailing absolute changes normalized to the two-epoch mean total; it is not a new index. It is zero if all local changes are in one direction. C≥0.05 is a pre-fixed descriptive threshold. Mixed change means G>0 and L>0. We count source components whose earlier adjusted count is positive and later count is zero; this is not an independently confirmed extirpation or evidence of movement.

### Conditional reference and uncertainty
For each panel we condition on actual N0,N1 and estimate a fixed composition from pooled component counts (a+b)/(N0+N1). We generate two independent multinomial samples, and as a fixed sensitivity independently multiply each epoch's pooled site weights by gamma-distributed multipliers (shape 25, scale 1/25; CV 20%), renormalize and draw multinomial counts. The gamma multipliers apply to unnormalized weights, not observed total counts; this is a hypothetical error model, not measured observation precision. Both conserve the total and use the original matched roster. Simulation ranges are conditional on the empirical pooled composition, not external validation; correlated errors and systematic changes in survey coverage are unmodelled.

We compare observed mean ΔU and D², C and the C≥0.05 fraction with model distributions. In the full set, ten taxa have at least three panels in each direction class and receive equal species weight; seven of these retain the same support in the exact-year subset. All 17 taxa are retained in the descriptive counts. We use 999 model simulations per panel and 1,999 resampled country–Master Site networks for percentile intervals, retaining a bootstrap draw only when all fixed species-by-class cells are present. Intervals are reported only when at least 95% of draws remain valid. Resampling accounts for repeated species within named networks, not all spatial or phylogenetic dependence. Null tail fractions describe the specified simulations, not field-calibrated ecological p-values. All-direction, year-aligned and gain–loss contracts were fixed before their respective new calculations, after prior exploratory results; the sequence is not an independently preregistered study.

### Selected Antarctic time series and recovery contrast
We reconstruct six previously studied fixed component panels: Palmer Station Cormorant, Humble and Litchfield Adélie penguins (1991–2017, Litchfield positive-total years through 2006), Signy Island Adélie and chinstrap penguins (22 complete seasons within 1996–2019), and Ross Island Adélie penguins (26 complete census years, 1985–2012, six nested census components). The Palmer and Signy source hashes and previously frozen effective-component endpoints were verified; all Signy annual effective numbers were reproduced. Ross uses the pinned six-component count matrix from the original aerial survey archive (DOI 10.7931/kf06-x745); the three-colony grouping is a distinct spatial resolution. Five of the six local panels were already selected for declining/concentrating trajectories, and the three regional systems are not six independent geographic experiments.

We select exact common starting years with all endpoints y+1, y+5, y+10 observed, and separately common ending years with y−1, y−5, y−10 observed; use positive totals at least two. No interpolated observations. We summarize within-anchor 10-year minus 1-year differences in the same measures, with a minimum of three eligible anchors per panel. We also compare mixed/nonmixed patterns for the same 71 common starts. Intervals overlap and long-term drift is not separable from interval length, so these are descriptive contrasts, not tests based on independent annual replicates. The Ross 1999–2001–2002 disturbance/rebound provides an additional previously analyzed case; it is neither a replicated experiment nor a proxy for total-adult demography.

## Results
### Spatial change is not restricted to declining monitored networks
Of 1,321 matched JNCC panels, 912 declined, 99 were near-stable and 310 grew under the fixed cutoff. Median TV was 0.367, 0.254 and 0.295, respectively. Among panels with a unique dominant site at both epochs, the dominant identity changed in 398/802, 32/87 and 130/272, respectively. Across the ten common taxa, species-equal ΔU was +0.0885, +0.0607 and +0.0835. Decline minus growth was +0.0050 (network-bootstrap 95% interval −0.0299 to +0.0375); this neither establishes a difference nor equivalence. All three observed group means exceeded the two conditional-model 95th percentiles. A concentration response can thus be observed without an aggregate decline, in these monitored panels.

### Distinct accounting routes to concentration
Of the 196 growing full-cohort panels with positive ΔU, 166 contained opposing local absolute changes and 30 did not. Among declining panels with positive ΔU, the corresponding counts were 312 and 283 of 595. Therefore simultaneous absolute gains and losses are common alongside concentration, but not a necessary condition for it. In the exact-year subset the same qualitative distinction remained (73 mixed and 25 nonmixed of 98 growing concentrating panels; 150 mixed and 151 nonmixed of 301 declining concentrating panels). The joint distribution describes the accounting routes, not a causal effect of C on concentration.

At ten years, 48 of 65 positive-concentration contrasts lacked simultaneous gains and losses; among the 55 declining concentrating contrasts, 47 lacked absolute gains at any component. The local panels therefore illustrate differential attrition more often than countervailing change over these selected long intervals. Only seven local ten-year contrasts were in the growth class; they do not provide a reliable independent estimate of growth-class frequencies. The census-grain, epoch length and selection differences prevent a formal JNCC–Antarctic prevalence comparison.

### Rebound need not restore the original configuration
In the Ross disturbance sequence, 96.97% of the 1999–2001 aggregate breeding-count loss was regained by 2002. All six monitored components declined during the shock and increased during the rebound, but rebound magnitudes were unequal: Crozier West accounted for 80.6% of the rebound. The six-component TV distance from the 1999 composition to 2002 was 4.97%, and the reduction in the distance from the pre-shock configuration was only 2.4% of the initial shock displacement (28.3% at the three-colony grain). These quantities measure configuration, not lost physical area. Earlier fixed Ross analysis also found that between 2001 and 2012 all six components increased while three-colony effective number fell 10.75% and six-component effective number fell 14.04%. Thus differential amplification during growth can also yield concentration without local absolute losses. The original deconcentration prediction failed; we do not reinterpret it as a prospectively confirmed prediction of concentration.

## Discussion
### Aggregate trajectories and configuration are complementary
The principal empirical contribution is the matched-network comparison across the direction of aggregate change, not the observation that sites vary or a new mathematical partition. In this sample, growing and near-stable breeding networks exhibited substantial compositional reorganization and mean concentration, and countervailing local changes were larger than expected under our two fixed-composition models. Prior abundance–occupancy and colony-size research already shows that amount and distribution can decouple. Our comparison documents how that distinction appears in paired monitored site counts after explicitly conditioning the reference on the observed totals.

This distinction is relevant for conservation because an increasing total cannot establish that all monitored components are recovering. A fixed roster does not measure the creation of new colonies outside the observed network, and a positive-to-zero adjusted count does not establish actual extirpation. Neither C nor ΔU can locate the fate of an individual. Reports of aggregate status could therefore be accompanied by consistent site-level occupancy/count histories, concentration, and gain/loss summaries, without treating these descriptive metrics as direct demographic mechanisms.

### Different routes, not one universal process
If all sites decline by exactly the same proportion, relative composition is unchanged, while absolute Bray–Curtis change is entirely an abundance-difference component. Unequal declines can increase concentration without any local increase. Conversely, unequal gains can increase concentration without any local loss, as illustrated by the Ross rebound. Mixed changes can alter the shares substantially even when the total remains almost constant. These are accounting routes to concentration, and many ecological processes can produce each. A stronger test of dispersal, demographic compensation, site attraction or density-dependent recruitment would require independently observed breeding histories, local vital rates and observation effort. Two census endpoints cannot estimate temporal covariance or identify compensatory regulation.

The six Antarctic panels illustrate persistent concentration over longer comparison intervals and the Ross episode illustrates incomplete configuration restoration. They do not establish a universal time-scale law or prove that short- and long-term dynamics differ intrinsically. Five panels were selected for decline, the time windows overlap, and the monitoring grain differs from that of the JNCC networks. The recovery mismatch is an illustrative case; similar totals at two dates do not establish a mechanistic hysteresis loop.

### Environmental interpretation and limits
In Antarctica, marine environmental changes may modify prey access and breeding participation, while land availability may constrain colony location. Existing analyses in this research program describe two marine environmental axes, seasonal optical isotope associations with salinity and winter polynya area, heterogeneous food-use responses, species replacement and latitudinal reweighting. Those analyses have distinct grains and supports. They cannot supply a demonstrated pathway from warming to colony concentration. The matched annual climate comparison failed the geographic-support gate, and no environmental reorganization coefficient was estimated. Future physical climate exposures are not validated future breeding-count predictions. Seal and vegetation links remain untested. The present manuscript does not attempt to integrate independent outcomes through an unsupported causal narrative.

### Measurement and sampling boundaries
The main censuses contain adjusted counts and nonidentical observation procedures. A consistent site identifier and same recorded year do not establish identical surveyed boundaries, equal effort or perfect detection. Some source taxa have published comparability cautions. A fixed-composition multinomial model and hypothetical independent gamma weights do not account for systematic observation shifts; the pooled shares are estimated from the same observations. Network bootstrap intervals depend on the observed networks and do not establish random taxon sampling, phylogenetic independence or continent-wide representativeness. The growing and declining groups differ in size and total change; C is arithmetically constrained by those totals, so between-class differences are not biological effect estimates. Source filtering excludes entire networks with inadequate positive totals and restricts the comparison to matched surveyed sites. Inferences concern those monitored networks only.

In summary, spatial monitoring of colonial breeders should distinguish numerical change, relative concentration and countervailing local change. An increasing or apparently stable aggregate does not by itself establish recovery of its component breeding sites.
