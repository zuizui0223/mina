# Winter coastal-polynya area is associated with annual variation in Adélie penguin optical isotope proxies

**Running title:** Winter polynyas and penguin isotopes

## Abstract

Coastal polynyas may connect winter sea-ice conditions with food webs observed during the following breeding season. We linked published Adélie penguin guano reflectance to June–October coastal-polynya area fractions within 100 km of colonies. The analysis included 439 colony-seasons at 85 sites in 11 Antarctic reporting regions during 2004–2010. Models related an optical nitrogen-isotope proxy (δ¹⁵N) to winter exposure, adjusting for summer sea-ice concentration, seawater temperature, image timing, colony effects and region-by-season effects. A 10-percentage-point increase in polynya fraction was associated with a 1.00‰ decrease in the proxy (cluster-jackknife 95% interval for eight informative regions: −1.68 to −0.33‰), or 0.92‰ after additional salinity adjustment. This negative direction contradicted the positive hypothesis fixed before the polynya analysis and is reported as an exploratory finding. On the 358-record timing panel, the preceding-winter coefficient was more negative than the following-winter coefficient, but their difference was uncertain (−0.69‰; cluster-jackknife 95% interval: −1.47 to +0.09‰). Point estimates remained negative under calibration sensitivity and each regional omission. Polynya exposure and summer salinity were weakly correlated after fixed-effect adjustment (r = −0.08). However, adding polynya exposure did not improve forward prediction, and its point estimate was smaller in magnitude in 2007–2010. Diet composition, prey isotope baselines and optical mixing remain unresolved explanations. These results identify a seasonal association for targeted validation, without establishing dietary switching or supporting future diet projections.

**Keywords:** *Pygoscelis adeliae*; coastal polynya; nitrogen isotopes; satellite reflectance; seasonal sea ice; Southern Ocean

## 1. Introduction

Seasonal sea ice links the physical environment around Antarctic penguin colonies to access, prey habitats and the timing of biological production. Its ecological associations depend on the season and spatial scale measured. Coastal polynyas provide a distinct description of the winter ice environment: recurring areas of low ice concentration associated with the coast, embedded in a wider ice-covered setting. Their mapped area is not interchangeable with total open water, summer ice concentration or regional sea-ice extent. Daily satellite delineations now allow this winter feature to be measured consistently around widely separated colonies (Lin et al. 2024).

The connection between sea ice and Adélie penguin food-web indicators already has substantial empirical support. Youngflesh et al. (2026) linked satellite guano reflectance, calibrated against nitrogen isotopes, to variation in sea ice and penguin populations. Their published record provides the optical observations used here. In the Ross Sea, Maccapan et al. (2023) compared guano isotopes at four colonies over two breeding seasons and related dietary differences to sea-ice persistence and breakup. Reaves et al. (2025) examined chick-bone isotopes from 15 colony locations, interpreting the higher δ¹⁵N of southern colonies in the context of latent-heat polynyas and greater fish consumption. These studies motivate a more specific question about the winter immediately preceding a breeding season, rather than a claim that annual ice–diet relationships are unexplored.

Spatial and temporal comparisons need not estimate the same relationship. A colony's long-term association with a particular polynya setting can reflect geography, prey communities and isotope baselines. Annual deviations in the mapped polynya area around that colony may represent different processes. Furthermore, a lower predator δ¹⁵N value need not indicate a change from fish to krill. Polito et al. (2019) showed that variation in the isotopic composition of krill can bias dietary inference even when prey proportions remain constant. An isotope proxy derived from reflectance adds measurement and optical-mixing uncertainties to this biological ambiguity.

We therefore ask whether June–October coastal-polynya area fraction within 100 km of an Adélie colony is associated with the subsequent November–February optical δ¹⁵N signal after accounting for summer sea-ice concentration, seawater temperature, image timing, colony effects and region-by-season effects. We compare the preceding and following winters on identical observations, assess the relation to summer salinity, and retain unsuccessful prediction and validation checks. The direct polynya analysis was originally designed to test a positive association after earlier environmental exploration. Its negative result, and the present decision to place it at the centre of the manuscript, are explicitly exploratory.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1 Optical isotope response and colony support

We reanalysed the original reflectance and calibration archive released by Youngflesh et al. (2026; Zenodo 17409948). The response is a reconstructed optical guano δ¹⁵N proxy, expressed in per mil (‰). It is neither a direct chemical measurement at every colony-season nor the published multistage diet posterior. The original reconstruction used five-component partial least squares regression (PLSR), 103 laboratory guano calibration observations and sensor-specific Landsat 4/5/7 spectral responses. Published guano-positive source pixels passing the archived quality flag were retained, with the original minimum average pixel-support requirement. Colony identifiers and centroids were matched to catalogue locations within 2 km.

The broader environmental match comprised 860 observed colony-seasons at 88 sites in 13 reporting regions. It used 131,014 source pixels from 2,659 image site-days; each observed image day contributed equally to its November–following-February seasonal mean. Missing image days and unobserved colony-seasons were not reconstructed as zero. The polynya analysis used the subset with complete winter exposure and covariates. These are sampled colony locations from the released archive, not a complete census of Adélie colonies or independent populations (Fig. 1). Differences between the released pixel archive and the final article's image list are documented in Supplement S1.

### 2.2 Winter coastal-polynya exposure

We used DEEP-AA version 1.1.0-2023 and its recommended SIC60_6.25km_20d product (Lin et al. 2024; Zenodo 13358042). The original archive was verified against its published checksum. Daily native 6.25-km grids identify individual polynyas; only positive even identifiers, representing coastal polynyas, were counted. Offshore polynyas, ordinary non-polynya water and open ocean were not relabelled as coastal polynyas.

For each colony and day, exposure was the area of coastal-polynya pixels divided by the actual ocean area within a 100-km geodesic circle. Land was excluded from the denominator and native projection-area distortion was corrected. Available days were averaged within each month, then the five June–October monthly means were weighted equally. Every month required at least 80% observed-day coverage. The 100-km radius and seasonal window were fixed before examining the direct polynya association; 25-km extraction was a prespecified support diagnostic, not an alternative radius selected for a favourable result. Valid measured zeros were retained, and missing winters were not filled using other years.

This procedure yielded 439 colony-seasons at 85 sites in 11 source reporting regions, for winters beginning in 2004–2010. Season y refers to winter June–October y and breeding-season observations November y–February y+1. Exposure was positive in 405 records and genuinely zero in 34. Its mean was 7.03%, standard deviation 6.32 percentage points, and range 0–26.68%. These are local area fractions, not the proportion of a colony's observed foraging trips using a polynya.

### 2.3 Summer conditions and statistical comparisons

Summer sea-ice concentration came from the original NOAA/NSIDC G02202 version 4 source, approximated with native pixel centres within 150 km and averaged over November–February (Meier et al. 2021). Surface practical salinity and potential seawater temperature came from GLORYS12V1 release 202311, matched to the nearest validated wet grid cell within 25 km (Lellouche et al. 2021). Different colony locations can share an ocean cell. The winter polynya and summer sea-ice variables consequently describe different seasons, definitions and spatial supports.

All four main models used the same 439 records. The baseline model M0 included colony and region-by-season fixed effects, the mean centred image day and mean squared centred image day, summer sea-ice concentration and seawater temperature. Image day was counted from 1 November, centred at day 60 and squared before seasonal averaging; the second timing term is not the square of the seasonal mean day. Models M_P, M_S and M_SP added winter polynya fraction, summer salinity, or both, respectively. Thus the polynya coefficient describes variation remaining within colonies after removing common region-season conditions and measured summer covariates. It does not compare only the long-term means of different colonies. We report polynya coefficients per 10-percentage-point increase in area fraction, matching the original frozen models. To distinguish this reporting unit from empirical support, we also computed the standard deviation of the polynya regressor after removing the same colony, region-by-season and baseline summer-covariate terms (and salinity where applicable), without changing models or fitting a new outcome contrast. A 10-point increment should not be interpreted as a typical within-colony deviation or experimentally imposed change (Supplement S2).

The archived analysis used region-cluster covariance with a CR1 correction and t intervals based on the nominal number of regions minus one. An independent support audit found that the three regions represented by only one colony contribute no identifying exposure variation after the fixed effects. Removing their 14 records leaves 425 records at 82 sites in eight informative regions, without changing any slopes or residual sum of squares. Information is also uneven among the eight remaining regions: Victoria Land and Wilkes Land supply 55.0% and 19.0%, respectively, of the fully residualised polynya sum of squares in M_P. Counting eight regions therefore does not establish adequate finite-sample precision.

To examine this leverage concern, we added a delete-one-informative-region jackknife covariance (CV3), centred at the full estimate and combined with t(7), following MacKinnon et al. (2023). Each omission refits the same model and fixed effects. This method was selected before calculating the new intervals; no alternative exposure, lag, response or favourable subset was selected. We display the CV3 intervals prominently and retain both eight-region and nominal 11-region CR1 results in Table S1 and the figure extracts. Both support and jackknife audits were added during manuscript preparation on 21 November 2026. All intervals remain approximate, unadjusted for the broader sequence of analyses, and unsuitable for confirmatory interpretation. Summer covariates occur after winter exposure, so these adjusted coefficients also do not estimate a total causal effect if summer conditions lie on a causal pathway.

We also retained leave-one-region-out point estimates and the proportionate reduction in residual sum of squares relative to M0. Polynya–salinity correlation was calculated after removing colony and region-by-season effects only; it was not a raw correlation or a test of causal independence. Supplement S2 specifies the covariance formula and reproducibility checks.

### 2.4 Temporal comparison and calibration sensitivity

To compare timing directly, we required both the preceding winter P0 and following winter P1 for the same optical observation. The DEEP-AA coverage gate excluded the following winter of the 2010 breeding-season records, leaving 358 observations from 2004–2009. A simultaneous model included P0 and P1, summer salinity and the baseline controls. We calculated the P0−P1 contrast using their covariance, rather than comparing the significance of two separate regressions. The 1,299 contributing image site-days occurred after P0 and at least 93 days before P1 began. The corresponding informative-region sensitivity retained 347 records in eight regions.

Measurement sensitivity used the original authors' R PLSR implementation and 1,000 stratified calibration splits of the same 103 laboratory observations. Sensor-specific fits used the original oscorespls method and five prediction components. We replaced the response with the calibration-ensemble mean on the unchanged 439- and 358-record panels and examined variation across individual fits. Calibration-split ranges describe uncertainty in this reconstruction, not 1,000 independent ecological replications. They do not reproduce the full published hierarchical diet model or validate the guano-selection process.

### 2.5 Prediction and complementary checks

Forward prediction was evaluated using 2004–2006 for training and 2007–2010 for testing at previously observed sites. The retained test set contained 291 records at 79 sites; 16 records at new sites were excluded because their site intercepts were unavailable. Prediction models were refitted with site intercepts and the numeric covariates, omitting region-by-season effects from both training and prediction; no held-out response was used to estimate a future intercept. Site-equal root mean squared error (RMSE) and paired region-bootstrap mean squared error differences compared models with and without polynya exposure. This is a distinct predictive exercise, not the in-sample fixed-effect fit.

We retained three limits on biological interpretation: the availability-selected chlorophyll subset, a separate Signy stomach-content comparison, and the spatial and temporal compatibility of published feather amino-acid isotope measurements. A subsequent same-source, results-blind-frozen scale diagnostic collapsed the 439 colony-seasons to 85 equally weighted colony means, then compared raw and adjusted between-colony slopes with the unchanged within-colony estimate. The primary between-colony model adjusted for reporting-region indicators, each colony's means of the original summer and image-date covariates, mean observation year and source latitude; uncertainty used a delete-one-informative-region jackknife with t(7). We also fixed, before examining between-colony outcomes, analyses restricted to sites observed in at least three seasons, adjusted for source region-by-season means and reconstructed with the original authors' calibration ensemble. These tests were exploratory post-selection diagnostics, not independent isotope measurements or an analysis of the Reaves et al. (2025) chick-bone observations. An attempted extension beyond 2012 was stopped at a prespecified measurement-performance gate, before estimating 2013–2021 ecological associations. Full denominators, unsuccessful checks and their different observational units are given in Supplement S4–S5. As an additional event-level falsification, we dated the Mertz Glacier Tongue calving following the collision of iceberg B-09B to February 2010 from independent physical reports (Kusahara et al. 2011; Tamura et al. 2012). Before evaluating the optical response, we froze a complete five-season 2006–2010 panel of 10 source colonies at 139–146°E and 18 distant East Antarctic controls, with one equally weighted observation per colony and year. We compared each group's 2010 polynya and isotope difference from its 2006–2009 mean, examined pre-period pseudo-events, both source-reporting-region contrasts, the same-source calibration mean and post-winter-covariate sensitivities. This is a benchmark of directional concordance, not a randomized or polynya-specific causal experiment (Supplement S4).

### 2.6 Computational assistance and reproducibility

ChatGPT (OpenAI) assisted with drafting analysis and plotting code, numerical cross-checking, source retrieval and manuscript preparation. The supplied Python scripts define the fixed models, dummy-matrix projection, CR1 and CV3 covariance calculations, source checks and plotting operations; generative-model output is not a statistical estimator or additional ecological observation. Frozen-source numerical reproduction and the new jackknife diagnostic are separate executable steps, with recorded input and script hashes. The documented runtime uses Python 3.12.14, NumPy 2.3.5, pandas 2.2.3 and SciPy 1.17.0. Author-method R calibration outputs remain separately archived. AI-assisted checks do not substitute for the authors' scientific review, which must be completed before submission.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Larger winter polynya fractions accompany lower summer isotope proxies

The M_P coefficient was −1.003‰ per 10 percentage points of winter coastal-polynya fraction. Its eight-region CV3 interval was −1.675 to −0.331‰, compared with the eight-region CR1 interval of −1.505 to −0.501‰ (Fig. 2). Adding summer salinity changed the estimate to −0.922‰, with a CV3 interval of −1.515 to −0.328‰ and an eight-region CR1 interval of −1.384 to −0.459‰. Both estimates were opposite to the positive direction specified before the direct polynya analysis. The wider jackknife intervals retain the negative main association; they do not resolve the limitations of having few, unevenly informative regions.

The original polynya area fraction varied by 6.31 percentage points (population standard deviation), but its variation after colony, region-by-season and M0 summer-covariate adjustment was only **1.60 percentage points** across the same 439 observations (1.63 across the 425 informative records). Thus the unchanged M_P slope corresponds to **−0.161‰ per one standard deviation of the identifying exposure residual**, rather than implying that a 10-point shift is typical. After additionally adjusting for summer salinity, the corresponding residual standard deviation was 1.59 points and the scaled M_SP association was **−0.146‰**. These are algebraic re-expressions of the same exploratory coefficients, not additional estimates, experimental responses or causal effects (Supplement S2).

Omitting each of the 11 nominal reporting regions retained a negative point estimate: −1.166 to −0.762‰ for M_P and −1.091 to −0.755‰ for M_SP. This sign consistency does not mean every omission-specific interval excludes zero. Adding polynya exposure reduced the baseline residual sum of squares by 3.79%; adding both polynya and salinity reduced it by 5.29%. The additional reduction when polynya was added to M_S was 3.22%. These are modest in-sample associations.

### 3.2 The preceding-winter estimate is more negative, but the difference is uncertain

On the matched 358 records, simultaneous coefficients were −1.379‰ for P0 and −0.691‰ for P1. Their direct difference was −0.688‰ per 10 percentage points. The archived 11-region CR1 interval was −1.296 to −0.079‰ and the eight-region CR1 interval was −1.346 to −0.029‰, whereas the eight-region CV3 interval was −1.470 to +0.095‰ (Fig. 3). Leave-one-region-out contrasts remained negative, ranging from −0.861 to −0.505‰. The jackknife interval includes zero: evidence for a difference between winters is sensitive to the uncertainty estimator.

The point estimates are compatible with a more specific association for the preceding winter, but do not establish that temporal distinction. Nor can they establish that winter polynyas caused the subsequent signal. Preceding and following winters were correlated, and time-varying local conditions, observation selection and persistent environmental processes can still contribute. Fixed effects remove stable colony differences, but do not remove every source of temporal confounding.

### 3.3 Polynya exposure is not a substitute for summer salinity

The correlation between winter polynya fraction and summer surface salinity was −0.080 after removing colony and region-by-season effects on the same 439 records (Fig. 4). Salinity alone reduced baseline residual sum of squares by 2.14%, compared with 3.79% for polynya and 5.29% for both. The small adjusted correlation and retained polynya coefficient show that these variables are not interchangeable descriptions of this panel. They do not identify two independent ecological mechanisms.

In M_SP on the same 439 observations, the salinity coefficient was +0.646‰ per practical-salinity unit, with an eight-region CV3 interval of −0.196 to +1.488‰ (Table S3). The separate, broader 860-record analysis gave +0.834‰, with a 13-region CR1 interval of −0.016 to +1.684‰. These are different sample-specific estimates, and both displayed intervals include zero. We therefore do not elevate salinity to a second established food-web response.

### 3.4 Calibration stability does not yield predictive or independent dietary validation

Replacing the original response with the author-method 1,000-fit ensemble mean produced M_P and M_SP coefficients of −1.023 and −0.941‰, respectively. Their eight-region CV3 intervals were −1.707 to −0.339‰ and −1.542 to −0.340‰. The temporal contrast was −0.693‰, with a CV3 interval of −1.476 to +0.090‰. Eight-region CR1 intervals remain in Table S1. Slopes and temporal contrasts were negative in all 1,000 calibration fits, but these negative point estimates do not establish a precise difference between winters. This stability concerns the same historical reflectance measurements and laboratory calibration set.

Adding polynya exposure did not improve forward prediction. Site-equal test RMSE was 1.840‰ for M0 and 1.849‰ for M_P, and 1.801‰ for M_S versus 1.812‰ for M_SP. Paired uncertainty intervals included zero, so the comparison also does not establish a predictive penalty. Descriptive period-specific M_SP estimates were −1.683‰ in 2004–2006 and −0.362‰ in the full 2007–2010 subset; the latter interval included zero. The difference between periods was not itself established by a formal interaction test.

Only 112 of the 358 temporal records had the required chlorophyll coverage. This subset had lower summer ice and higher winter polynya exposure, and its polynya coefficient was already positive before chlorophyll adjustment. It cannot establish mediation of the full-panel negative association. The separate Signy analysis also provided no clear corroboration from stomach contents: across 14 breeding seasons, the estimated krill-fraction association had a wide interval and changed sign under some sensitivity checks. These results leave the dietary pathway unresolved (Fig. 5).

### 3.5 No positive between-colony polynya association was identified in the same optical panel

The 439 historical colony-seasons comprised 85 source colonies, each observed in two to seven winters. With equal weight per colony, the slope between colony-mean optical isotope proxy and colony-mean winter polynya fraction was −0.319‰ per 10 percentage points; including reporting-region fixed effects gave −0.795‰. The pre-frozen primary between-colony model additionally adjusted for mean summer conditions, image timing, observation year and latitude, giving **−1.295‰ per 10 percentage points** (eight-informative-region jackknife 95% interval **−4.965 to +2.376‰**). The point estimate remained negative with the original authors' alternative optical calibration (−1.310‰), on the 82 colonies with at least three observed winters (−1.256‰), and after removing reporting region-by-season common conditions before colony aggregation (−1.263‰). The between-colony association was imprecise: omitting Wilkes Land changed its adjusted slope to approximately +0.16‰. Thus the data do **not** demonstrate an opposite-sign, positive between-colony effect, and they cannot establish a geographic-scale reversal or a shared ecological mechanism (Supplement S4).

### 3.6 An externally dated iceberg-driven polynya contraction did not reproduce the predicted optical response

After the February 2010 calving of the Mertz Glacier Tongue, the source-matched 10-colony area had an average winter polynya fraction of 4.16%, down from 10.28% in 2006–2009, compared with 9.22% versus 9.80% across 18 distant control colonies (difference-in-differences **−5.55 percentage points**). Despite this externally documented physical disruption, the original optical isotope proxy did **not** show the positive differential change implied by transporting the broad negative polynya slope to an event-driven contraction. Its 2010 change was **−0.555‰** relative to controls, and **−0.545‰** under the original authors' calibration sensitivity. Earlier pre-event pseudo-year differences in the optical proxy were larger in absolute magnitude, and the sign differed between reporting-region comparisons (Victoria Land +0.494‰; Wilkes Land −1.390‰). Summer sea-ice concentration, temperature and optical image support also changed simultaneously. Consequently, neither a reproducible biological response to this event nor a causal polynya-mediated food-web pathway was established (Supplement S4).

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 A winter association beyond the measured summer covariates

The most defensible result is a negative association between a specific preceding-winter coastal-polynya area fraction and the subsequent summer optical isotope proxy in an established satellite archive. It persists after measured summer conditions and fixed effects are removed, across region omissions, under an author-method calibration sensitivity and with cluster-jackknife uncertainty. The direct preceding-versus-following-winter comparison describes timing on the same observations, but its interval includes zero under the jackknife sensitivity. The seasonal association warrants focused biological validation; the difference between winters remains unresolved.

They do not establish that ice geometry is more important than ice quantity. The polynya metric is itself an area fraction, while the ice covariate measures concentration in a different season and buffer. We did not hold same-winter total sea-ice area or extent constant while manipulating spatial configuration. The appropriate advance over the published sea-ice analysis is the explicit addition of a winter coastal-polynya metric and separation of within-colony annual variation from stable colony differences. The present result is a reanalysis of the released optical record, not an independent replication of Youngflesh et al. (2026).

### 4.2 Between-colony context and within-colony annual variation

Reaves et al. (2025) associated the southern Ross Sea's higher chick-bone δ¹⁵N with foraging settings that include latent-heat polynyas and greater fish consumption. Our negative coefficient estimates something different: annual variation in satellite-mapped coastal-polynya area within a fixed colony buffer after colony and region-season effects are absorbed. Their comparison involves different colony settings and samples of different calendar ages; ours involves contemporary seasonal optical measurements. The apparent directional tension is therefore an ecological question, not a contradiction between estimates of the same exposure. In particular, the available Reaves evidence is not a directly comparable positive regression of annual polynya area fraction. The pre-frozen source-specific between-colony diagnostic also yielded negative but highly uncertain slopes across the 85 optical colonies, rather than a robust positive counterpart to the annual within-colony coefficient. A scale-dependent sign inversion is therefore **not supported** by our matching optical panel, irrespective of its distinctness from Reaves et al.'s chick-bone and historical comparisons.

Maccapan et al. (2023) further show why the seasonal definition matters. Their Ross Sea comparison connected longer sea-ice persistence and delayed breakup with isotopic evidence of greater krill contributions at some colonies across two seasons. Summer breakup, winter polynya area, and the enduring distinction between northern and southern foraging settings need not vary together. A future comparison would need common colonies, comparable seasonal windows, prey measurements and isotope baselines before assigning different signs to contrasting mechanisms. The present study supplies one explicitly defined estimand within that broader literature.

### 4.3 Dietary change, isotope baselines and optical mixing remain separable possibilities

If prey isotope baselines and optical measurement properties were stable, a lower δ¹⁵N signal could be consistent with greater contribution of lower-trophic prey. That interpretation remains conditional. Intraspecific changes in krill isotopes can move a predator's δ¹⁵N without changing the proportion of krill and fish consumed (Polito et al. 2019). Winter processes could therefore be related to either prey composition or baseline nitrogen cycling. Our data do not distinguish these paths.

The optical measurement adds a third possibility. Calibration robustness shows that the slope is not eliminated by the examined laboratory-training splits, but does not prove that all selected pixels represent equivalent guano mixtures. Band-attribution calculations are algebraic decompositions of the same optical response and its fitted polynya coefficient; they cannot distinguish biological isotope changes from varying rock, snow, guano or image conditions. Independent prey or amino-acid measurements must match the colony and tissue-formation season. The available feather dataset did not supply such matches for the historical polynya panel, and Signy stomach contents sampled a different location and response. Neither the chlorophyll subset nor these external checks establishes a production-to-diet mechanism.

### 4.4 Scope, prediction and future exposure

The association covers seven historical winters, with eight unevenly informative regional clusters; its later-period point estimate is smaller in magnitude. The direction was discovered after a positive hypothesis failed, and the study accumulated several exploratory analyses before this manuscript was framed. Nominal confidence intervals cannot remove that selection history. Neither regional clustering nor fixed effects guarantees removal of dependence shared across reporting regions or systematic error in the common optical calibration. The absence of forward predictive improvement is a substantive limitation, not an omitted negative result. The unsuccessful between-colony sign-inversion check adds another limit on generalizing the negative annual coefficient into a spatial habitat-selection or opposing trophic process. In the externally dated 2010 Mertz iceberg event, a pronounced local polynya contraction was not accompanied by the positive optical isotope change predicted by a naïve transfer of that coefficient. Concurrent summer ice and temperature changes, geographically distant controls, and only one post-event year prevent using the event as an instrument for isolating the effect of polynya area. Thus the event contrast reinforces the distinction between a conditional observational relationship and a transportable mechanistic response. The 2013–2021 extension also remains untested: a fixed classifier recovered only 64.8% of previously labelled guano-positive observations in its 2008–2012 validation period, below an 80% criterion. That statistic is a known-positive recovery rate, not overall accuracy or proof of the purity of newly selected pixels (Supplement S5).

Earlier nest-weighted climate-exposure calculations are retained solely as supplementary geographic context. Their denominator contrasts describe exposure of counted colonies; they do not validate the polynya–isotope association. The selected CMIP6 outputs used in that work describe seasonal ice concentration and other physical fields but have not been validated for this local coastal-polynya measure. They consequently support neither a future polynya projection in this analysis nor a forecast of penguin diet. Declining surrounding sea ice can also change whether an open-water area is classified as a polynya, so future ice loss cannot simply be substituted for increasing polynya exposure.

## 5. Conclusion

Larger preceding-winter coastal-polynya fractions were associated with lower subsequent summer optical δ¹⁵N at sampled Adélie colonies, conditional on the measured summer environment and fixed effects. Calibration and region-omission checks retained the negative point estimates, and the main interval remained below zero with cluster-jackknife uncertainty. The preceding-winter point estimate was more negative than the following-winter estimate, but their difference was uncertain. Its exploratory origin, few and unevenly informative regions, weaker later-period estimate and lack of predictive improvement constrain interpretation. Matched measurements of prey composition, isotope baselines and guano optical properties are needed before this seasonal association can be translated into a dietary mechanism or future ecological projection.
