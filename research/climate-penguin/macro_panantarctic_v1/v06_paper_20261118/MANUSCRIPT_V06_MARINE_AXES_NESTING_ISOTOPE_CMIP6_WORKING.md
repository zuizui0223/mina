# Nest-weighted geography changes estimated marine climate exposure of Antarctic penguins

**Integrated manuscript candidate v0.6 — 18 November 2026.** Source-verified retrospective macroecological synthesis; not submitted, not a causal attribution study. The complete and previously reviewed v0.5 manuscript, its main figures, Paper 1/Ecology Report, Paper 2, and the repository main branch remain unchanged. This file is the single new full-length working manuscript; earlier diagnostic documents are source provenance, not separate companion manuscripts.

## Abstract

Assessments of Antarctic penguin climate exposure often give each breeding site equal weight, although recorded nests are strongly concentrated among sites and regions. We integrated source-verified colony censuses (2,456 observations from 595 catalogued sites), historical coastal marine conditions and three selected climate models to compare environmental change at site locations with change under fixed recorded nest weights. For the 271 sites with count observations in 2011–2020, the late-century high-emissions model-average reduction in coastal sea ice was 28.63 percentage points when sites were weighted equally but 13.28 points when weighted by observed nest medians. The resulting 15.36-point difference comprised a 15.66-point between-region component, offset by a −0.30-point within-region component. These differences describe geographic exposure, not demographic buffering. In historical cohorts of revisited Adélie colonies, the nest-weighted centre shifted 88–107 kilometres south between the 1990s and 2010s, even as corresponding weighted coastal sea-ice concentrations differed by only 0.02–0.63 percentage points. In a separate 23-site panel, reallocation of nest weights contributed 2.25 points to the change in mean sea-ice exposure, comprising 2.66 points between reporting regions and −0.41 points within regions. The within-region sign reversed when only 17 marine-complete sites were retained. Among six matched Adélie–gentoo censuses, the gentoo-to-Adélie nest ratio increased at every site, but the two-species subtotal rose at five. Marine conditions were not reducible to a single ice gradient: salinity formed a second statistical dimension and showed a positive but regionally inconclusive association with a satellite-derived nitrogen-isotope proxy. Future sea-ice and salinity changes were also geographically model-dependent. These contrasts show that spatial denominators and observation coverage materially alter estimated climate exposure; they do not identify climate-driven dispersal, prey switching or future penguin abundance.

**Keywords:** *Pygoscelis adeliae*; sea-ice concentration; surface salinity; breeding-site census; spatial sampling; emission scenarios.

## 1. Introduction

Sea ice is one of the strongest organizing elements of Antarctic marine ecosystems. Through access to prey, travel conditions, recruitment and seasonal habitats, it can affect penguin populations. Long-term Adélie population growth has already been linked nonlinearly to prevailing sea-ice conditions, although annual variability is less predictable (Iles et al. 2020). In the western Antarctic Peninsula and Scotia Sea, both Adélie and chinstrap declines challenged simple species-specific expectations based on contrasting winter ice preferences (Trivelpiece et al. 2011). Consequently, another correlation between penguin counts and sea ice, without identifying the observational scale and geographic denominator, has limited ecological novelty.

Two developments motivate a more discriminating synthesis. First, Antarctic coastal marine change is not a scalar warming gradient. Sea ice, upper-ocean thermal conditions, and freshwater/salinity conditions can covary differently across sectors and periods. Salinity may reflect sea-ice production and melt, glacial freshwater inputs, circulation and mixing; these processes need not produce the same local temporal history. Recent satellite evidence of increased Southern Ocean surface salinity after 2015 (Silvano et al. 2025) also cautions against assuming that all intervals have the same observed direction.

Second, biological change is not one variable. Redistribution of nest counts *among fixed known colonies*, species-specific changes in two-colony assemblage counts, and variation in guano isotope composition measure different ecological states. Colony count redistribution is not the location change of tracked birds. A growing species' relative share is not necessarily a declining total number of recorded nests. Finally, predicted guano δ¹⁵N is not identical to direct krill or fish intake: it reflects trophic position, environmental isotope baselines and optical prediction error. Youngflesh et al. (2026) already demonstrated that Landsat-derived penguin diet variation is associated with sea ice and long-term abundance. An additional independent salinity association is interesting only if its source, timing, environmental adjustment and spatial scope are explicit.

Our central question is **how much the geographic distribution of actually recorded nests, rather than the number of catalogued breeding sites, changes estimates of historical and projected Antarctic coastal marine exposure**. We distinguish three linked but non-interchangeable observational contrasts: (i) site-equal versus nest-median-weighted physical exposure at fixed known colony locations, with source reporting-region decomposition and coverage sensitivity; (ii) historical nest redistribution and paired-species nesting composition, without treating observed count changes as individual movement; and (iii) the additional salinity dimension and its geographically limited association with a satellite-derived nitrogen-isotope proxy. We separately describe projected sea-ice and salinity changes from three selected climate models, without transferring observational coefficients into future nest counts. The resulting paper is a measurement- and geography-aware macroecological account, not a demonstration of adaptive dispersal, a validated population model or a new mechanism for prey switching.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1 Original nesting observations and strict observation units

Our fixed observational source is a three-species, method-filtered MAPPPD-derived colony cohort. We retained a unique colony identifier and location, each actual survey season, source species, genuinely reported nest count and its survey method. Within each site–species series the most frequently used known method was retained, producing **2,456 observed site–species–season records at 595 previously catalogued colony identifiers in 18 source reporting regions**. Missing species or unvisited seasons were never filled with zero. A source-reported count of zero was retained as an observed count but does not automatically prove ecological absence outside the surveyed footprint.

Colony records differ in coverage, observer methods and collection years. They are not a complete annual census of Antarctica. We therefore define site rosters before computing temporal changes. Nest-count-weighted summaries describe the distribution of *recorded nests among these sites*, not the path of identifiable penguins, first occupation of an unvisited nesting patch or a species-range displacement. Repeated measures and shared local marine grids are not independent climate realizations.

### 2.2 Historical marine environment and its two statistical axes

We used original OSI SAF satellite sea-ice concentration records for August–November 1991–2020, summarized within fixed original nearshore 100-km observation supports. Original Copernicus GLORYS12V1 202311 surface practical salinity and potential seawater temperature were matched to the nearest validated wet ocean cell, requiring ≤25-km geodesic distance, for August–November 1993–2020. This produced **588 marine-matched colony sites, 378 distinct native GLORYS ocean cells, 18 regions and 16,464 site×year physical records**. Identical ocean cells shared by several nearby colonies were retained as a known form of dependence.

The marine principal component analysis (PCA) was computed once, using the three variables standardized across the complete 16,464-row historical panel and **without using biological outcomes**. Component 1 was signed toward warmer sea temperature and lower sea ice; component 2 was signed toward greater salinity. Variance fractions and loadings were checked against the originally frozen physical analysis. Because PCA produces orthogonal axes by construction, these components are environmental *descriptors*, not proof that ice loss and freshening are causally independent.

### 2.3 Decadal nest-count redistribution and two-species assemblage states

For Adélie nesting redistribution we retained the same original colony locations between 1991–2000 and 2011–2020. Within each decade each colony was weighted by the median of its actually recorded counts. Nested rosters required (i) at least two observed seasons in each of all three decades (19 colonies), (ii) two in each endpoint decade (26 colonies), or (iii) one in each endpoint decade (35 colonies). We computed the change in the nest-weighted mean south latitude, converting each degree to 111.195 km, and compared the matched observed nest-weighted nearshore SIC. Regional and spatial-block decompositions were descriptive identities; they are sensitive to source boundaries and do not identify bird immigration or demographic survival. As a separate coverage sensitivity, we used an original, source-fixed 1993–2000 versus 2011–2020 Adélie cohort requiring two genuine source census years per interval. The 23-site panel had complete nearshore SIC; six large Victoria Land colonies were absent from matched GLORYS salinity and SST, leaving 17 complete-ocean sites. For each panel, we decomposed the symmetric nest-reallocation contribution to mean coastal SIC exposure, Σᵢ(wᵢ₁−wᵢ₀)(Eᵢ₀+Eᵢ₁)/2, exactly into between-source-region and within-source-region terms by nesting normalized site shares within each region. We also recomputed the terms after omitting each recorded site and reporting region. As a conditional source-year sensitivity, we additionally resampled actually observed annual nest counts with replacement within each site and early/late period (4,999 common parent23/marine17 draws) and recalculated the site median and region decomposition without inventing unobserved years. The draw quantiles describe sensitivity to recorded census-year medians, not ecological population confidence intervals. No null was used to infer individual birds' location selection, and no missing ocean observations were imputed.

For composition, we required the *two species* to have genuine census records at the same colony, in overlapping years, with source citation and survey method matched. A strict long-term subset contained six Adélie/gentoo paired-colony series, all in two source regions. We compared changes in the slope of log(1 + gentoo nests) – log(1 + Adélie nests) with changes in the first-to-last observed sum of both species' nest counts, and retained their true first/last source survey years. The ratio and subtotal answer different questions. Strict citation matching cannot guarantee identical observation day or complete colony coverage.

### 2.4 Original-image Adélie optical isotope–salinity association

To examine a biological marine association outside conventional census counts, we used the original published data of Youngflesh et al. (2026), Zenodo 10.5281/zenodo.17409948 (original 256,425,816-byte archive; source MD5 e29a2f68c84e3a515044d554540def2f). Our reproduction used 103 original guano stable-isotope calibration observations, published spectral-response profiles of Landsat sensors 4/5/7, and five-component partial least squares (PLSR) to reconstruct a **guano optical δ¹⁵N proxy**. Sensor-specific 100-repeat stratified calibration holdout RMSE medians for the original single-model reconstruction used in the present **860-record salinity analysis** were approximately 1.72–1.74‰. A subsequent source-matched sensitivity reconstructed the original authors' R oscorespls five-component, 1,000-fit calibration ensembles for Landsat 4/5/7 and verified the previously observed winter-polynya slopes in the **same historical 439- and 358-record subsets** (Supplementary source audit). That later sensitivity **does not re-estimate the present 860-record salinity coefficient, validate a new study period or reproduce the published multistage Stan isotope/diet posterior**. In particular, neither optical reconstruction should be relabeled a measured fish fraction or an independent dietary response.

We recovered the original ≥2-average-pixel-per-colony image support gate and then required exact colony site ID and unambiguous spatial matching within 2 km to original MAPPPD colony locations. The resulting **860 genuine colony×breeding-season records (88 Adélie sites, 82 distinct GLORYS wet cells, 13 reporting regions, 15 source seasons)** had Nov–following-February source salinity and seawater temperature, and the original NOAA/NSIDC G02202 version 4 sea-ice source approximated by a 150-km native-pixel-center buffer. Satellite image day and image support were retained.

The fixed observational model was the day-equal season-average optical δ¹⁵N as a function of observed-day timing (linear and squared), Nov–following-February sea ice, seawater temperature and salinity, with site and source region×breeding-season fixed effects. It was estimated using a two-way fixed-effect residualization with wet marine cell clusters, and repeated with 13 source-region clusters. We report the conditional regression slope and residual partial R². Region-specific slopes, omitted-region checks, site-specific imaging controls and both temporally shifted salinity and within-region marine-cell trajectory rearrangements are **post-result exploratory sensitivity checks**, not randomized or prospective causal experiments.

The source site/year overlap with direct nesting data is imperfect. Only 70 actual Adélie nest count records occur at exactly the same 49 original isotope-site seasons, and only 29 records at eight repeatedly counted colony sites can support a within-colony count/isotope exploratory regression. There are **no** matching same-colony/same-season records between the 860 optical isotope records and the 351 actual two-species same-site co-census pairs. This prevents treating the isotope and species-composition analyses as a mediation model.

### 2.5 Future physical changes, source-model agreement and geographic grain

We reused the original pinned 27 CMIP6 Zarr stores from **ACCESS-CM2**, **BCC-CSM2-MR** and **CESM2**, with one selected ensemble member per model. Variables were modeled sea-ice concentration (siconc, percentage points), sea-surface salinity (sos, native practical-salinity-scale 0.001 units) and *near-surface air temperature* (tas, °C). Model historical source reference was 1995–2014; the four future comparisons were SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 × 2041–2060 and 2081–2100, all using August–November means. The full extraction contains **21,420 site×model×variable×future scenario records** with complete source periods at all 595 known colony locations.

For each site we distinguished the sign of the **three-model mean** from agreement among **each of the three models**. We report the number of known sites where all three models separately project decreasing salinity, decreasing sea ice, or both. To compare physical exposure of observed nesting, fixed site weights were the 2011–2020 observed median nest counts of each site–species, summed over species: 331 site–species medians at 271 actually counted colony sites, total 2,848,329.5 summed median-nest proxy. This is not a contemporaneous Antarctic nesting total. The remaining catalogued sites have *unknown* modern nesting counts, not zeros. A separate physical grid-quality analysis retained only 158 of those counted sites at which all three models' original sea-ice and salinity grid centers lie ≤50 km from the colony (80.21% of recorded nest-median weight). Grid distance is a support-quality filter, not a penguin foraging radius.

We compared model-specific *changes* in sea ice and salinity across sites, after demeaning within original reporting regions, and after reducing colony grid repetition to one model SOS ocean cell × source region. We held source geography fixed in 18 leave-one-region-out calculations. For a historical sanity check, we compared each model's 1995–2014 absolute SOS and siconc means with corresponding GLORYS reanalysis salinity and OSI SAF nearshore sea ice at identical original site IDs and months. These are different gridded products. They measure historical geographical agreement, **not** skill in predicting late-century change. CMIP6 tas is not interchangeable with GLORYS seawater thetao; similarly, a model Aug–Nov SOS change is not the Nov–Feb local GLORYS salinity predictor in the isotope analysis. We did not multiply the historical δ¹⁵N coefficient by future modeled salinity to predict isotope, prey or nesting outcomes.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Historical marine conditions contain both thermal–ice and salinity dimensions

The original 588-site, 16,464-year–site marine panel was not well summarized as one ice-and-warming axis. PC1 explained **60.3654%** of variance, with loadings approximately −0.707 (sea ice), +0.706 (potential seawater temperature) and −0.038 (salinity). PC2 explained **33.3773%**, with a salinity loading +0.998 and much smaller ice and temperature loadings (Fig. 1). This reproduces a strong salinity-associated physical dimension alongside the thermal–ice component.

Neither this eigenvector separation nor the absolute geographic gradient establishes what salinity does biologically. Source ocean cells occur repeatedly across colony IDs, and the strongest temporal salinity synchronization in 2019–2020 remains sensitive to source-era changes in the GLORYS product. Hence the two-axis interpretation is a physically supported description of this source panel, not a validated annual environmental mechanism in all regions.

### 3.2 Reallocation among known Adélie colonies and relative gentoo–Adélie nesting

Across the 19-site Adélie roster with records in every decade, the nest-count-weighted position became **107.31 km more southerly** between the 1990s and 2010s. The 26-site endpoint roster yielded **88.41 km**, and the 35-site endpoint roster **99.60 km** (Fig. 2a). The corresponding differences in fixed-site nest-weighted nearshore sea-ice exposure were only +0.022, +0.625 and +0.908 percentage points, respectively. These are three *nested observation rosters* rather than independent replications. The reweighting occurred without any site coordinates moving; it does not demonstrate individual southward dispersal, niche tracking, colonization or a uniform environmental response.

In the independently defined 23-site SIC-only sensitivity, the observed nest-reweighting contribution was **+2.252 percentage points**, partitioned into **+2.663 between reporting regions** and **−0.412 within regions**. Restriction to the 17 sites with complete SIC, salinity and temperature changed the same terms to **+5.584**, **+5.336** and **+0.247 percentage points**, respectively. Those 17 sites retained only **50.6% of earlier and 39.9% of later observed nest-median weight** in the original 23-site source cohort. All 23 leave-one-site and all four leave-one-region exclusions retained a positive between-region term. The negative 23-site within-region term turned positive if the large CRZW site was excluded, and the positive overall reallocation did not survive every region exclusion. These are descriptive source-coverage sensitivity results, not confidence intervals or independent climate-response replicates (source checks and exact decomposition in Supplementary Methods). Under 4,999 source-year resamples, the 23-site between-region term was positive in **all 4,999 draws** (conditional 2.5–97.5% **+2.255 to +3.039 pp**), whereas its within-region component crossed zero (**−0.673 to +0.034 pp**). The positive between-region direction thus survived variation among original observed annual counts; the within-region sign and regional population generality remain unresolved. In a separate explicitly post-result diagnostic that exhaustively reassigned the four observed regional growth ratios to the four fixed sea-ice geographies, the actual positive between-region component ranked **2nd of 24** (23 sites) and **3rd of 24** (17 sites). Under 4,999 within-site observed census-year resamples, the actual region-growth arrangement ranked in the top two in only **53.4%** and **19.4%** of draws, respectively. Regional growth rates are not ecologically exchangeable, so these are descriptive ranks, not permutation-test p values or evidence of active climate-niche tracking.

At all **six strictly source/citation/method-matched Adélie–gentoo colony series**, the gentoo-to-Adélie log nest ratio increased. In the same six matched series, Adélie log nests decreased and gentoo log nests increased. Yet the observed two-species nest subtotal rose at **five of six**, with one decline (Fig. 2b). Thus a larger gentoo share is not equivalent to a falling recorded combined nesting subtotal. Four of the six matched sites had negative nearshore SIC time slopes and two had positive slopes. Those facts describe real local assemblage restructuring, not competitive replacement or an Antarctic-wide temperature effect.

### 3.3 Salinity adds positive, geographically heterogeneous information about an Adélie isotope proxy

In **860 original-image Adélie site×season records**, the positive conditional slope of optical δ¹⁵N against Nov–Feb surface salinity remained after accounting for same-season sea ice, potential seawater temperature, imaging day, site identity and region-by-season variation: **β = +0.8341458‰ per salinity unit**. Adding salinity to the model reduced the preceding conditional residual sum of squares by **2.88018%**. The source-marine-cell-cluster normal 95% interval was **+0.389 to +1.280**; a conservative small-cluster interval using only **13 reporting-region groups** was **−0.016 to +1.684** and included zero (Fig. 3).

Regional coefficients were not uniform. Within-region exploratory models suggested positive associations in Victoria Land and Wilkes Land, a negative association in the southwest Antarctic Peninsula, and a near-zero coefficient in the northeast Peninsula; only eight reporting regions met minimum multi-site/marine-cell support. The pooled relation could also be highly conditioned on geographic sample selection: in four regions where co-censused penguin species pairs occurred, the same isotope model yielded **β = +0.080** and a residual partial R² of 0.043% from 227 site×seasons. That reduction was not simply because residualized salinity variation vanished there.

A source-matched spatial rearrangement of complete wet-cell salinity trajectories within reporting regions yielded far smaller conditional residual R² than the true matched geography in most of 1,000 post-result rearrangements; however, model-specific marine cells are not physically exchangeable experimental units. On a matched 721-site-season subpanel, *following-season* salinity was associated with current isotope in the opposite direction, warning that temporal autocorrelation, environmental baselines or other processes may contribute to the present-season association. The source-matched POM isotope measurements necessary to separate shifting food-web δ¹⁵N baselines from prey use are rare: only **20/860** isotope seasons had a same-season POM observation within 100 km. Our positive salinity–δ¹⁵N relationship is therefore an exploratory **biological isotope-proxy association**, not an identified fish-to-krill dietary switch or a salinity-mediated mechanism of population growth.

The exact overlap with observed same-site Adélie nesting yielded 70 seasons from 49 sites; only eight sites had repeats (29 site×seasons). A site-equal regression on those repeated records gave a positive δ¹⁵N coefficient of +0.134 in log(1 + nesting count) units after marine and temporal controls, but its eight-site clustered interval was **−0.149 to +0.418** and the coefficient fell to about +0.004 upon excluding Victoria Land. This exploratory count link is insufficient for a regionally transferable demographic inference and is not the first reported diet–abundance association (Youngflesh et al. 2026).

### 3.4 Future sea ice and salinity are spatially related in model-dependent ways

In SSP5-8.5 late-century projections, the three-model mean projected a **−21.44-percentage-point** sea-ice difference across the 595 equally weighted known sites and a **−0.252** source-unit mean salinity difference. On the **same 271 source-counted sites**, the mean projected sea-ice loss was **−28.63 percentage points under equal site weights**, compared with **−13.276 percentage points under fixed observed nest-median weights** (difference **+15.36 points**). Exact source-region accounting separated this gap into **+15.66 points between reporting regions** and **−0.30 points within regions**. The fixed-nest-weighted salinity difference was **−0.257** source units; it is not a modeled salinity effect on nests. This within-271 comparison is a distinct denominator from the equally weighted 595-site average, not a test of population buffering. The smaller nest-weighted sea-ice loss arises overwhelmingly from the current recorded nest distribution *between reporting regions*, particularly Victoria Land, rather than a measured within-colony response or guaranteed persistence of future nesting.

Although the **three-model mean** SOS difference was negative at every one of the 595 known site locations, all three models separately projected negative salinity at **464/595 (78.0%)**. They jointly projected *both* negative salinity and negative sea ice at **384/595 (64.5%)**. Among 271 recently counted sites, 179 had unanimous negative salinity and 152 unanimous negative *both*; their fixed nest-median shares were **93.54%** and **59.08%**, respectively (Fig. 4b). These are fractions of *previously observed nesting weights at physically exposed locations*, not probabilities of population decline.

Model disagreement extended beyond the number of locations with the same sign. For SSP5-8.5 and 2081–2100, the model-specific raw geographical correlations across the same 595 known sites between projected changes in sea-ice concentration and salinity were **+0.743** (ACCESS-CM2), **−0.296** (BCC-CSM2-MR) and **−0.333** (CESM2). Restricting each model's climatic units to unique native SOS ocean cells×reporting region and removing region means yielded **+0.404**, **−0.178**, and **−0.350**, respectively (Fig. 4a). The regional-within-site sign persisted through omission of each original reporting region in turn. Model-to-model variation also changed with SSP and century period, with ACCESS-CM2 reversing its spatial coupling sign between near and far periods. Hence a model mean that projects broad freshening does **not** fix a universal location-specific relationship between ice retreat and freshening.

### 3.5 Historical absolute conditions warn against fine-scale biological transfer

When the three selected climate models' 1995–2014 August–November absolute marine means were compared against the corresponding original 588-site GLORYS and OSI SAF sources, mean model-minus-GLORYS salinity offsets were approximately +0.076, −0.059 and −0.440 for ACCESS-CM2, BCC-CSM2-MR and CESM2. Salinity source-site correlations after removing regional means were only **+0.204**, **+0.208** and **+0.248**, whereas sea-ice comparisons gave **+0.676**, **+0.367** and **+0.610**. These are historical geographically averaged patterns; they do not quantify future trend verification. They do show why model coarse-cell future salinity should not be substituted for precisely observed colony-specific source ocean salinity or used as an uncalibrated isotope threshold.

At all 88 historical isotope-observed site IDs, all four CMIP6 physical scenario panels were available. In SSP5-8.5 late century the site-mean projected salinity difference was −0.328; all three models agreed on negative salinity at **80/88**, but on negative salinity and ice jointly at only **43/88**. On the 46 sites passing all-model ≤50-km wet-grid checks, those counts fell to **40 and 17**. We did not calculate future δ¹⁵N: these historical observations are from Nov–Feb and 1996–2012, whereas model physical changes are from Aug–Nov and future decades, with different ocean model grid cells and a different temperature variable. Apparent data coverage is not causal biological transferability.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 A two-dimensional physical setting, not a one-dimensional ecological response

Three independent observations support a more careful macroecological description of Antarctic change. First, historical near-colony marine variation contains a large thermal–ice component and a distinct salinity-associated component. Second, source-reconstructed Adélie δ¹⁵N carries an additional positive association with salinity conditional on a sea-ice and seawater-temperature measure, although this relationship is regionally heterogeneous and its effective geographical replication is limited. Third, future modeled ice loss and freshening are widespread but are **not geographically coupled in the same direction across three models**. These findings justify showing salinity as a separate marine climate axis rather than treating it as a trivial or rejected covariate.

At the same time, physical separation and a positive isotope coefficient are not a causal chain. Salinity is affected by meltwater, sea-ice brine rejection, circulation and stratification, all of which may also influence available food, plankton isotopic baselines and the optical properties of guano. The observed isotope relationship could represent several processes and need not imply that fish consumption itself increases with salinity. Previous continental satellite diet and sea-ice associations (Youngflesh et al. 2026) mean that our contribution cannot be the simple discovery that penguin diets and ice covary. Instead, its scope is a **source-aligned conditional salinity association and the limits on spatial generalization**.

### 4.2 The biological units prevent a universal climate-to-breeding story

The distribution of nest counts among previously known colonies moved toward more southerly locations in the source-fixed Adélie comparison. This does not mean that individual penguins migrated south or that Antarctic colonies uniformly shifted their climate niche; large changes in the numbers observed at immobile sites can produce the same aggregate. Moreover, a local increase in gentoo's share of co-surveyed nests did not invariably imply loss of the combined observed two-species nesting total. These two biological contrasts are ecological states in their own right, not noisy versions of a single population response. The 23-versus-17-site sea-ice accounting also exposes a stricter geographical distinction: **a positive change in exposure caused by aggregate colony-count weights need not be produced by positive sorting among colonies within the same reporting region**. The positive between-region term survived complete-marine-data selection and all region exclusions, whereas the within-region term changed sign with coverage and with one influential colony. This is evidence for a region-composition effect in a monitored sample, not a demonstration that birds intentionally track sea-ice niches or that shifting colony weight confers population resilience. The observed four-region growth-ratio assignment was near the positive edge of an exhaustive 24-label comparison, but that ranking was sensitive to the years used to construct site count medians, particularly in the 17-site ocean-complete panel. Given only four nonexchangeable reporting regions and post-result hypothesis selection, this rank contrast is not sufficient evidence of a reproducible ecological selection mechanism.

It is tempting to connect sea-ice retreat, salt freshening, isotope changes and nest redistribution into a single predictive path. The matched data do not support that link. There are no same-site same-season matches between the 860 isotope records and 351 genuine co-census pair records; only seven original source region×season units overlap, in different colonies. Only eight original Adélie colonies contribute repeated same-season isotope and nesting values, and their estimated slope is regionally fragile. Even where real associations exist in each data set, their observation units are not interchangeable. This mismatch is a result about **what can be established from available Antarctic monitoring**, not evidence that a biological mechanism cannot exist.

### 4.3 Model uncertainty changes which present breeding locations face joint physical exposure

Late-century modeled surface freshening extends widely, but the three models give markedly different maps of the co-occurrence of salt and ice change. The model-average salinity trend can appear smooth and consistently negative while individual models disagree on where ice loss and freshening co-vary. This matters when describing environmental change at real colony locations: a single source-average ice metric will not convey the model-dependent freshwater regime.

Present-day nesting-weighted summaries further differ from site-equal summaries, even when both are evaluated on exactly the same 271 recently counted source sites: −28.63 versus −13.28 percentage points of projected late-century sea-ice change, respectively. Their +15.36-point contrast reflects predominantly **between-reporting-region nesting geography** (+15.66 points), with an opposite-sign within-region contribution (−0.30). These figures provide ecological **exposure accounting**, not demographic buffering or adaptation. In particular, sites with no source counts during 2011–2020 cannot be treated as biological zeros, and the census medians are from non-synchronous source years. Large repeatedly counted colonies in Victoria Land carry disproportionate fixed nesting weight, while source-model ocean cells can cover several colony identifiers. The defensible future result is therefore a physical-exposure comparison that reports **equal-site and source counted-nest weights, their denominators, model agreement and regional influence separately**, not a map of future colony persistence or species-specific reproductive success.

### 4.4 The bounded advance beyond existing penguin projections

Previous work already demonstrated regional contrasts in Adélie nesting change (Lynch & LaRue 2014), sea-ice associations with penguin population growth (Iles et al. 2020), and satellite guano-based relationships among diet, ice and abundance (Youngflesh et al. 2026). More recent studies projected future marine habitat suitability and conservation priorities for penguins (Ramírez et al. 2026), modeled Antarctic ecosystem value with penguin colony counts and demographic projections (DuVivier et al. 2026), and documented pronounced penguin community change at a major colony (Ratcliffe et al. 2026). Neither colony weighting, climate projections, nor species replacement is therefore novel in principle. A salinity dimension also does not imply a previously unknown independent physical mechanism (Silvano et al. 2025).

The more specific contribution here is **source-checked, paired-denominator accounting of projected physical exposure at the same real colony locations**, linked to historical count reweighting and explicit geographic and measurement-support diagnostics. Under the selected source climate models, equal-location and fixed observed-nest weighting led to a 15.36-percentage-point difference in projected ice loss across the same 271 counted sites; most of that contrast was due to source-reporting-region composition. Historical 23-versus-17 colony comparisons independently reveal how excluding large colonies without nearby reanalysis cells changes within-region accounting, although these panels are not independent biological replications of the future model analysis. The conditional salinity-isotope association and the six-site paired-species comparison supply contextual measurement contrasts, not independent evidence for a single causal chain.

The limits are substantial. The 271-site source cohort is not the full contemporary census; nest medians reflect non-synchronous observation years, region membership is not randomized, and a small number of large colonies dominate several contrasts. Coastal reanalysis or coarse future-model grid cells do not directly measure feeding routes or prey accessibility. The historical isotope-proxy salinity slope is positive but includes zero in its conservative reporting-region interval, and independent matched diet-baseline data are insufficient. Only three selected climate models were used, with spatially variable biases. The findings should consequently be evaluated as **an Antarctic ecological monitoring and exposure-estimation study**, not a global biological response law, an adaptive-refuge mechanism, or a population forecast.

The priority for stronger biological interpretation is genuinely matched spatial and temporal evidence: consistent colony-level counts, independent prey or compound-specific isotope baselines, and colony-associated marine conditions across more source regions. Additional post hoc radius, lag or threshold searches on the same panels cannot substitute for this information.

## 5. Conclusion

At the same 271 source-counted Antarctic penguin colonies, equal-site and fixed recorded-nest weighting yield markedly different late-century coastal sea-ice exposure estimates (−28.63 versus −13.28 percentage points under one higher-emissions scenario and three selected climate models). The corresponding geographic-accounting difference is mainly between reporting regions, not evidence that individuals tracked sea ice. Historical fixed-colony Adélie reweighting and the sensitivity of within-region estimates to marine-data coverage demonstrate why the spatial denominator must be explicit. Salinity adds descriptive marine variation but its isotope-proxy association is not regionally conclusive; species-ratio changes and climate scenarios are separate observational domains. These results support a **count-aware, source-limited description of physical climate exposure**, not inferred adaptation, prey switching or projected breeding abundance.
