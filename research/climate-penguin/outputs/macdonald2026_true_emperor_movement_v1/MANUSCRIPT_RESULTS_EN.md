# Results: Ice-breeding Emperor penguins show within-season spatial mobility, without ubiquitous annual relocation of colony centroids

To test whether the physical mobility of fast-ice breeding platforms produces whole-colony relocation, we reconstructed time-stamped, georeferenced breeding-group locations from the openly archived observations of Macdonald et al. (2026) at three Antarctic Emperor penguin colonies: Atka Bay, Coulman Island and Cape Washington. The 2017–2024 original UK Polar Data Centre archive (DOI 10.5285/e1e00e5d-fc4c-4948-9e00-8d02e8b359d9) comprised **24 source year layers (144 shapefile components), 1,110 dated satellite observations, and 3,681 independently mapped penguin-group coordinates**. Esri MultiPoint geometry required decoding each recorded group instead of assigning a single coordinate to each image. **636/1,110 observations (57.3%)** contained more than one mapped group. The WGS84 source-member canonical SHA-256 is independently pinned and verified.

### Within-season movement versus contemporaneous group fragmentation

We characterized a satellite record using the unweighted geographic centroid of its detected group locations and retained the maximum distance to any source-recorded individual group. Across **24 colony-years**, the median of the largest pairwise centroid distance within the winter observation window was **0.776 km**, compared with **2.091 km** for spring/summer. The latter was larger in **22/24 colony-years**, although observation-period duration, frequency and optical/SAR sensor mix were not matched. A colony-year's largest centroid distance from its first image exceeded 2 km in **12/24** cases; at least one detected group lay more than 2 km from its initial image centroid in **24/24**. Among Atka Bay years, the >2-km *transient maximum centroid* displacement occurred in four of eight years; the comparable counts were six of eight at Coulman and two of eight at Cape Washington.

The furthest individual detected Atka group from that year's initial unweighted centroid was **5.550 km in 2023**, close to the approximate 5.57 km reported in the primary publication (different precise matching definitions may apply). The first-to-last observed colony-year centroid movement was substantially smaller: median **0.891 km**, with **3/24** colony-years exceeding 2 km. Transient seasonal excursions therefore need not translate to a net persistent shift in the mapped footprint.

### Year-to-year comparison at the same breeding-season phase

To avoid conflating a year's last summer position with the next breeding season's first location, we compared the detected multi-group centroid on the original image nearest to **15 September (±15 days)** and separately nearest to **15 October (±10 days)**. All **21** across-year comparisons among the three colonies had September support, and **19** had October support. Only **one** September comparison and **one** October comparison involved more than 2 km of centroid displacement. The September outlier was Atka Bay, 2021–2022 (**2.319 km**).

This describes an observed seasonal-geometry contrast: recurring colonies may show substantial movement of component groups within a season while their same-phase centroid locations are usually closer between years. It does **not** indicate individual site fidelity or absence of fast-ice break-up. Furthermore, the median discrepancy in observed unweighted group centroids from different satellite images on the **same calendar day** was **0.567 km over 36 image pairs**. Sub-kilometre interannual differences are therefore close to the scale of within-day imagery and group-detection variation; they cannot be treated as precisely measured biological relocation distances.

The original year-coded shapefiles followed the breeding-season starting year and contained **51** source observations dated January–March in the following calendar year, a distinct detail that was retained rather than reassigned to a second breeding season.

### Implication for the terrestrial versus ice-breeding substrate hypothesis

These independently observed ice-breeding positions establish that mapped Emperor breeding-group spatial organization can be locally dynamic. They neither substantiate the prediction that ice-breeding populations primarily lose complete colonies nor connect individual colony displacement to whole-continent abundance decline. A separately analyzed 50-colony LaRue et al. (2024) abundance model found a decline in the inverse-Simpson effective number of colonies of **10.39%** from marginal posterior mean indices (2009–2018), but almost all of that change reflected allocation among colonies retaining positive model indices; removing only the two model-zero endpoint sites from baseline would change N_eff by just **0.61%**. That independent model has no year-specific colony-coordinate trajectories, and its 2009–2018 global panel cannot be identified with these three tracked 2017–2024 colony-year distributions.

In contrast, the previously observed Palmer/Signy terrestrial-breeding Pygoscelis concentration occurs *within* colonies using nest-count spatial sectors, not among 50 continental colonies. This observational and spatial-scale mismatch prevents identifying breeding-substrate causality. The testable macroecological distinction is thus not whether ice colonies always disappear while land colonies contract, but whether substrate dynamics alter the proportion of spatial change attributable to within-season location shifts, temporary occupancy turnover, persistent-colony reweighting, and within-colony sector contraction **under comparable ecological and observation units**.

### Observability and lag-matched checks for seasonal displacement

A winter-anchored comparison can artificially favor later observations; the maximum of all pairwise distances is also sensitive to phase duration and number of images. We therefore performed an additional **reference-free time-lag control** using only consecutive observations separated by **3–14 days within the same winter (April–August) or late-spring (October–November) phase**, deduplicating images at the same calendar day before calculating displacement. Across 24 colony-years there were **451 eligible observed day pairs: 338 winter and 113 late-spring**. Both phases had at least two comparable intervals in **21/24 colony-years**. Late-spring median mapped-group-centroid displacement per elapsed day was higher in **17/21** of those paired site-years, with a median paired difference **+0.01244 km/day**. This measured *image centroid change rate* is neither an estimate of individual penguin locomotion nor fast-ice physical velocity. Different satellites, group detection thresholds, winter/spring image availability and non-independence of years remain confounded.

As a complementary *winter-anchored* control, we used the first three observed winter days as the position reference and compared the median spatial offset of other winter observations against those from October–November. Late-spring centroid displacement was greater in **23/24** colony-years (median of paired differences **+0.635 km**), and the corresponding equally weighted individual group-point offset was greater in **24/24**. These values are transparently susceptible to winter-reference selection and cannot replace the lag-matched control. Same-day images from different sensors showed **median centroid disagreement 0.567 km among 36 pairs, 95th percentile 1.785 km and maximum 2.818 km**. Apparent shifts near 1–2 km can partly reflect changes in mapped group representation, rather than true whole-colony relocation.

### True year-matched overlap with the published 50-colony adult abundance model

Exactly three colonies occur with identical names and original site IDs in both Macdonald et al.'s 2017–2024 movement imagery and LaRue et al.'s earlier 2009–2018 Bayesian adult-index model: **ATKA, COUL, WASH**. For 2017–2018, their modelled marginal-mean adult indices changed **−11.90%, +23.21% and +0.32%**, respectively, while their matched September detected-group centroid shifts were **0.156 km, 0.488 km and 0.220 km**. Within-season mapped-group movement was documented in both observed years for all three sites.

These results are a real *site-and-year crosswalk*, not a general model of movement-induced demographic change. The adult-index endpoint is modelled from a different satellite and observation design than the movement coordinates, there are **only three colonies and one year-to-year abundance transition**, and year-specific motion for the other 47 sites in LaRue's global panel remains unavailable. The fact that site indices changed in opposing directions despite observed within-season mobility makes a universal simplistic "any mobility causes decline" model implausible descriptively, but **does not identify its causal effect**. A test of the platform mechanism must link daily fast-ice fracture/persistence data to these exact mapped group observations and obtain similarly resolved terrestrial breeding-group locations under comparable sensor and detection conditions.
