# Results: Moving emperor penguin breeding footprints are observable independently of modeled colony abundance

The central ice-versus-land substrate hypothesis assumes that the mobility of a sea-ice breeding platform translates into a different mode of spatial breeding reorganization, potentially favoring abrupt colony disappearance rather than gradual within-colony contraction. We independently examined original, time-stamped spatial observations from Macdonald et al. (2026; doi:10.1038/s43247-026-03906-0; tracking dataset doi:10.5285/e1e00e5d-fc4c-4948-9e00-8d02e8b359d9) and linked them to the fixed named colony IDs in the 50-colony model-based population panel published by LaRue et al. (2024).

### Position-source provenance and observability

The original LaRue satellite survey workbook had 669 observations, but all 211 consecutive-year comparisons retaining valid image-position fields and positive detected areas had identical coordinate pairs. These fields behave as stationary colony reference/image locations, and **cannot** be used as evidence for zero colony movement. We then downloaded and checksum-validated the Macdonald 2017–2024 source ZIP, containing exactly 24 WGS84 MultiPoint shapefiles representing 3 emperor penguin colonies (Atka Bay, Coulman Island, Cape Washington) over 8 breeding seasons. Direct parsing of SHP/DBF/PRJ yielded **1,110 date-stamped survey geometries, 3,681 original detected subgroup positions and 1,075 distinct within-site-season observation days**. Fifty-one observations were dated January–February of the subsequent calendar year, consistent with breeding-season file labels rather than missing or fabricated observations.

### Measured changes in mapped group distribution

From the observed source MultiPoints we calculated the centroid of detected group positions at each original observation, and collapsed multiple images on the same calendar date. The median first-to-last detected-group centroid change across 24 colony-years was **0.891 km**, with **three** source colony-years exceeding 2 km. Matching the closest image to 15 September in each successive breeding year yielded **21 comparable interannual pairs**, including **one** detected-centroid change above 2 km; matched mid-October yielded 19 pairs, likewise one above 2 km. Both were Atka Bay between 2021 and 2022 (September **2.319 km**, October **2.044 km**). These distances are displacements of the **set of detected subgroup locations**, not distances travelled by individually identified penguins or stable subgroups.

The same-day imagery quality check yielded 36 pairs of image-specific centroid differences (median **0.567 km**, 95th percentile **1.785 km**, maximum **2.818 km**). Thus differences near 2 km must be interpreted alongside observation/segmentation and subgroup-composition uncertainty; the apparent centroid is not a perfectly measured physical colony center.

### Phenological mobility and reference-point sensitivity

Using the first three observed April–August winter days as each colony-year's origin, mean detected-subgroup displacement was larger in October–November than in winter in **24 of 24 source colony-years**, with a median difference of **0.645 km**. Because choosing a winter origin can favor smaller winter distances by construction, we also compared **baseline-free** centroid changes across consecutive original observations spaced **3–14 days**, normalized by elapsed time. Among 338 winter observation pairs and 113 late-spring pairs, 21 colony-years had at least two observations pairs per season; **17 of 21** had greater median apparent spring centroid displacement rate, with a median within-colony-year late-minus-winter difference of **+0.01244 km per day**. These results reproduce the original paper's evidence of increased spring/summer mobility, but SAR winter imagery, optical spring imagery, and changing numbers of detected subgroups confound the exact rate and prohibit interpreting it as penguin locomotion speed or an independent biological response to climate.

### Same-site overlap with the modeled 50-colony abundance source

Three Macdonald colony names matched the original LaRue fixed-ID model output exactly (ATKA, COUL, WASH), each with strictly positive modeled adult indices in both years of common coverage, 2017 and 2018. The 2017→2018 posterior marginal mean adult-index change was **−11.90% at Atka Bay**, **+23.21% at Coulman Island**, and **+0.32% at Cape Washington**. All had spatial observations of multiple colony-group positions. Consequently, physical within-season redistribution of a breeding colony's detected footprint can be observed without that named colony becoming modelled unoccupied, and is not equivalent to the **between-colony relative adult-index reweighting** summarized by the previously reproduced global 2009–2018 inverse-Simpson N_eff loss.

This n=3 paired source crosswalk is descriptive; it cannot identify the direction of a movement–abundance relation, predict extinction or infer whether mobile sea ice causes colony disappearance. The 50-colony posterior aggregation does not include annual subgroup coordinates, and the 3-colony movement study covers a distinct period and observation process. No matched annual terrestrial Pygoscelis colony-group centroid dataset was available for an equivalent mobility contrast.

**Implication:** A substrate-based macroecological test should distinguish (i) physical centroid/footprint changes, (ii) modelled occupation or temporary absence, and (iii) effective number and relative abundance allocation among fixed colony IDs. The formerly proposed mechanism that whole-colony loss dominates spatial concentration among ice breeders was not supported by the modelled 50-colony decomposition, despite independent direct evidence that emperor breeding group positions are mobile during the season. A causal difference between fixed terrestrial islands and mobile fast ice remains an open hypothesis, not a demonstrated result.
