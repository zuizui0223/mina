# Island capacity as a switch in the spatial scale of Adélie redistribution — hypothesis v2

**Status:** revised mechanistic hypothesis generated after correcting the Beaufort proportional-growth baseline and before opening Ross Island breeding-pair magnitudes. V1 is retained as the prerevision hypothesis.

## Revision

V1 predicted "intensification before expansion." The Beaufort decomposition does not support that ordering once growth is compared with a proportional baseline.

From 2004 to 2010:

- the new Beaufort subcolony increased from 460 to 957 pairs (+108%);
- the main colony increased from 47,725 to 63,760 (+33.6%);
- the new subcolony share increased from 0.95% to 1.48%;
- effective two-unit number increased from about 1.019 to 1.030.

The fact that 97% of the absolute island gain occurred in the main colony is not evidence for intensification because the main colony already contained about 99% of breeders. Relative to proportional growth, the new subcolony received about 3.15 times its expected share of the net gain.

## Revised biological question

Does breeding capacity change **the spatial scale at which population redistribution is expressed**?

The key contrast is not "intensification versus expansion." It is:

    redistribution within the current island
    versus
    redistribution across an island boundary

## Mechanistic idea

For a breeder on island i, local capacity changes the payoff landscape without changing geographic distance.

Higher residual breeding capacity can:

- relax local crowding;
- permit infilling;
- permit spread to additional breeding patches or subcolonies on the same island;
- reduce the incentive to cross to another island.

Thus local expansion and reduced inter-island emigration are not contradictory. They are the predicted combination if capacity redirects redistribution to a lower spatial level.

## Independent Beaufort evidence

LaRue et al. (2013) reported:

- a 71% increase in usable nesting habitat at Beaufort over 1958–2010;
- rapid population growth;
- a newly founded disjunct subcolony;
- Beaufort-to-Ross-Island emigration/visitation peaking near 3% around 2005 and then decreasing as local habitat became available.

The current claim is deliberately narrow:

> In the Beaufort case, changing local breeding capacity was associated with less inter-island export while breeding distribution within the island became slightly more spread out.

This is evidence for a capacity-linked redistribution-scale switch in one natural experiment, not proof that island boundaries universally impose such a mechanism.

## Predictions

### P1 — capacity release can reduce inter-island export

When usable breeding space increases on the current island, inter-island emigration should decline, all else equal.

Beaufort provides case-level support from independent individual resighting evidence.

### P2 — local retention does not require spatial intensification

If residual capacity exists, locally retained growth may appear as either:

- infilling of established breeding units, or
- disproportionate growth/founding of smaller breeding units on the same island.

Therefore an increase in within-island E is compatible with reduced between-island movement.

### P3 — island crossing is a higher-level redistribution event

When local breeding capacity is available, redistribution should preferentially be expressed within the island rather than across the island boundary.

The general test requires multi-island individual movement or colonisation data with known search effort. Ross Island aerial counts alone cannot establish this prediction.

### P4 — the relevant variable is effective breeding capacity, not geometric island area

Usable nesting habitat can change through glacier retreat, snow/ice cover, terrain and access without any change in island distance or total geometric area.

Capacity should therefore be treated as a dynamic state variable.

## Nested spatial hierarchy

    subcolony/component
        ↓
    colony
        ↓
    same island
        ↔
    another island

The hypothesis predicts redistribution among these levels, not a universal sequence in which every lower level must fill completely before the next one is used.

## What would be novel

The novel proposition is:

> islands may regulate the **scale** of demographic redistribution: environmental changes in usable breeding capacity can shift population response between within-island spreading and inter-island dispersal.

This is stronger and more testable than treating islands as static patches, while remaining narrower than claiming that island boundaries are universally mechanistic.

## Immediate evidence boundary

Current evidence can support:

- differential attrition during decline in Palmer/Signy;
- within-island proportional versus disproportionate growth in Beaufort;
- individual Beaufort-to-Ross-Island movement from the published band/resighting study;
- a pre-effect Ross Island test of decline contraction versus recovery deconcentration across established colonies.

Current evidence cannot establish:

- a universal threshold for island crossing;
- individual movement from aerial counts;
- a general competition coefficient;
- colonisation from first census observation.
