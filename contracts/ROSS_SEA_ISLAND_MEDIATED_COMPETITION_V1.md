# Island-mediated competition switch in Adélie penguins — hypothesis v1

**Status:** generated hypothesis for the Ross Sea follow-up. This is independent of the frozen Ecology submission.

## Biological question

Does island structure convert density-dependent competition within Adélie penguin breeding populations into discrete inter-island redistribution?

The focal process is not simply whether density dependence exists. It is whether the **spatial expression** of competition is organized by island boundaries.

## Mechanistic idea

For breeders currently on island i,

```
U_stay(i) = Q_i + S_i(n_i) - C_i(n_i / K_i) + Phi_i
```

and for moving to island j,

```
U_move(j) = Q_j + S_j(n_j) - C_j(n_j / K_j) - D_ij - F_ij
```

where:
- Q = physical breeding quality;
- S = benefits of conspecific presence;
- C = density-dependent competition;
- K = island/colony breeding capacity;
- Phi = philopatry/familiarity benefit;
- D = inter-island travel cost;
- F = settlement/founding uncertainty.

Because breeding habitat is discontinuous, an island boundary makes redistribution a discrete decision. Competition can therefore be absorbed within an island until local residual capacity falls enough that another island becomes the better option.

## Independent published evidence known before this analysis

The southern Ross Sea Adélie metapopulation comprises Ross Island colonies (Cape Royds, Cape Bird, Cape Crozier) and nearby Beaufort Island.

Published Beaufort work reports:
- expansion of available nesting habitat as ice retreated;
- rapid population growth at Beaufort;
- a decline in emigration from Beaufort to Ross Island after 2005 as local habitat availability increased.

This is direct evidence that breeding-space capacity on one island can change movement among islands.

The 1981–2012 Ross Sea synthesis treats Ross Island + Beaufort as a semi-isolated southern metapopulation and identifies a switch from negative growth in 1981–2000 to positive growth in 2001–2012.

## Frozen island-level predictions

### P1 — competition release retains breeders on the same island

When usable breeding habitat expands on an island, emigration to neighbouring islands should decline.

This is already supported qualitatively by the independent Beaufort movement study and is used as external mechanism evidence, not re-tested from census counts alone.

### P2 — recovery should first intensify existing island nodes

During regional population growth, most numerical gains should occur within already occupied colonies/islands before a new island node is established.

The Ross Sea count workbook may be used descriptively to quantify allocation of growth among **already occupied** southern Ross Sea nodes, but first observation of a census row is never interpreted as colonisation without documented prior search.

### P3 — island crossing is a higher-threshold transition than within-island growth

If island boundaries raise dispersal/settlement costs, increasing abundance should be accommodated within an island until local capacity/quality becomes limiting.

Evidence would consist of:
- sustained growth within established island colonies;
- independent evidence of local habitat limitation;
- increased inter-island movement or colonisation only when local residual capacity is low.

### P4 — island capacity, not island area alone, should govern redistribution

Changes in usable nesting habitat should alter emigration even when geographic distance between islands is unchanged.

Beaufort provides the key natural experiment: island distance to Ross Island did not change, but habitat availability did.

## Within-island versus between-island hierarchy

The southern Ross Sea offers a nested spatial hierarchy:

```
subcolonies/components
    ↓
Cape Royds / Cape Bird / Cape Crozier colonies
    ↓
Ross Island
    ↔
Beaufort Island
```

A mechanistic analysis should distinguish:
1. redistribution among components within a colony;
2. growth among colonies on the same island;
3. redistribution across island boundaries.

If the competition switch is island-mediated, the cost/threshold of level 3 should be larger than the cost of levels 1–2.

## Species interactions

The primary hypothesis is **intraspecific** density dependence because the Ross Sea southern Adélie metapopulation is dominated by conspecific breeding-space and foraging competition.

Interspecific competition is secondary:
- terrestrial nesting-space competition may alter effective local K where Pygoscelis species overlap;
- marine competition may be reduced by spatial/depth/time partitioning and is not assumed to be the principal driver.

## What would be novel

The novel proposition is not that islands are isolated or that Adélie penguins compete.

It is:

> **island boundaries may convert continuous changes in local density into thresholded redistribution: competition is first absorbed within occupied island breeding space, and only after local capacity is constrained does growth become inter-island expansion.**

This makes island structure part of the mechanism of penguin population regulation rather than merely a geographic label.

## Immediate Ross Sea analysis boundary

The current aerial-count workbook can quantify:
- established-colony intensification;
- island-level shares;
- allocation of regional numerical growth;
- synchrony within versus between islands where survey coverage permits.

It cannot by itself identify:
- individual dispersal;
- true colonisation from first observation;
- competition coefficients.

Direct inter-island movement inference must rely on independent banding/resighting evidence or another individual-movement dataset.
