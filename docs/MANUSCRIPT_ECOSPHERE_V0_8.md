# Regional decline, local concentration: within-island reorganization of Adélie penguin breeding groups

**Ecosphere-oriented manuscript v0.8 — breeding-component concentration promoted; v0.8 retained as provenance**

## Abstract

Population decline can involve more than loss of abundance: the spatial organization of breeders can also contract and reorganize. We analysed a complete 1991–2017 census of five neighbouring Adélie penguin (*Pygoscelis adeliae*) breeding islands near Palmer Station, West Antarctic Peninsula, asking whether regional decline was accompanied by progressive concentration among within-island breeding components. The five island populations shared a strongly coherent long-term decline (PC1 of standardized log abundance = **96.4%**) but retained different demographic endpoints. In the three islands with unchanged colony-code rosters, effective colony number declined from **3.54 to 2.86** on Cormorant (**−19%**), **4.62 to 2.28** on Humble (**−51%**), and **5.78 to 1.00** on Litchfield before local extinction (**−83%**). We compared these trends with a fixed-composition null that retained each observed island-total trajectory and added the same independent Poisson and Gamma–Poisson count-error sensitivities used elsewhere in the analysis. Even under the uncalibrated 20% multiplicative-CV model, the observed concentration slope remained unusual on Cormorant (*p* = **0.038**) and was not reached in 100,000 simulations on either Humble or Litchfield (plus-one *p* = **0.000010** each); no simulation produced slopes simultaneously as negative on all three islands (joint *p* = **0.000010**). Thus, decline was accompanied by redistribution toward a smaller effective set of breeding components rather than simple proportional thinning plus independent count noise. Effective colony number also retained a small positive conditional association with next-year growth (**+0.1126** after two lagged growth terms) under structured and count-error nulls, although its held-out predictive increment was weak (**+0.000444**) and unsupported. Independent Torgersen mapping documented parallel physical contraction from 23 historic active subcolonies to five active footprints and habitat-structured extinction, providing phenomenon-level rather than identifier-level spatial triangulation. An observed sub-island beta-variability hierarchy was retained only as secondary context because a severe uncalibrated count-error scenario could reproduce it. The strongest ecological inference is that Palmer Adélie decline has a within-island spatial-demographic architecture: breeders become progressively concentrated as island populations contract.

**Keywords:** Adélie penguin; breeding colonies; demographic decline; island ecology; long-term monitoring; population dynamics; spatial concentration; colony organization

## Introduction

Island ecology asks why populations and communities diverge among habitat patches embedded within a broader regional setting. Seabird islands are a particularly strong form of land–sea coupling: birds acquire resources in marine food webs, return to land to reproduce, and transport marine-derived materials that can restructure terrestrial soils and communities [@mulder2011; @grant2022]. Most seabird-island work has therefore emphasized what seabirds do to islands. Here we ask the reciprocal demographic question: **how does the internal state of a terrestrial breeding patch condition the fate of a marine-foraging population?**

In many terrestrial systems, the patch contains both breeding habitat and much of the trophic resource base, making local resource availability, competitors, predators and physical habitat difficult to separate. Antarctic penguin breeding islands differ in a useful way. Pygoscelis penguins forage at sea but reproduce on discrete terrestrial patches, so the food environment is largely external to the breeding island while nesting habitat remains spatially localized. We refer to these as **externally subsidized breeding islands**: marine conditions can impose shared regional forcing, while snow, topography, drainage, access and the spatial organization of nest sites can generate local filters. The phrase describes the demographic geometry of the breeding system; it does not imply that terrestrial biotic interactions are absent.

The Palmer Archipelago is well suited to resolving these scales. Adélie penguins near Palmer Station have experienced major long-term population declines, while gentoo penguins have expanded and some breeding sites have undergone species replacement [@pickett2018; @checastaldo2023]. At the same time, neighboring islands differ in breeding habitat and snow accumulation. Long-term work on Torgersen and Humble islands shows that regional environmental conditions interact with island geomorphology to produce local variation in breeding phenology [@cimino2019]. Earlier work proposed that breeding-habitat quality, shaped by geomorphology and snow deposition, contributes substantially to differences among local Adélie populations [@fraser2013]. More recent spatial reconstruction on Torgersen independently mapped strong subcolony loss and showed that persistence was non-random with respect to terrain and snow-related habitat features [@cimino2025].

These observations suggest that decline may have an internal spatial architecture. A population can shrink approximately proportionally across all breeding components, preserving their relative representation, or it can become progressively concentrated as some components lose breeders faster than others. The distinction matters biologically because Adélie reproductive performance varies at subcolony scale and with nesting configuration [@schmidt2021], while independent Torgersen mapping shows strong, habitat-structured loss of breeding footprints [@cimino2025]. We therefore ask not only how many breeders remain on an island, but whether the distribution of those breeders among census breeding components changes systematically during decline.

Our primary within-island hypothesis is a **proportional-thinning null**. If regional decline simply scales down a stable distribution of breeders, then a fixed colony-code composition subjected to the observed island-total trajectory should reproduce the apparent loss of effective colony diversity once sampling and counting error are included. Conversely, a decline in effective colony number more rapid than this fixed-composition null would indicate progressive redistribution toward a smaller effective set of breeding components. Because public colony codes are nominal census units rather than mapped polygons, such a result would establish demographic reorganization but not identify the physical patches or mechanisms responsible.

We retain two additional scale questions as bounded context. First, spatial population synchrony and beta variability describe how observed fluctuations combine across colony, island and archipelago levels [@wang2014], but independent lower-level sampling error can create apparent compensation [@santinjanin2014; @loreau2021]. Second, if within-island organization carries demographic information, effective colony number may be conditionally associated with subsequent population growth even after current abundance and recent growth are controlled. We treat this association separately from the concentration trend and do not interpret either result as forecasting or causal spatial insurance.

We therefore combined four evidence layers. We quantified the common regional decline and divergent island endpoints; tested progressive within-island concentration against a fixed-composition count-error null; retained the hierarchical beta partition as observation-scale context with an explicit error boundary; and used prospective environmental tests plus independent Torgersen mapping to bound mechanisms. The result is a distinction between **how much a population declines** and **how its breeding structure reorganizes while declining**.

## Materials and Methods

## Materials and Methods

### Study system and data roles

The primary demographic analysis used five Adélie breeding islands monitored by the Palmer Station Antarctica Long Term Ecological Research program: Christine (CHR), Cormorant (COR), Humble (HUM), Litchfield (LIT) and Torgersen (TOR). We used the public colony-level breeding-pair census, aggregated annually within island. The synchronized panel spans 1991–2017 without missing island-years and contains 27 census seasons. The source table and its checksum are frozen in the mina reproducibility receipts (Palmer LTER data DOI: 10.6073/pasta/89dd52217ca37e3a72a67f7a9bc3c82e).

We used the Antarctic Penguin Biogeography Project (APBP/MAPPPD) as a broader assembly context rather than as the synchronized primary time series [@checastaldo2023]. An outcome-blind local inventory within 20 km of Palmer Station identified six true-island breeding sites—Torgersen, Litchfield, Humble, Christine, Cormorant and Dream—and retained Biscoe Point on Anvers Island as a non-island benchmark. This distinction matters because Biscoe Point shows strong Adélie-to-gentoo replacement, whereas several true islands show decline or vacancy without equivalent gentoo replacement.


### Five-island scale separation

Colony-level breeding-pair counts were summed to island-year totals. For each island, we transformed abundance as \(\log(1+N)\). To quantify the common long-term component, we standardized each island's 1991–2017 log-abundance trajectory and performed principal component analysis across islands. We also calculated annual log growth,
\[
g_{i,t}=\log(1+N_{i,t})-\log(1+N_{i,t-1}),
\]
and Pearson correlations for all ten island pairs.

We decomposed total log-abundance variation using additive ordinary least squares models containing island and year factors. We report the fractions uniquely attributable to year and island and the residual fraction. These are descriptive scale partitions; the year term is not interpreted as a causal climate effect.

### Prospective regional and terrestrial mechanism tests

Mechanism tests were frozen before the corresponding predictor values were inspected or joined to the response.

For the annual marine test, the response was island annual growth and the primary regional predictor was Palmer DSR Grid sea-ice season duration for the completed sea-ice season preceding the breeding census. The baseline model contained island fixed effects; the regional model added standardized sea-ice duration. Validation withheld all five island observations from one calendar year at a time. Support required lower held-out mean squared error and a positive full-data coefficient.

After this annual formulation was not supported, a separate fixed-window test asked whether low-frequency sea-ice duration at a predeclared five-year scale improved prediction beyond a temporal baseline. Three- and seven-year windows were fixed robustness checks rather than tuned alternatives. Overlapping windows were purged from training during validation.

For the terrestrial weather test, annual island growth was converted to a local deviation by subtracting the mean growth of the other eligible islands in the same interval. The predictor was the number of October days with numeric positive snowfall at Palmer Station, and the island modifier was the published fraction of snow-prone/suboptimal breeding habitat from Fraser et al. [@fraser2013]. Post-extinction Litchfield zero-to-zero intervals were excluded. The predeclared prediction was a negative snowfall-by-habitat interaction and improved leave-one-year-out prediction.

### Within-island colony organization

The LTER census contains nominal island-specific colony codes. For each island-year, let \(p_j\) be the share of breeding pairs in colony code \(j\). We defined effective colony number as
\[
N_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{1}{\sum_j p_j^2}.
\]
This quantity is high when breeders are distributed more evenly among multiple colony codes and low when breeding pairs are concentrated in fewer groups.

The primary next-year model used annual transitions for which current island abundance was positive. The baseline contained island fixed effects, standardized current \(\log(1+N)\), and standardized census year. The full model added standardized \(\log(1+N_{\mathrm{eff}})\). Validation withheld all island transitions ending in one calendar year together. Support required a positive coefficient and lower held-out-year mean squared error.

Two specificity checks were fixed in advance. First, we replaced effective colony number with the number of active positive-count colony codes. Second, we evaluated an externally defined historical threshold of >50 breeding pairs per group rather than searching thresholds on the present outcome. The >50-pair analysis was treated as a directional validation: predictive improvement alone was insufficient if the coefficient had the opposite sign to the historical positive-retention hypothesis.

### Progressive concentration of breeding components

We then asked whether breeder distributions became progressively concentrated during decline. This post-hoc ecological extension was frozen after the hierarchy count-error audit highlighted the need to distinguish biological reorganization from abundance-dependent counting effects. To avoid changes in census-unit identity, the analysis was restricted to Cormorant, Humble and Litchfield, the three islands with unchanged reported colony-code rosters throughout the synchronized series. Litchfield contributed only years with positive island abundance.

For each eligible island, the primary observed statistic was the ordinary least-squares slope of annual (N_{\mathrm{eff}}) against centered calendar year. A negative slope indicates increasing concentration among colony-code components. We tested this trend against a proportional-thinning null derived from the same fixed-composition construction used in the hierarchical count-error audit. Within each island, a single time-invariant vector of colony-code shares was estimated from cumulative 1991–2017 breeding-pair counts. In each year, those shares were multiplied by the observed island total, so the latent composition did not change through time even though total abundance followed the empirical decline.

We then added the already-frozen independent count-error family: Poisson sampling and Gamma–Poisson sensitivities with 10% and 20% multiplicative CV. Positive observed island-years were conditioned on at least one simulated breeding pair, matching the previous error diagnostic. For each model we generated 100,000 realizations. The island-specific one-sided statistic was the probability that the simulated (N_{\mathrm{eff}}) slope was at least as negative as observed. We also calculated the probability that all three simulated island slopes were simultaneously at least as negative as their observed slopes. Support required each island-specific probability and the three-island joint probability to remain at or below 0.05 under all three frozen error models. We did not open alternative concentration indices, time windows, intermediate CV values or colony-roster reconciliations.

This test isolates demographic redistribution beyond the combination of declining island abundance, a fixed latent colony composition and independent counting error. It does not map colony codes to physical nesting polygons or identify snow, topography, predation, movement or habitat quality as the causal driver.

### Hierarchical variability partition

We next asked where temporal compensation occurred in the nested breeding system. Following Wang and Loreau [@wang2014], for additive raw breeding-pair abundance we defined local variability as
\[
\alpha_{CV}^{2}=\left(\frac{\sum_j \sigma_j}{\sum_j \mu_j}\right)^2,
\]
and aggregate variability as
\[
\gamma_{CV}^{2}=\frac{\mathrm{Var}_t\left(\sum_j X_{jt}\right)}{\left(\sum_j \mu_j\right)^2}.
\]
Spatial beta variability was \(\beta=\alpha_{CV}^{2}/\gamma_{CV}^{2}=1/\phi\), where \(\phi\) is spatial synchrony. Values of \(\beta>1\) therefore indicate that aggregation reduces temporal variability relative to perfectly synchronous components; we use this descriptively and do not equate it with a demonstrated causal spatial-insurance mechanism.

The within-island component analysis required an unchanged reported colony-code roster through the full 1991–2017 census, because silently adding, deleting or reconciling census units would manufacture temporal covariance. Cormorant, Humble and Litchfield met this gate; Christine and Torgersen did not and were retained only for island-level context. For the stable-roster subset we calculated \(\alpha_{sub}\) across colony codes, \(\alpha_{island}\) across island totals and \(\gamma_{arch}\) for their summed abundance. This yields an exact nested factorization,
\[
\beta_{total}
=\frac{\alpha_{sub}}{\gamma_{arch}}
=\underbrace{\frac{\alpha_{sub}}{\alpha_{island}}}_{\beta_{within}}
\underbrace{\frac{\alpha_{island}}{\gamma_{arch}}}_{\beta_{among}}.
\]
We report the relative contribution of each transition on the additive \(\log\beta\) scale; this is a decomposition of the multiplicative variability ratio, not a causal variance fraction.

Two raw-abundance sensitivities were fixed: restricting Cormorant, Humble and Litchfield to 1991–2006, before Litchfield extinction, and retaining only the two persistent stable-roster islands, Cormorant and Humble, across 1991–2017. To separate the dominant directional decline from shorter-term covariance, we additionally calculated covariance-based \(\phi\) and \(\beta=1/\phi\) for linearly detrended \(\log(1+N)\) and for annual \(\log(1+N)\) growth. Because log transformation destroys additive abundance identities, these centered-signal analyses are sensitivities only and are not presented as exact three-level alpha/gamma partitions.

Because the within-island level contains more component series than the among-island level, we also performed a fixed post-hoc component-count robustness diagnostic. We calculated normalized excess beta, \((\beta-1)/(n-1)\), where \(n\) is the number of component series, and independently summarized mean pairwise temporal correlation at each level for raw abundance, linearly detrended \(\log(1+N)\), and annual log growth. This diagnostic asks whether the observed hierarchy can be explained solely by comparing seven to nine colony-code component series within an island with only three island series among islands. It is a robustness audit, not a replacement for the Wang–Loreau partition or a new confirmatory endpoint.

### Observation-error sensitivity of the variability hierarchy

Independent count errors among colony-code units can create apparent within-island asynchrony even when their latent trajectories are perfectly synchronous. We therefore froze a separate observation-error null after identifying this concern and before generating the final result receipt. For each stable-roster island, we estimated one time-invariant colony-code composition (q_j) from cumulative 1991–2017 breeding-pair counts. The latent mean for colony code (j) in year (t) was then \(\lambda_{jt}=q_jN_t\), where \(N_t\) was the observed island total. Before observation error, every colony-code trajectory within an island was therefore exactly proportional to the same island trajectory and latent within-island beta variability equalled one.

We reused, without adding intermediate error levels, the same error family already frozen for the effective-colony mechanical-coupling diagnostic: independent Poisson counts and Gamma–Poisson sensitivities with 10% and 20% multiplicative CV. For an island-year known to have positive abundance, the simulated colony vector was redrawn only if all colony counts were zero; frozen zero island totals remained zero. Simulated island totals were the sums of the simulated colony counts and were used in the among-island calculation, so error aggregation was represented at both hierarchical steps. We generated 100,000 realizations per error model. The two primary statistics were raw \(\beta_{within}\) and \(\log(\beta_{within}/\beta_{among})\). The detrended-log and annual-growth within/among beta ratios were fixed secondary diagnostics and were not allowed to rescue the raw result if compatible with the error-only null.

The public Palmer census metadata state that counts followed CCAMLR Ecosystem Monitoring Program methods with documented program-specific details [@palmer_census; @ccamlr2014]. A contemporaneous Palmer/Torgersen protocol description reports a minimum of three independent observer counts per colony and targeted between-count agreement within ±5%, or ±10% for colonies exceeding 1,000 pairs [@patterson2001]. Those historical agreement targets are not equivalent to the multiplicative CV parameter in our Gamma–Poisson model and the public analysis table does not retain the replicate observer counts needed for direct calibration. We therefore treat 10% and 20% CV strictly as inherited sensitivity levels, not estimates of Palmer observer error.

### Post-positive N_eff uncertainty and count-error diagnostics

Because the original effective-colony analysis produced only a small held-out error reduction, we froze an uncertainty diagnostic before using the result as a manuscript pillar. We kept next-year growth, current abundance, island identity and year fixed and permuted only effective colony number as synchronized start-year blocks. Donor values came from the same island in another year, with a common donor-year mapping across islands; years were stratified mechanically by island availability so the five-island pre-extinction phase was not mixed with the four-island post-Litchfield phase. We repeated the exact leave-one-end-year-out C0/C1 pipeline for 20,000 permutations. The primary statistic was \(\Delta\mathrm{MSE}=\mathrm{MSE}(C0)-\mathrm{MSE}(C1)\).

We next corrected the coefficient null for serial structure. For each island, we circularly shifted the complete ordered sequence of \(\log(1+N_{\mathrm{eff}})\) across its eligible transition years while keeping response, abundance, island identity and calendar year fixed. This preserves the island-specific marginal distribution, cyclic ordering, circular autocovariance and Fourier power spectrum while breaking calendar alignment with future growth. The primary structured null used 100,000 independent island-wise shifts. A fixed sensitivity applied one common lag to Christine, Cormorant, Humble and Torgersen while shifting Litchfield independently, allowing all 26 × 16 = 416 lag combinations to be enumerated exactly; this preserves cross-island N_eff covariance among the four persistent islands.

We then repeated the same-census mechanical-coupling diagnostic using circularly shifted, rather than year-block-permuted, latent colony composition. Target-year total abundance remained fixed, but colony-share vectors came from the same island at a circularly shifted donor year. Colony counts were simulated under independent Poisson sampling and fixed Gamma–Poisson sensitivities with 10% and 20% multiplicative overdispersion. Coupled simulations derived effective colony number and current abundance from the same simulated count vector; decoupled simulations used an independent current-total draw. These are stylized sensitivity models rather than empirical estimates of Palmer observer error.

Finally, we tested whether effective colony number merely tracked short-term demographic momentum. We added the preceding annual growth rate and then the preceding two annual growth rates to the same baseline, using complete cases (115 and 110 transitions, respectively). For the coefficient sensitivity we did not create a new null family: the complete 26-year effective-colony sequences were shifted using the same 100,000 independent island-wise circular shifts and the same exact 26 × 16 joint-shift group defined above, and lag-ineligible early transitions were removed only after the full-series shift was constructed. We report the lag-conditioned coefficient and leave-one-end-year-out MSE gain as effect-size diagnostics; the earlier synchronized year-block test remains the governing test of out-of-year predictive support.

### External spatial triangulation

The census colony codes are nominal and are not accompanied by a public one-to-one mapping to independently reconstructed GIS subcolony polygons. We therefore did not claim colony-ID-level spatial validation. Instead, we used the 2025 Torgersen reconstruction as process-level triangulation [@cimino2025]. That study used historic maps, drone imagery, GPS, digital surface models and satellite snow mapping to reconstruct historic and current subcolony footprints and their physical habitat.

Our spatial criterion was deliberately qualitative and bounded: mapped subcolony contraction and non-random habitat-associated attrition could converge with the internal colony-organization interpretation, but unresolved identifier correspondence prevents a causal test linking the exact LTER effective-colony metric to mapped topography.

### Reproducibility and frozen result family

All data extraction, endpoint contracts, result receipts, figure-data exports and figure rendering are versioned in the `mina` repository. Figure-data generation fails if the primary census no longer reproduces the frozen PC1 variance fraction, median annual-growth correlation or conditional effective-colony coefficient. The manuscript figure package uses the same frozen source fingerprint as the primary analysis.

## Results

### A common long-term decline provides the regional background to divergent local endpoints

All five island populations declined strongly between 1991 and 2017. Christine fell from 1,410 to 27 breeding pairs (1.9% remaining), Cormorant from 681 to 23 (3.4%), Humble from 1,085 to 68 (6.3%), Litchfield from 497 to zero, and Torgersen from 3,016 to 22 (0.7%). Litchfield reached zero breeding pairs in 2007 and remained locally extinct through the end of the synchronized series.

Despite these different endpoints, the standardized long-term trajectories were highly coherent. PC1 explained **96.4%** of five-island log-abundance variation and had similar same-sign loadings across islands. Annual dynamics were less synchronous: the median pairwise annual-growth correlation was **0.373**, with pairwise correlations ranging from negative to >0.7. Because synchrony is known to be timescale-dependent, we use this contrast to define the regional and local scales of the analysis rather than as a stand-alone discovery.

The additive decomposition similarly separated scales. Year uniquely accounted for **53.5%** of total log-abundance variance, island identity for **33.0%**, and residual variation for **13.5%**. Thus, the dominant long-term decline was regional in direction, but substantial persistent island differences and year-specific local deviations remained.

### Breeders became progressively concentrated during island decline

All three stable-roster islands showed declining effective colony number. Cormorant decreased from **3.54** in 1991 to **2.86** in 2017, a **19.1%** reduction, with an (N_{\mathrm{eff}}) slope of **−0.0310 yr−1**. Humble decreased from **4.62** to **2.28** (**50.6%**), with a slope of **−0.0855 yr−1**. Litchfield decreased from **5.78** to **1.00** by its final positive census in 2006 (**82.7%**), with a slope of **−0.3681 yr−1**.

These trends were steeper than expected from declining island totals plus a time-invariant colony composition and independent count error. Under Poisson error, the one-sided probabilities of slopes at least as negative as observed were **0.0121** for Cormorant and **0.000010** for both Humble and Litchfield. Under Gamma–Poisson CV10%, the corresponding probabilities were **0.0194**, **0.000010** and **0.000010**. The deliberately severe CV20% sensitivity still retained the signal: **0.0380** for Cormorant and **0.000010** for Humble and Litchfield. In all three error models, zero of 100,000 realizations produced slopes simultaneously as negative as observed on all three islands (plus-one joint *p* = **0.000010**).

The fixed-composition null itself predicted mild abundance-dependent declines in effective colony number, especially on Litchfield as total abundance approached zero, but not of the observed magnitude. The result therefore rejects simple proportional thinning as a sufficient description of decline in the stable-roster islands: breeder distributions progressively shifted toward a smaller effective set of colony-code breeding components.

### The observed variability hierarchy remained measurement-error-sensitive context

At the island-to-archipelago level, raw long-term dynamics were close to complete synchrony. Across all five islands, beta variability was **1.0137** (\(\phi=0.9864\)); excluding extinct Litchfield yielded \(\beta=1.0111\) (\(\phi=0.9890\)). Aggregating neighbouring islands therefore removed little of the long-term temporal variability imposed by their shared decline.

Within-island component decomposition was restricted by the frozen roster gate. Cormorant, Humble and Litchfield retained unchanged reported colony-code rosters across all 27 years, whereas Christine and Torgersen changed roster composition and were not reconciled or zero-filled. Across the 25 stable colony codes in the three eligible islands, \(\alpha_{sub}=0.8612\), \(\alpha_{island}=0.8021\), and \(\gamma_{arch}=0.7932\). The resulting observed within-island beta variability was **1.0737**, compared with **1.0112** among islands; their product exactly recovered the total component-to-archipelago \(\beta=1.0858\). On the additive log-beta scale, **86.4%** of this observed multiplicative transition occurred from colony-code components to islands and 13.6% from islands to the aggregate.

The raw ordering was not generated by Litchfield's terminal zeros. Restricting the three islands to 1991–2006 gave \(\beta_{within}=1.1030\) and \(\beta_{among}=1.0178\); removing Litchfield entirely and retaining Cormorant and Humble across 1991–2017 gave **1.0818** and **1.0052**, respectively. On the observed counts, removing the long-term trend also increased the apparent separation: for linearly detrended (log(1+N)), among-island beta was **1.1688**, whereas within-island beta ranged from **1.768** to **7.134**; for annual log growth, among-island beta was **1.5063** and within-island beta ranged from **2.679** to **5.141**. Because those transformed signals are non-additive and can amplify independent lower-level error after the common trend is removed, they are reported only as sensitivities rather than stronger evidence.

The post-hoc component-count audit gave the same observed ordering. Normalized excess beta was **0.00562** among the three islands, compared with **0.01067** for Cormorant, **0.01176** for Humble and **0.00612** for Litchfield. Mean pairwise correlation was also lower within every eligible island than among islands for raw abundance, detrended log abundance and annual log growth. This rules out component number alone as the explanation, but it does not address independent observation error and therefore is not treated as a measurement-error robustness result.

The fully synchronous-subcolony error null changed the strength of the hierarchy inference. Under independent Poisson error, none of 100,000 simulations reached either the observed raw \(\beta_{within}=1.0737\) or the observed hierarchy contrast \(\log(\beta_{within}/\beta_{among})=0.05995\) (plus-one *p* = **0.000010** for each); the 97.5th percentiles were **1.0320** and **0.01868**, respectively. The inherited Gamma–Poisson 10% sensitivity gave the same result (0/100,000 exceedances; 97.5th percentiles **1.0516** and **0.03485**). In contrast, under the uncalibrated 20% sensitivity the null mean within-island beta was **1.0882**, exceeding the observed 1.0737; the observed within-island beta had *p* = **0.924**, and the hierarchy contrast had *p* = **0.448**. The apparent strengthening after detrending or conversion to annual growth did not rescue the result: the detrended median within/among ratio had one-sided probabilities **0.054**, **0.082** and **0.191** under Poisson, 10% and 20% sensitivities, while the annual-growth ratio had probabilities **0.447**, **0.478** and **0.530**. The event that all three eligible islands individually exceeded the among-island beta was itself common under the error-only null. Thus the observed raw hierarchy is robust to Poisson and 10% sensitivities but not to the full fixed error family.

### Simple sea-ice-duration and snowfall formulations did not explain the scale mismatch

Adding preceding sea-ice duration to the annual island baseline yielded only a small held-out mean squared error improvement (0.07704 to 0.07591), while the full-data coefficient was **−0.050**, opposite the predeclared positive direction. A year-cluster bootstrap interval included zero. Adding the published static habitat interaction worsened held-out prediction.

The fixed low-frequency rescue also failed. At the primary five-year scale, adding trailing sea-ice duration worsened purged out-of-window mean squared error from 0.00373 to 0.00501 and produced a negative sea-ice coefficient (−0.016). Fixed three- and seven-year checks showed the same qualitative outcome. We therefore closed the sea-ice-duration window family rather than searching additional smoothing scales.

The terrestrial annual interaction was also unsupported. October snowfall days plus their interaction with published snow-prone habitat modestly increased in-sample \(R^2\) from 0.252 to 0.287, but leave-one-year-out RMSE worsened from 0.308 to 0.325. The interaction coefficient was **+0.073**, opposite the predeclared negative direction. These results reject the tested formulations, not the ecological importance of sea ice or breeding habitat.

### Less concentrated breeder distributions were conditionally associated with better next-year growth

Effective colony number had a positive standardized conditional coefficient (**+0.1168**) after island identity, current abundance and secular time were included. Adding previous-year demographic growth left the coefficient essentially unchanged (**+0.1185**) with a held-out MSE gain of **+0.000929**. Conditioning simultaneously on growth over the preceding two annual intervals yielded **+0.1126** across 110 transitions, retaining **96.4%** of the original coefficient, while the incremental held-out MSE gain fell to **+0.000444** (**43.2%** of the original gain). Under the same full-series structured null family, only 101 of 100,000 independent circular shifts reached the lag1+lag2 coefficient (plus-one one-sided *p* = **0.00102**), and five of 416 exact covariance-preserving shifts were at least as large (*p* = **0.0120**). The association is therefore not well explained as a proxy for one- or two-year decline momentum, even though its incremental predictive contribution is very small. The original leave-one-end-year-out MSE decreased from 0.07311 to 0.07208, a gain of **+0.0010288**.

That gain did not survive the frozen uncertainty diagnostic. Under 20,000 synchronized year-block permutations, the null mean gain was −0.000202 (SD 0.00236), the 95th percentile was +0.00442, and **5,244/20,000** permutations produced a gain at least as large as observed. The one-sided permutation probability was **0.262**, placing the observed gain at the 73.8th percentile of the null distribution. We therefore withdraw the claim that effective colony number provides robust held-out-year predictive information.

The coefficient itself behaved differently. Under 100,000 independent island-wise circular shifts, no surrogate coefficient reached the observed +0.1168 (Monte Carlo one-sided *p* < 0.00001; null 97.5th percentile = 0.0628). In the exact joint-shift sensitivity, which preserved cross-island N_eff covariance among Christine, Cormorant, Humble and Torgersen, the identity alignment was the only one of 416 combinations reaching the observed coefficient (exact *p* = 1/416 = **0.0024**).

The count-error result also persisted after replacing the latent year-block null with circularly shifted colony composition. An observed-scale coupled coefficient was rare under Poisson (*p* = **0.00030**), Gamma–Poisson CV10% (*p* = **0.00150**) and Gamma–Poisson CV20% (*p* = **0.00780**) simulations. Median paired coupling bias was −1.7%, +0.5% and +6.6% of the observed coefficient, respectively. Thus, neither serial structure nor simple shared census-count error under the fixed sensitivity models reproduced the positive coefficient. The circular-shift surrogate has a wrap-around boundary and the count-error models are not empirically calibrated observer-error distributions, so this remains evidence for a bounded conditional association rather than causal identification.

Specificity checks did not restore a predictive interpretation. Active-colony count worsened held-out prediction. The externally fixed >50-pair group count and breeder fraction improved prediction but had negative coefficients, opposite their historical positive-direction hypothesis. We therefore retain effective colony number only as a conditional same-census association whose biological interpretation requires independent spatial validation.

### Independent Torgersen mapping showed real spatial contraction

The independent spatial record on Torgersen provides a physical counterpart to the internal colony-state signal [@cimino2025]. Of 23 historic active subcolony footprints, only five were active in 2022, an active-footprint fraction of 21.7%. All ten historic south-aspect subcolonies were extinct, compared with eight of 13 north-aspect subcolonies. Larger historic subcolonies tended to disappear later (reported \(R=0.75\), \(p=0.0009\)).

This evidence provides independent phenomenon-level support that real breeding-patch attrition on Torgersen is spatially structured rather than a purely nominal property of census coding. It does **not** validate the exact census-derived effective-colony metric against polygon topography, because a public machine-readable crosswalk between LTER colony codes and the independently mapped polygons has not been resolved.

### Broader local assembly endpoints were heterogeneous

The APBP context showed that local decline does not imply a single replacement pathway. Litchfield reached observed local Adélie extinction without focal-species replacement in the strict panel. In contrast, at the non-island Biscoe Point benchmark, Adélie declined from 3,020 breeding pairs in 1971 to 408 in 2025 while gentoo increased from an explicit zero in 1984 to 5,405 in 2025. Dream Island was consistent with a different Adélie-decline/chinstrap-increase pathway, although synchronized composition coverage was insufficient for a confirmatory community trajectory. These endpoints motivate treating regional decline and local assembly outcome as distinct ecological dimensions.

## Discussion

### Decline reorganized within-island breeding structure rather than simply thinning populations

The clearest new ecological result is that breeder distributions became progressively concentrated during decline. On Cormorant, Humble and Litchfield, effective colony number fell through time more rapidly than expected from the observed loss of total breeders acting on a fixed colony composition, even after independent count error was added. The effect was modest but detectable on Cormorant, stronger on Humble, and extreme on Litchfield before local extinction. The joint result across all three islands was not reproduced in 100,000 simulations under any frozen error model. Thus, regional decline did not simply reduce every within-island breeding component in parallel; it was accompanied by reorganization toward a smaller effective set of components.

This pattern gives the Palmer decline a spatial-demographic architecture. It is consistent at the phenomenon level with independent Torgersen reconstruction, where 23 historic active subcolonies were reduced to five active footprints by 2022 and extinction was structured by landscape and snow-related habitat [@cimino2025]. It is also ecologically plausible that the configuration of remaining breeding groups matters: subcolony-scale nesting habitat and colony geometry are associated with Adélie reproductive performance, and smaller or more edge-dominated groups can experience different ecological conditions [@schmidt2021]. These external studies do not identify the mechanism behind the LTER colony-code trend, because the census codes have not been crosswalked one-to-one to mapped polygons. They do, however, show that within-colony spatial structure is biologically meaningful rather than merely bookkeeping.

The concentration result also clarifies what the (N_{\mathrm{eff}}) association can and cannot mean. Effective colony number is not only a cross-sectional topology statistic: in the three stable-roster islands it declined directionally beyond a fixed-composition error null. Higher (N_{\mathrm{eff}}) was also conditionally associated with better next-year growth after current abundance, island, time and recent growth were controlled. Yet the held-out predictive gain remained weak and null-compatible. We therefore interpret (N_{\mathrm{eff}}) as a **demographic state descriptor of within-island organization**, not as an early-warning indicator or a demonstrated causal buffer.

### Observed compensation is a secondary, measurement-sensitive scale pattern

The Wang–Loreau partition still contributes useful context. The observed counts contain little additional variability reduction from islands to the three-island aggregate but a larger reduction from colony-code components to islands. However, unlike the concentration trend, this beta hierarchy is compatible with the uncalibrated CV20% fully synchronous count-error null. Independent lower-level observation error can depress apparent synchrony and inflate aggregate stabilization [@santinjanin2014; @loreau2021]. The hierarchy therefore remains an observation-scale result rather than evidence that biological spatial insurance operates below the island level.

This difference in robustness is informative. The concentration test conditions on each observed island-total trajectory and asks whether the **composition itself changes directionally** more than counting noise predicts; it survives the full frozen error family. The beta hierarchy asks whether lower-level trajectories fluctuate asynchronously; that quantity is much more directly inflated by independent error. Separating those two questions prevents the weaker buffering inference from obscuring the stronger biological result that breeding structure is reorganizing during decline.

### An externally subsidized island system exposes breeding-patch filtering

### An externally subsidized island system exposes breeding-patch filtering

The Palmer system provides a demographic complement to the more familiar ecosystem-engineering view of seabird islands. Seabirds are well known to move marine-derived nutrients onto land and alter recipient island ecosystems [@mulder2011; @grant2022]. Our analysis focuses on the reciprocal constraint: the breeding population itself remains tied to discrete terrestrial breeding groups even when its principal food resources are external, and those within-island groups can be progressively pruned or concentrated during decline. These penguins acquire food in the ocean but concentrate reproduction on discrete terrestrial patches. This externalization of trophic resources reduces—but does not eliminate—the overlap between “island food supply” and “island breeding habitat.” Marine niche partitioning can also permit closely related species to coexist despite dietary overlap [@pickett2018]. As a result, terrestrial breeding-patch structure can emerge as a distinct axis of local vulnerability.

This perspective also clarifies why regional and local processes need not compete as explanations. Regional marine change may set the broad demographic direction, while terrestrial patch quality, colony history and the geometry of remaining nest habitat determine how that direction is realized locally. Biscoe Point further shows that local outcomes can include species replacement rather than simple vacancy. The appropriate island-ecology object is therefore not only species presence or total abundance, but the coupled state of regional marine forcing, breeding-patch occupancy and within-patch colony organization.

### Limitations and next tests

The primary synchronized analysis contains five nearby islands in one regional marine system. The progressive-concentration result is narrower still because unchanged colony-code rosters were required: it applies directly to Cormorant, Humble and Litchfield, and Litchfield contributes only through its final positive census in 2006. That is a strength for resolving scale under shared forcing but a limitation for generalization to other Antarctic archipelagos. The exact within-island component hierarchy is narrower still: only Cormorant, Humble and Litchfield have unchanged reported colony-code rosters across all 27 years, so Christine and Torgersen cannot enter the primary colony-code decomposition without an external identifier crosswalk. Colony codes are census units rather than mapped connectivity. More importantly, the public analysis table lacks the independent replicate counts needed to calibrate subcolony observation error. Historical Palmer counting targets make the inherited 20% multiplicative-CV sensitivity deliberately severe, but they cannot substitute for record-level uncertainty estimates. The hierarchy should therefore not be described as measurement-error robust.

The effective-colony coefficient survives the predeclared circular-shift and count-error nulls and remains similar after one- and two-year demographic-momentum controls, but lagged growth cannot remove all time-varying confounding and the held-out predictive gain is not stronger than synchronized year-block permutation noise. We also intentionally stopped after a finite set of failed environmental formulations, so untested pathways—including prey availability, sea-ice phenology other than duration, snow persistence at each island, and age-structured recruitment—remain plausible.

The highest-value next steps require external information rather than another tuning pass over the same census. First, archived replicate observer counts or another empirical calibration of colony-level counting error would determine whether the relevant uncertainty is closer to the low-error scenarios that the raw hierarchy exceeds or to the 20% sensitivity that reproduces it. Second, an external spatial crosswalk matching LTER colony codes to independently mapped historic and current polygons would allow direct tests of whether effective colony number tracks loss of snow-free habitat area, aspect, elevation or physical separation. Replicating the colony-organization result in another monitored archipelago would provide a stronger test of generality. Multi-species time series could then ask whether externally subsidized island systems tend toward vacancy, persistence or replacement depending on the identity and habitat requirements of potential colonists.

## Conclusion

Five neighbouring Adélie breeding islands underwent a strongly coherent multi-decadal decline but did not simply thin in proportion across their internal breeding structure. In each of the three islands with stable colony-code rosters, breeders became progressively concentrated among a smaller effective set of breeding components; this trend was stronger than expected from the observed island-total decline combined with a fixed latent composition and independent Poisson or Gamma–Poisson count error, including the uncalibrated CV20% sensitivity. The strongest ecological inference is therefore that Palmer Adélie decline includes within-island demographic reorganization, not only loss of total abundance.

A higher effective colony number was also weakly associated with better next-year growth after abundance, island, time and recent growth were controlled, but it did not provide robust held-out prediction and is not interpreted causally. Independent Torgersen mapping provides spatial convergence by showing strong physical subcolony contraction and habitat-structured extinction, while the unresolved census-code/GIS crosswalk prevents patch-level attribution. The observed beta-variability hierarchy is retained as secondary scale context because high independent count error can reproduce it. Together, these results separate three features of decline: a shared regional direction, island-specific demographic fate, and progressive within-island concentration of the remaining breeders.
