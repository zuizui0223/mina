# Ross differential recovery — biological mechanism map v1

**Date:** 2026-10-07  
**Status:** post-freeze mechanism synthesis for future work.  
**Important:** this document does not modify the frozen Ecology submission manuscript or reopen any endpoint.

## Phenomenon to explain

At the biologically natural three-colony grain, focal 1999→2001→2002 local restoration was:

- Cape Crozier: **105.2%**
- Cape Bird: **68.3%**
- Cape Royds: **38.7%**

At six-component grain, local restoration ranged from **10.8% to 109.8%**.

The mechanism question is:

> **Why did local breeding abundance recover at such different rates after the same regional iceberg disturbance?**

The answer is unlikely to be one scalar “colony quality” variable.

---

## Two mechanistic time scales

### I. Immediate expression: 2001→2002

The one-observation rebound is too fast to attribute primarily to chicks produced during the disturbance. The dominant mechanisms must act on birds already alive or already in pre-breeder cohorts.

Candidate processes:

1. colony access / distance to open water;
2. spring arrival and body condition;
3. breeding propensity / temporary nonbreeding;
4. adult survival;
5. recruitment of already-existing prebreeders;
6. adult and prebreeder movement;
7. census timing / early abandonment.

### II. Multi-year amplification: 2002 onward

Longer trajectories can additionally reflect:

1. reproductive success;
2. cohort recruitment after the 3–7 year maturation lag;
3. persistent habitat fragmentation;
4. skua edge predation;
5. nest-site fidelity and social-information feedback;
6. density-dependent food competition;
7. changing age/sex composition;
8. metapopulation movement and Beaufort capacity;
9. longer-term survival.

## The strongest integrated hypothesis: a limiting-factor switch

The most interesting interpretation is not “large colonies recover better.”

Large colonies have both **costs** and **benefits**.

### Negative density dependence

At large colonies, especially Crozier:

- local prey depletion and competition increase;
- foraging trips can become longer;
- chicks can be smaller;
- central-place foraging costs increase.

This should penalize very large colonies under ordinary conditions.

### Positive density dependence / Allee-like benefits

At small or fragmented colonies:

- perimeter-to-area ratio rises;
- a larger fraction of nests is exposed to edges;
- per-capita skua pressure can increase;
- social protection and compact nesting decline;
- site fidelity can preserve a fragmented, suboptimal configuration.

These effects penalize small colonies, especially after a fragmentation event.

### Extreme-environment gate

The iceberg adds a third axis:

- access to open water / polynya / MIZ;
- migration and arrival cost;
- breeding participation;
- ability to forage under abnormal sea-ice geometry.

The key hypothesis is therefore:

> **Extreme disturbance can switch the dominant limiting factor from competition at high density to access and positive-density feedback at low density.**

Under normal conditions:
- Crozier pays strong food-competition costs;
- Royds benefits from low local competition.

Under the iceberg perturbation:
- western access constraints and fragmentation strongly penalize Royds;
- Crozier's geographic reliability and large-group nesting benefits can outweigh its normal competition costs.

This is a **disturbance × density interaction**, not a simple colony-size effect.

## A plausible causal sequence

### Fast phase

    iceberg geometry
        ↓
    colony-specific access / arrival cost
        ↓
    breeding propensity + early abandonment
        ↓
    observed breeding-pair rebound

with additional contributions from:
- adult survival;
- existing prebreeder recruitment;
- movement/emigration.

### Slow phase

    initial shock
        ↓
    colony shrinkage / fragmentation
        ↓
    perimeter:area ↑ + skua edge exposure ↑
        ↓
    reproductive success ↓
        ↓
    delayed cohort recruitment ↓
        ↓
    persistent slow recovery

while:

    high site fidelity + incomplete social information
        ↓
    fragmented configuration persists

At Crozier, the opposite feedback can occur:

    large compact aggregation
        ↓
    lower relative edge exposure / higher nesting success
        ↓
    stronger demographic pipeline

but is opposed by:

    large colony
        ↓
    stronger food competition / prey depletion.

The observed trajectory is the balance of these opposing density-dependent processes.

## What the existing evidence already rules out

### Not just “Crozier was largest”

Simple size advantage is contradicted by known negative density dependence in Crozier foraging and chick mass.

### Not just adult survival

Bird and Crozier have similar/higher survival than Royds, but survival does not reproduce Crozier > Bird.

### Not just recruitment

Age-specific recruitment matches Crozier > Bird > Royds, but survival-adjusted cohort recruitment ranks Bird > Crozier > Royds.

### Not just breeding propensity

Long-term breeder propensity ranks Crozier > Royds > Bird, not the focal restoration rank.

### Not just habitat capacity

Ross Island had substantial unused nesting habitat; Beaufort is the cleaner capacity-limited case.

### Not just iceberg presence as a common forcing

All colonies experienced the disturbance, but productivity effects and recovery were colony-specific.

---

## Most promising biological explanation

The best current synthesis is:

> **local recovery reflects the interaction of disturbance exposure with colony-specific demographic and social feedbacks, not one fixed “colony quality” axis.**

More specifically:

> **the iceberg likely changed the identity of the limiting process—from ordinary density-dependent foraging costs toward access limitation and small-colony fragmentation/Allee costs—thereby favoring a different colony ranking during recovery than under normal conditions.**

This is a hypothesis, not a result of the frozen submission.

## Bottom line

The next mechanistic question is not:

> Why did the largest colony recover most?

It is:

> **Why did an extreme environmental perturbation reverse the usual balance between the costs and benefits of colonial density, allowing some colonies to rebound while others remained trapped?**
