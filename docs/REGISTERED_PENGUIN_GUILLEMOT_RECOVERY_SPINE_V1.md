## Working title

**Population recovery need not retrace spatial collapse in colonial seabirds**

Alternative:

**Breeding-site recovery need not reverse the spatial pathway of population decline**

## One paper-level question

> **Is spatial recovery the reverse of spatial collapse in colonial breeders?**

The paper does not ask whether abundance recovers. It asks whether a recovering population reconstructs breeding space along the same ecological path by which that space was lost.

## Two complementary observations

### Antarctic penguins — discovery at population scale

Within declining Pygoscelis breeding systems:

- effective breeding-component number falls beyond proportional thinning;
- the result is prospectively replicated at Signy across geography and species;
- the concentration endpoint recurs at a broader regional monitoring scale within declining networks.

The regional boundary is crucial:

- all three eligible increasing regional networks finish with lower effective breeding-site number than at their first retained complete season;
- in two networks abundance increases substantially while effective breeding-site number decreases.

Therefore current abundance is not sufficient to describe breeding-space state.

This does **not** by itself demonstrate hysteresis.

### Isle of May guillemots — direct same-place test

For individually mapped breeding sites, compare a site's transition into annual vacancy with its later reoccupation.

For site j:

[
H_j=A_{r,j}-A_{v,j}.
]

Prediction:

[
H_j>0.
]

The focal site is excluded from the surrounding population state.

This directly tests whether the same place is recovered under the same population state at which it was previously lost.

## Competing ecological models

### Reversible spatial response

Breeding-site occupancy is determined by current population state and persistent place quality.

For the same place:

[
A_r \approx A_v.
]

Once abundance returns, previously used space should be recoverable along approximately the reverse occupancy path.

### History-dependent spatial recovery

The state of a site depends partly on its occupancy history.

Once a breeding site has become vacant, its later recovery occurs at a different surrounding population state:

[
A_r>A_v.
]

Possible causes include social information, established-colony attraction, demographic composition, delayed recruitment or time-varying site conditions.

The primary analysis does not identify which cause is responsible.

## Introduction logic

### Paragraph 1 — abundance recovery is not spatial recovery

Population status is usually summarized by abundance, but colonial populations occupy discrete breeding locations. Two populations with the same abundance can therefore differ in how broadly reproduction is distributed.

### Paragraph 2 — decline can alter spatial organization

Antarctic penguin data show that declines are not simply proportional thinning. Breeding becomes disproportionately concentrated into fewer effective components, replicated across independent populations and species.

### Paragraph 3 — numerical increase does not guarantee spatial reconstruction

At a broader regional level, effective breeding-site number can remain low or decline even while abundance increases. This generates a stronger question: is the spatial pathway itself history dependent?

### Paragraph 4 — existing theory allows asymmetry but does not provide this empirical test

Island biogeography and metapopulation theory distinguish extinction and colonization, and colonial-seabird studies already show density- and quality-dependent site use. Guillemot work further distinguishes new from reoccupied sites.

The missing test is whether **the same place** undergoes loss and later recovery at systematically different population states.

### Paragraph 5 — complementary systems

Antarctic penguins provide the population-level discovery.

The Isle of May guillemot time series provides individually mapped same-site histories across population increase, decline and recovery.

Together they ask whether spatial recovery retraces spatial collapse across two independent colonial-seabird systems and two spatial resolutions.

## Discussion if supported

### 1. Recovery is not simply the reverse movie of decline

The combined evidence would show two forms of non-reciprocity:

- population-level breeding space does not necessarily recover with numbers;
- at the same breeding places, reoccupation occurs at a different surrounding population state from prior vacancy.

### 2. Occupancy history becomes an ecological state variable

A single current abundance value is insufficient to predict spatial state if the same abundance can correspond to different site-use configurations depending on whether the population is contracting or rebuilding.

This is the central conceptual contribution.

### 3. Relation to island biogeography

Classical island biogeography treats colonization and extinction as the processes underlying occupancy.

The result would add an empirical within-place perspective:

> the transition into absence and the later transition out of absence need not be reciprocal functions of population state.

The breeding sites function as habitat islands nested within colonial populations, while the penguin systems show the same non-reciprocity at larger spatial units.

### 4. Relation to coloniality

Colonial breeding can make occupancy state itself biologically consequential because conspecific presence carries information and changes settlement payoff.

This makes social positive feedback a plausible mechanism.

It remains an interpretation, not an identified cause.

### 5. Conservation implication

A recovering head count can overstate recovery if occupied breeding space has not been rebuilt.

Monitoring only total abundance may therefore miss a lagging spatial dimension of recovery.

The practical unit to retain in long-term monitoring is not merely total breeders but the history and distribution of occupied breeding locations.

## Strongest allowed synthesis

If the guillemot primary passes all frozen conditions:

> **Antarctic penguin populations repeatedly lost breeding space faster than expected from proportional thinning, and numerical growth did not necessarily rebuild that space. In an independent common-guillemot system, previously used breeding sites were reoccupied at higher surrounding population states than those associated with their earlier transition to vacancy. Together, these results show that population recovery need not retrace spatial collapse in colonial seabirds.**

## What the paper still cannot claim

Even after full support, do not claim:

- a universal seabird law;
- permanent irreversibility;
- an exact continuous-time colonization threshold;
- individual behavioural thresholds;
- causal social attraction;
- causal Allee effects;
- habitat/site quality is unimportant;
- the same mechanism links penguins and guillemots.
