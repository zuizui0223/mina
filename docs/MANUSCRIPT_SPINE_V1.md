# Manuscript spine v1 — Palmer penguin island ecology

## Working title

**Common decline, divergent collapse: breeding-colony network structure predicts local resilience across Antarctic penguin islands**

Alternative shorter title:

**Regional decline and local colony-network erosion across Antarctic penguin islands**

## One-sentence claim

Across neighboring Antarctic breeding islands, Adélie populations share an exceptionally strong long-term decline but differ in short-term dynamics and extinction, and modest local resilience information lies in how breeders are distributed among subcolonies rather than in simple sea-ice-duration, annual snowfall, or large-group-retention metrics.

## Why this is island ecology

This is not a generic climate–population correlation study. Pygoscelis penguins are central-place marine foragers: most trophic resources are external to the breeding island, while reproduction is spatially constrained to discrete terrestrial patches. The system therefore separates regional marine forcing from local breeding-patch state unusually well.

The empirical hierarchy is:

1. **archipelago assembly** — breeding sites differ in species composition and replacement endpoint;
2. **regional population direction** — five Adélie islands collapse together over decades;
3. **local demographic response** — year-to-year synchrony and extinction risk differ strongly among islands;
4. **within-island structure** — the distribution of breeders among subcolonies contains modest predictive information about next-year growth.

## Results order

### R1. Apparent island phenotype is mostly compositional

The original Palmer Penguins signal is retained only as motivation. Morphology predicts island relative to a pooled marginal, but species identity carries more information and the species-conditioned morphology increment is negative. Within Adélie, island morphology/isotope signatures transfer across years at approximately the three-class chance reference.

Biological point: the obvious “island phenotype” largely reflects who occupies the breeding site rather than a stable within-species island ecotype.

### R2. Long-term assembly endpoints are heterogeneous

APBP/MAPPPD establishes that local decline does not imply a single replacement pathway. Litchfield reaches observed Adélie local extinction without focal-species replacement, whereas the non-island Biscoe Point benchmark shifts from Adélie dominance to >90% gentoo in the observed focal composition. Dream suggests an Adélie–chinstrap pathway but lacks synchronized composition coverage for confirmatory trajectory inference.

Biological point: regional change produces **vacancy, persistence, and replacement**, not one universal succession rule.

### R3. Five true islands share the long-term direction, not the annual dynamics

For Christine, Cormorant, Humble, Litchfield and Torgersen, the complete 1991–2017 panel gives:

- PC1 = 96.4% of standardized log-abundance variation;
- median pairwise annual-growth correlation = 0.373;
- unique year fraction = 53.5%;
- unique island fraction = 33.0%.

Litchfield reaches zero in 2007 while the other four persist at low abundance through 2017.

Biological point: **regional synchrony and local vulnerability coexist at different timescales**.

### R4. Simple environmental forcing formulations fail

Prospectively bounded tests do not recover the common decline from:

- positive annual response to preceding sea-ice season duration;
- 3-, 5-, or 7-year smoothed duration after temporal baselines and overlap-purged validation;
- October snowfall × published snow-prone habitat as an annual local-deviation mechanism.

These failures are scientifically useful because they prevent the 96.4% common component from being relabeled post hoc as any convenient climate covariate.

### R5. Distributed colony structure predicts local resilience

Effective colony number at year t provides a small positive held-out-year increment for next-year island growth after current abundance, island identity and secular year are included. The effect persists after previous growth is added and is stronger in the four persistently extant islands.

Crucially:
- simple active colony count does not improve transfer;
- an external historical >50-pair group hypothesis improves prediction but in the **opposite** direction to its predeclared positive prediction.

Biological point: the signal is not “more colonies” or “more large groups.” It is consistent with **how breeders are distributed across the colony network**.

## Discussion spine

### D1. Externally subsidized islands separate two ecological scales

Marine resources can impose a broad regional demographic regime while island breeding surfaces determine where that regime ends in persistence, concentration, or extinction.

### D2. Synchrony does not identify mechanism

A near-common long-term decline is not evidence for a specific annual sea-ice mechanism. The prospective negative tests show why common trajectories and mechanistic attribution must be kept separate.

### D3. Colony-network erosion is a state variable of local vulnerability

A population can retain the same total abundance while becoming increasingly concentrated among fewer dominant subcolonies. Effective colony number captures that internal organization. Its predictive signal is modest, but its specificity relative to active-colony count and the failed >50-pair hypothesis makes it biologically sharper.

### D4. Do not overclaim fragmentation yet

Colony codes are observational breeding groups, not independently mapped habitat patches. The next causal step is external spatial/topographic validation, not further tuning of topology metrics.

### D5. Broader island-biogeographic implication

For highly mobile marine consumers, island quality is not reducible to geographic isolation or island area. A more relevant state space may combine marine access, usable breeding surface, and the spatial distribution of breeding groups. This provides a form of island biogeography in which resources are external but reproduction remains patch-limited.

## Claims to avoid

Do not claim that sea ice is unimportant, that colony-network diversity causally protects populations, that large groups are harmful, that Biscoe Point is a true island replicate, or that the modest colony-network gain explains the dominant long-term decline.
