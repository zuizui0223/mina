# Literature positioning — replicated breeding-space contraction v1

## Core gap

Long-term penguin studies already show population decline, colony/subcolony disappearance, habitat-structured persistence, and fine-scale fragmentation. The present manuscript does **not** claim any of those observations as new.

The unresolved question is distributional:

> When total breeding abundance declines, does breeding effort simply thin in fixed proportion across established breeding components, or does the relative breeding distribution itself contract?

The paper fills this gap by preserving each observed abundance trajectory under a time-invariant latent breeding composition and asking whether the observed decline in effective breeding-component number is more negative than proportional thinning plus the frozen count-error family can produce.

## What was already known

### Signy population decline and colony loss

Dunn et al. (2016) documented long-term declines of Adélie and chinstrap penguins at Signy and disappearance of monitored colonies. Those results establish decline and local colony loss. They do not test whether the *relative distribution* of breeding pairs among a fixed set of breeding components changes more strongly than expected from the total-abundance trajectory alone.

### Fine-scale fragmentation during Adélie decline

McDowall & Lynch (2019) showed theoretically that declining Adélie abundance can produce fragmentation of nest aggregations, with site fidelity and edge effects creating feedbacks. That process operates at a finer spatial level than the present effective-component endpoint.

### Subcolony habitat and reproductive performance

Schmidt et al. (2021) showed that subcolony-scale habitat and geometry are associated with Adélie reproductive performance. This establishes biological relevance of within-colony spatial organization but does not provide a longitudinal proportional-thinning test.

### Physical subcolony extinction at Torgersen

Cimino et al. (2025) reconstructed loss of historical Torgersen subcolony footprints and showed non-random persistence with respect to snow and terrain. The mapped record supplies physical spatial triangulation for coarse breeding-component loss. It does not validate nominal LTER colony codes one-to-one and is not used as a merged response dataset.

## General ecological framing

Rodríguez (2002) showed at geographic-range scale that equal losses of total abundance can generate different spatial contractions depending on where within a distribution decline is concentrated. The present study is a nested within-population analogue: instead of species-range cells, the units are established breeding components within colonial populations.

The general point is therefore not “penguin colonies disappear.” It is:

> **Population totals do not determine the spatial trajectory of decline.**

In five declining penguin population units, breeding effort contracted onto fewer effective breeding components beyond proportional thinning. The population-level endpoint recurred across two *Pygoscelis* species and two Antarctic systems, while the identity of the components retaining dominance differed between systems.

## Novel contribution

1. **Explicit proportional-thinning null.** The observed total-abundance trajectory is retained while latent relative breeding composition is fixed.
2. **Repeated Palmer discovery.** Three stable-roster Adélie island populations independently show concentration beyond the frozen error family.
3. **Prospective external replication.** The same endpoint was frozen before effect computation in an independent Signy Adélie panel and was supported.
4. **Prospective cross-species replication.** A separately frozen Signy chinstrap panel also supported the same concentration endpoint.
5. **Mechanistic boundary.** Post-hoc component trajectories show that the replicated endpoint does not require a universal “largest colony survives” mechanism: Palmer shows dominance turnover, whereas Signy shows core retention.
6. **Hierarchical spatial synthesis.** Coarse loss of breeding components can coexist with fine-scale fragmentation inside retained components.

## Wording boundaries

### Safe

- “breeding-space contraction recurred across the studied declining Adélie and chinstrap populations”
- “decline was accompanied by loss of effective breeding-component number beyond proportional thinning”
- “the same population-level endpoint arose through different component-level routes”
- “a within-population analogue of spatial contraction during abundance decline”

### Avoid

- “first evidence that penguin colonies contract during decline”
- “first demonstration of penguin colony extinction”
- “large colonies are universally more resilient”
- “concentration is an early-warning signal”
- “public information or breeding dispersal causes concentration”
- “the result applies to all penguins”
- “N_eff is physical occupied area or genetic effective population size”

## Citation set for the main conceptual argument

- Rodríguez 2002 — abundance loss versus spatial range contraction.
- McDowall & Lynch 2019 — fine-scale fragmentation mechanism during Adélie decline.
- Schmidt et al. 2021 — biological relevance of subcolony habitat/geometry.
- Dunn et al. 2016 — known Signy abundance declines and colony disappearance.
- Cimino et al. 2019 — local snow/geomorphology context near Palmer.
- Cimino et al. 2025 — mapped Torgersen subcolony loss, habitat-structured persistence and within-footprint fragmentation.

## Editorial pitch

This is a population/spatial ecology paper rather than a monitoring-method paper. The transferable principle is that abundance decline and spatial contraction are distinct state changes: replicated population decline compressed breeding effort into fewer effective components, but the internal route to that endpoint varied among systems.
