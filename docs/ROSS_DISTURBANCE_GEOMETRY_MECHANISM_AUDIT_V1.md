# Ross Island disturbance-geometry mechanism audit v1

**Status:** post-result mechanism audit. This file asks whether the 1999–2002 shock/rebound asymmetry has an exogenous spatial explanation before invoking endogenous Allee/hysteresis mechanisms.

## Main conclusion

The immediate shock/rebound asymmetry has a strong prior explanation in **colony-specific access geometry and environmental forcing**.

Configuration-mediated hysteresis remains plausible for longer-term recovery, especially Cape Royds, but should not be the first explanation for the 2001–2002 pulse.

## Published natural experiment

B-15A and C-16 lodged against Ross Island in 2001.

Published work describes several spatially explicit consequences:

- the icebergs physically separated Cape Crozier from the western Ross Island colonies;
- they altered spring migration routes;
- they changed the persistence and movement of sea ice;
- extensive sea ice increased travel distance to open water and food;
- iceberg years increased breeding dispersal;
- effects differed among colonies.

The disturbance was therefore region-wide but **not spatially homogeneous in how colonies experienced access costs**.

## 2001 shock ranking

Observed 1999 -> 2001 three-colony breeding-count multipliers:

    Bird    0.556
    Crozier 0.429
    Royds   0.378.

This means Bird retained the largest fraction of its pre-shock breeding count and Royds the smallest.

Contemporary observations are qualitatively concordant:

- Cape Crozier's approach was disrupted by B-15A and very few of the usual breeders had arrived by November 2001;
- after a major December wind event, Cape Bird regained comparatively good access to open water and contemporary reporting noted substantially greater chick presence there than at the more severely affected colonies;
- Cape Royds remained burdened by very long fast-ice travel distances and was especially severely affected.

Thus the temporary 2001 equalization is parsimoniously compatible with **differential accessibility under the same regional disturbance**.

## Rebound and iceberg-era demographic differences

Observed 2001 -> 2002 colony multipliers:

    Crozier 2.400
    Royds   1.638
    Bird    1.546.

Cape Crozier produced the largest immediate rebound.

Longer-term studies report that:

- Bird and Crozier breeder apparent survival was consistently higher than Royds;
- movement probabilities increased in iceberg years;
- movement increased especially from Royds to Bird, exceeding 3% in iceberg years;
- in difficult years Crozier generally had the most consistent access to ocean resources because of the adjacent polynya;
- Royds remained strongly constrained by persistent fast ice for several years.

This supplies an exogenous/environmental plus movement pathway for unequal recovery.

## Long-term pre-shock comparison

1999 -> 2012 breeding-count multipliers:

    Royds   0.852
    Bird    1.599
    Crozier 1.741.

Royds still had not recovered its 1999 breeding count, whereas Bird and Crozier were far above it.

That pattern is concordant with published evidence for:

- lower apparent survival at Royds;
- elevated iceberg-era dispersal away from Royds;
- difficult ocean access;
- later work showing a fragmented, edge-rich nesting configuration at Royds that could further slow recovery.

## Mechanism hierarchy

### Level 1 — direct disturbance geometry

Best-supported explanation for the immediate 2001 shock pattern:

    iceberg / sea-ice geometry
        -> colony-specific access and breeding costs
        -> unequal breeding participation / abandonment
        -> temporary redistribution of breeding counts.

### Level 2 — demographic redistribution

Supported independent processes during iceberg years include:

    colony-specific apparent survival
    + increased breeding dispersal
    + altered breeding propensity / attendance.

These can make rebound fractions differ across colonies.

### Level 3 — configuration-mediated memory

Prior theory and Royds observations make this plausible for **persistence of slow recovery**:

    sharp decline
        -> fragmented / edge-rich configuration
        -> lower reproductive performance
        -> slower rebuilding.

But this level is not needed to explain the immediate 2001–2002 spatial pulse and is not identified causally by the census.

## Consequence for the hysteresis interpretation

The matched-N branch difference remains real as a descriptive state-space pattern:

    same approximate N
    -> different E / composition on different branches.

But the cause may be:

- exogenous environmental geometry;
- movement and breeding participation;
- endogenous configuration memory;
- or their interaction.

Therefore use:

> **branch dependence consistent with ecological memory**

rather than:

> **evidence that configuration hysteresis caused the Ross rebound.**

## Island-ecology interpretation

The relevant spatial variable is not island area alone.

For central-place breeders, the effective breeding landscape includes:

- nesting substrate;
- routes from breeding sites to the ocean;
- temporary barriers such as fast ice and icebergs;
- access to predictable foraging areas/polynyas.

A single regional disturbance can therefore be filtered differently by colonies on the same island.

This gives a stronger spatial-ecology statement:

> **shared environmental forcing can become spatially asymmetric because colony-to-resource access geometry differs among breeding nodes.**

## Novelty boundary

This mechanism is not newly discovered:

- the iceberg natural experiment and altered dispersal are published;
- colony-specific survival and movement differences are published.

PR189's contribution is to connect those known mechanisms to a formal distinction between:

    aggregate breeding restoration

and

    spatial restoration of the breeding network.

## Next prospective test

For future disturbances, freeze before outcome inspection:

1. colony-specific access cost to open water/foraging habitat;
2. current breeding count;
3. prior configuration;
4. breeding participation / success;
5. subsequent proportional restoration of local losses.

Primary prediction:

> local restoration fraction should be higher where disturbance-induced access cost is lower, after accounting for initial loss magnitude.

Secondary prediction:

> after access conditions normalize, prior fragmentation/configuration may explain residual recovery differences.
