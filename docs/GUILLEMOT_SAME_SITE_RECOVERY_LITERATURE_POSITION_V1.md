# Guillemot same-site recovery — literature position v1

**Date:** 2026-10-06  
**Status:** literature boundary fixed before raw EIDC biological outcomes are opened.

## Existing findings that are not novel here

### Site-dependent regulation and buffer effects

Kokko, Harris & Wanless (2004) showed in the Isle of May common-guillemot population that breeding-site quality strongly affects site use and that high-quality sites are preferentially retained or taken over.

Therefore this project does not claim novelty for:

- competition for breeding sites;
- site-dependent population regulation;
- preferential use of high-quality sites;
- rapid takeover of high-quality vacant sites.

### Population size, trend and site occupancy

Bennett et al. (2022, Journal of Animal Ecology, doi:10.1111/1365-2656.13674) analysed the same five long-term sub-colonies across increase, decline and recovery.

They established that:

- breeding-site occupancy depends on site quality and sub-colony size;
- the occupancy relationship can vary with population trend;
- the sub-colonies share a broad increase → decline → recovery sequence;
- historically used sites can be distinguished from newly occupied sites.

Therefore this project does not claim novelty for population-size-dependent occupancy or for analysing site use across recovery.

### New versus reoccupied sites

Bennett et al. explicitly classified historically occupied sites that became vacant and were later reoccupied. They compared:

- the proportion of new versus reoccupied sites;
- site quality of new versus reoccupied sites;
- these quantities across increase, decline and recovery phases.

They found no corresponding difference between the proportion of new and reoccupied sites during recovery, although reoccupied recovery-phase sites were of higher average quality.

Therefore this project does not claim novelty for distinguishing reoccupation from first occupation or for showing that previously used sites can return during recovery.

### Temporary non-breeding

Bennett et al. note that common guillemots can skip breeding for one or more years, with an average annual skipping frequency of 7.1% in the cited individual history.

Therefore annual site vacancy is not treated as permanent abandonment. The preregistered >=2-year vacancy restriction is an interpretive sensitivity, not a replacement primary analysis.

## Adjacent metapopulation literature

Classical metapopulation and island-biogeographic studies model or estimate colonization and extinction as distinct patch-level processes. Empirical studies often relate extinction to local population size or patch attributes and colonization to connectivity/isolation.

That literature establishes that colonization and extinction need not be governed by identical covariates.

It does not by itself test the paired question posed here:

> for one previously used breeding site, is the surrounding population state at later reoccupation systematically different from the state associated with its earlier transition to vacancy?

## Candidate contribution

The candidate contribution is deliberately narrow:

> **A preregistered same-site paired test of vacancy–reoccupation population-state asymmetry in individually mapped breeding sites.**

For each completed site history,

[
1 \rightarrow 0 \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow 0 \rightarrow 1,
]

the focal site is removed from its own surrounding sub-colony population state, and the transition-state proxy at reoccupation is compared with the transition-state proxy at the earlier move into vacancy.

The design is stronger than a phase comparison because:

1. the same breeding site is paired against itself;
2. fixed site identity and fixed site quality are therefore held constant by construction;
3. first occupation is excluded;
4. the event roster is frozen using occupancy states before population-size magnitudes are opened;
5. repeated events are collapsed within site;
6. inference is replicated across five sub-colonies rather than treating sites as independent;
7. a non-circular temporal-shift null asks whether the result is more than generic event alignment on the observed population trajectory.

## Allowed novelty statement if supported

> Previously used breeding sites were reoccupied at higher surrounding population states than those associated with their earlier transition to vacancy, revealing history-dependent spatial recovery within a colonial breeding population.

## Stronger wording that remains prohibited

Do not write:

- “we discovered the buffer effect”;
- “we first showed that guillemots reoccupy historical sites”;
- “we demonstrated permanent abandonment thresholds”;
- “we identified an individual return threshold”;
- “we proved an Allee effect”;
- “social attraction caused the pattern”;
- “site quality does not matter”;
- “all seabirds show hysteretic recovery”.

## Relation to the Antarctic penguin result

The penguin evidence establishes a different observation:

- decline repeatedly concentrates breeding effort beyond proportional thinning;
- numerical increase at broader regional scale does not necessarily restore effective breeding-site number.

Those data generate a recovery-asymmetry hypothesis but do not prospectively identify same-site loss/recovery events.

The guillemot data can supply the direct same-place test.

A supported result would therefore connect two different levels of evidence:

1. population-level spatial concentration in Antarctic Pygoscelis;
2. site-level vacancy/reoccupation asymmetry in an independently studied North Atlantic colonial seabird.

The combined paper would test non-reciprocity of spatial loss and recovery, not a universal mechanism.
