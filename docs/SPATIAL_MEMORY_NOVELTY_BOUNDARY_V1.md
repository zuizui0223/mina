# Integrated penguin manuscript — novelty boundary v1

**Date:** 2026-10-07  
**Canonical manuscript:** \`docs/MANUSCRIPT_SPATIAL_MEMORY_INTEGRATED_V0_4.md\`

## Not novel

The manuscript must not claim novelty for:

- ecological memory or biological legacies after disturbance;
- spatial recovery or metapopulation recovery theory;
- temporary emigration, breeding propensity, or unobservable nonbreeding states;
- breeding-site fidelity;
- animals returning to familiar areas after acute disturbance;
- breeding dispersal changing after colony disturbance or restoration;
- site fidelity affecting colony use or colony-size distributions;
- unequal local demographic rates changing aggregate spatial composition;
- the general result that abundance or function can recover while composition or patch occupancy remains altered.

Relevant prior art includes Johnstone et al. (2016), Zelnik et al. (2019), Wilson et al. (2023), Souchay et al. (2014), Kreling et al. (2021), Brown et al. (2017), Spendelow et al. (2016), and Safine et al. (2020).

## Relation to recovery prior art

Community and metapopulation recovery already establish a one-way warning:

> **aggregate recovery can hide structural non-recovery.**

Hillebrand et al. (2020) showed that abundance and biomass commonly recover more completely than multivariate community composition after pulse disturbances. Wilson et al. (2023) explicitly described metapopulation "hidden collapses" in which total abundance recovers despite persistent local patch collapse.

The Ross result is useful as the converse interpretive case:

> **aggregate collapse can overstate structural loss when a previous spatial allocation remains recoverable.**

This converse framing is descriptive and does not imply that latent structure was directly observed during the 2001 trough.

## Distinct empirical contribution

The distinctive observation is at the **population-allocation scale**:

> **A breeding population can lose more than half of its observed breeding abundance and still recover almost the same multi-node spatial allocation at the next census.**

Ross quantifies that statement:

- observed breeding abundance loss = 54.3%;
- aggregate loss restored next census = 96.97%;
- six-node loss/rebound cosine = 0.99695;
- inverse-path mismatch = 9.41% of rebound.

The 9.41% mismatch is not exceptional within Ross. The contribution is the conjunction of:
1. an externally documented severe disturbance;
2. near-complete aggregate rebound;
3. quantitative recovery of the multi-node allocation.

## Distinct comparative contribution

The same research program then shows why this is not a generic property of abundance change.

- Palmer + Signy: persistent decline changes relative allocation through unequal attrition beyond proportional thinning.
- Beaufort: changing capacity changes the spatial opportunity set, with disproportionate growth of a small/new within-island unit and reduced inter-island export.
- Bird / Emperor / Heard: aggregate direction does not sign-lock spatial direction.

Therefore the paper's comparative contribution is:

> **temporary suppression of an expressed breeding distribution, erosion of the site-affiliated demographic pool, and alteration of breeding capacity are biologically different routes to observed population change and leave different spatial signatures in the available cases.**

## Strongest novelty sentence

> **What disappears from a breeding census is not always what disappears from the breeding network—and what returns in total abundance is not always what returns in spatial structure.**

## Forbidden novelty inflation

Do not write that this study:
- discovers spatial memory;
- discovers site fidelity as a recovery mechanism;
- proves that fidelity caused the Ross rebound;
- discovers temporary nonbreeding;
- establishes a universal process-dependent law;
- shows that short-term disturbance is reversible and long-term change irreversible.

## Main remaining ceiling

Only one independent high-confidence temporary-shock system has the required baseline–trough–rebound spatial design.

A general law requires prospective replication in a new system.
