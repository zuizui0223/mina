# Ross Island breeding-abundance measurement audit v1

**Status:** post-result interpretation audit. This does not alter the frozen Ross V2 numerical result; it narrows what the census response means biologically.

## What the Ross aerial census measures

The public workbook is explicitly a **breeding-pair census**.

Lyver et al. (2014) describe annual Ross Island surveys as high-angle aerial photographs taken as close as possible to 1 December (25 November-8 December). At that stage of the breeding cycle, colonies are represented almost entirely by one incubating adult per pair and few non-breeders.

The count therefore approximates:

    occupied nesting territories / breeding pairs

not:

    total living adults in the local population.

Consequently, annual changes in the count can reflect a mixture of:

- survival;
- recruitment;
- immigration/emigration;
- breeding propensity / decision to breed;
- arrival/access to the colony;
- early breeding failure or abandonment before the census.

## No obvious 2001 measurement-method discontinuity

The large 2001-2002 change is not aligned with the main technical transitions in the aerial census.

According to Lyver et al. (2014):

- surveys were annual helicopter photographs on Ross Island;
- photographs were taken within the same late-November/early-December seasonal window;
- prior to 2006, the same medium-format film camera workflow was used;
- digital acquisition began in 2006;
- semi-automated counting began in 2010.

Thus there is no documented camera/counting-method switch between 2001 and 2002 that could trivially explain the concentration pulse.

Phenological variation within the two-week survey window is acknowledged by the source paper and described as a possible small component of annual variation.

## 2001 is biologically exceptional

The source paper explicitly identifies a common low point in southern Ross Sea breeding-pair counts around 2001.

That low point coincided with the arrival and grounding of giant icebergs B-15A and C-16.

The resulting sea-ice conditions:

- reduced ocean and food access;
- altered primary production and circulation;
- caused many Adélie adults to fail or abandon breeding attempts early in the initial iceberg season;
- affected Royds especially strongly, with prolonged fast ice and documented later emigration toward Bird.

Therefore the 2001-2002 increase should not be read as one-year multiplication of the total adult population.

It is better interpreted as a large change in **breeding abundance / breeding participation and spatial allocation** following an exceptional disturbance, with underlying demographic change also potentially contributing.

## Consequence for PR189

The frozen arithmetic remains valid because all quantities are calculated on the same census metric.

What changes is the biological noun.

Prefer:

- breeding-pair abundance;
- breeding abundance;
- breeding-distribution concentration;
- redistribution of breeding effort/territories among colonies;
- recovery of breeding abundance.

Avoid, unless explicitly qualified:

- total adult population doubled;
- all local populations grew;
- recruitment alone generated the 2001-2002 pulse;
- survival alone generated the 2001-2002 pulse.

## Ross result under the correct response definition

Frozen 2001-2012 endpoints:

    breeding pairs N: 94,798 -> 351,119
    E3: 1.7286 -> 1.5428
    E6: 2.2919 -> 1.9702.

Every monitored colony/component has a higher breeding-pair count at the 2012 endpoint than at the 2001 endpoint.

The largest compositional shift occurred in 2001-2002:

    breeding-pair N +115%
    E3 -12.8%
    E6 -20.6%
    Crozier share 70.8% -> 79.0%.

Thus the strongest defensible statement is:

> **During the transition out of the 2001 breeding-abundance low, recovery of occupied nesting territories was disproportionately expressed at Cape Crozier, producing an early concentration pulse despite higher breeding-pair counts in every monitored component.**

This does not require the total adult population to have increased by the same amount.

## Why this still matters ecologically

For colonial seabirds, breeding distribution is itself a meaningful population state.

It determines:

- where reproduction occurs;
- how breeding effort is exposed to local predators and terrain;
- which colonies carry most reproductive output;
- the spatial redundancy of the breeding population.

Therefore a mismatch between total breeding abundance and its spatial allocation remains biologically important even when annual breeding propensity contributes to the count.

## Connection to individual demography

The 25-year mark-recapture study shows that breeding propensity, survival, recruitment and movement all vary among Ross Island colonies.

This independently reinforces the interpretation that breeding-pair trajectories integrate multiple demographic processes rather than a single abundance process.

## Claim boundary

PR189 is a study of the **spatial organization of breeding abundance**.

It does not estimate total adult population size.

It does not decompose annual breeding-pair changes into survival, recruitment and breeding propensity.

Any causal statement about those components remains external mechanistic interpretation.
