# Spatial redundancy as dominance-weighted demographic arithmetic

**Status:** superseded by `SPATIAL_REDUNDANCY_GROWTH_IDENTITY_V2.md`. The algebra is correct, but V1 overinterpreted the finite-interval identity as requiring amplification of initially dominant nodes; the Heard Island dominance-reversal example shows that long intervals can end with E lower after a formerly subordinate node overtakes the initial dominant.

## State variable

For breeding units i with abundance n_i,

    N = sum_i n_i
    p_i = n_i / N
    H = sum_i p_i^2
    E = 1 / H

E is the Hill-Simpson effective number of equally abundant breeding units.

It measures abundance-weighted spatial redundancy over a fixed roster. It is not occupancy richness, geographic range, or dispersal.

## Continuous-time identity

Let

    r_i = d log(n_i) / dt

be the instantaneous per-capita growth rate of breeding unit i, and

    r_bar = sum_i p_i r_i

the abundance-weighted metapopulation growth rate.

Because

    d p_i / dt = p_i (r_i - r_bar),

the Simpson concentration H obeys

    dH/dt = 2 sum_i p_i^2 (r_i - r_bar).

Define dominance weights

    w_i = p_i^2 / H

and the dominance-weighted mean local growth rate

    r_D = sum_i w_i r_i.

Then

    d log(E) / dt = 2 (r_bar - r_D).

Therefore:

- E decreases exactly when r_D > r_bar;
- E is stationary when r_D = r_bar;
- E increases exactly when r_D < r_bar.

The sign of total population growth is irrelevant to this condition.

## Ecological meaning

### Differential attrition

If total abundance is falling but initially dominant units decline less rapidly than the average unit,

    r_bar < 0
    r_D > r_bar

and E decreases.

Concentration is produced by unequal losses.

### Differential amplification

If total abundance is rising and initially dominant units grow faster than the average unit,

    r_bar > 0
    r_D > r_bar

and E also decreases.

Concentration is produced by unequal gains.

Thus the same spatial endpoint can be generated by opposite demographic arithmetic.

### Spatial spreading

If smaller units grow sufficiently faster than the dominance-weighted expectation,

    r_D < r_bar

then E increases.

This can occur during numerical recovery, as in the corrected Beaufort example.

## Exact finite-interval identity

For an interval from 0 to 1, define the local multiplication factor

    G_i = n_i1 / n_i0

and starting shares p_i0.

The aggregate multiplication factor is

    G_bar = sum_i p_i0 G_i
          = N1 / N0.

Let

    H0 = sum_i p_i0^2
    w_i0 = p_i0^2 / H0

and define the starting-dominance-weighted RMS multiplication factor

    G_D = sqrt(sum_i w_i0 G_i^2).

Because

    p_i1 = p_i0 G_i / G_bar,

it follows exactly that

    E1 / E0 = (G_bar / G_D)^2.

No regression, simulation, or asymptotic approximation is involved.

Therefore over any finite interval:

- E falls iff G_D > G_bar;
- E is unchanged iff G_D = G_bar;
- E rises iff G_D < G_bar.

If every site changes by exactly the same proportion, all G_i are equal and E is unchanged.

## Ross Island recovery

Frozen three-colony 2001 -> 2012 multiplication factors:

- Royds: 2.2553x
- Bird: 2.8763x
- Crozier: 4.0579x

Starting shares were approximately:

- Royds: 0.0144
- Bird: 0.2776
- Crozier: 0.7080

Hence:

    aggregate G_bar = 3.7039
    dominance-weighted RMS G_D = 3.9206
    G_D / G_bar = 1.0585

and therefore

    E2012 / E2001 = (3.7039 / 3.9206)^2
                  = 0.8925.

Observed:

    E3: 1.7286 -> 1.5428
    change: -10.75%.

Every colony grew, but the colony carrying most starting abundance also had the highest multiplication factor.

This is differential amplification.

## Ross Island decline

For 1985 -> 1999:

- Royds: 1.1263x
- Bird: 0.8845x
- Crozier: 0.9331x

The total system declined:

    aggregate G_bar = 0.9242

but the dominance-weighted RMS factor was slightly higher:

    G_D = 0.9287.

Therefore:

    E1999 / E1985 = 0.9903

and three-colony E declined slightly.

At the finer six-component scale the decline in E was much stronger, showing that the demographic arithmetic is scale dependent.

## Beaufort recovery

For 2004 -> 2010:

- established main colony: 1.3360x
- new subcolony: 2.0804x

The new unit began with less than 1% of breeders but grew far faster.

Hence:

    aggregate G_bar = 1.3431
    dominance-weighted RMS G_D = 1.3361
    G_D / G_bar = 0.9948

and therefore

    E2010 / E2004 = 1.0105.

Observed:

    E: 1.0193 -> 1.0300.

Thus Beaufort lies on the opposite side of the same identity: growth was disproportionately allocated to the initially tiny breeding unit.

## What this changes conceptually

The primary ecological quantity is not the sign of N change and not E alone.

It is the **association between starting dominance and subsequent local growth**.

A concise interpretation is:

> Spatial redundancy declines whenever demographic change is preferentially amplified in already dominant breeding units, regardless of whether the metapopulation as a whole is declining or recovering.

This makes three superficially different processes comparable:

    decline + dominant nodes lose least
        -> differential attrition
        -> E down

    recovery + dominant nodes grow most
        -> differential amplification
        -> E down

    recovery + small/new nodes grow most
        -> spatial spreading
        -> E up

## Relation to mechanism

The identity itself does not explain why local growth differs.

Mechanism enters through local growth rates, for example:

    r_i(t) = R_t + q_i + c_i(t) + epsilon_i(t)

where:

- R_t is regional forcing shared among sites;
- q_i is persistent local demographic quality;
- c_i(t) is dynamic breeding-capacity release or constraint.

Ross is consistent with persistent quality differences: independent demographic data rank recruitment Crozier > Bird > Royds, matching the recovery multipliers.

Beaufort is consistent with a dynamic capacity release: newly usable nesting habitat allowed a previously tiny/new unit to gain disproportionate share.

## Inference boundary

- This identity is a decomposition of abundance arithmetic, not a causal model.
- E is dominance-sensitive and should not be described as patch occupancy or geographic range.
- A site-size versus growth regression is not required and should not be substituted for the exact decomposition.
- Mechanistic inference still requires independent demographic, habitat, or movement evidence.
