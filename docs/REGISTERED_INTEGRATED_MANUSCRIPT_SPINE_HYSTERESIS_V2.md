## Working title

**Population recovery does not retrace spatial collapse in colonial seabirds**

Alternative:

**Loss and recovery of breeding sites occur at different population states in colonial seabirds**

## One hypothesis

> **The spatial pathway of recovery is asymmetric: the same breeding sites are recovered at higher surrounding population states than those associated with their earlier loss.**

## Introduction logic

1. Island ecology is built from local extinction and colonization.
2. Theory already allows non-reciprocity and hysteresis; that concept is not new.
3. Colonial seabird studies already distinguish persistence, colonization and recolonization; that distinction is not new.
4. What remains to test directly is whether **the same breeding places are lost and later recovered at different surrounding population states** across multiple species.
5. Antarctic penguins provide a discovery: spatial concentration during decline is replicated, and regional space is not necessarily reconstructed during numerical increase.
6. SMP provides the independent prospective same-place test.

## Discussion if supported

### Recovery is not the reverse movie of collapse

Numerical recovery and spatial recovery are different processes.

### Place history matters

The current population state does not uniquely determine whether a formerly occupied breeding place is reoccupied.

### Link to island biogeography

The result provides a multi-species empirical instance of non-reciprocal local extinction and recolonization dynamics at the same places.

### Link to coloniality

Social attraction, site fidelity and Allee-like feedback are plausible mechanisms, but not identified.

### Conservation implication

Population totals can recover before historical breeding-space structure does.

## Strongest allowed conclusion

> **Across colonial seabirds, later recovery of the same breeding places occurred at higher surrounding population states than their earlier loss, beyond non-circular temporal placement on the observed local abundance trajectories. Population recovery therefore did not simply retrace spatial collapse.**
