## Global Ecology and Biogeography

Ecological information measured at one spatial scale is often assumed to remain informative when compressed into broad landscape descriptors, but that transfer is rarely tested directly. We examined this problem across nested scales in Antarctic penguins. At Palmer Station, five neighbouring Adélie penguin (*Pygoscelis adeliae*) populations shared a highly coherent long-term decline while breeders became progressively concentrated into fewer within-island colony-code groups. A dynamically updated late-season colony state predicted relative redistribution during the denominator-separated *t*+1 to *t*+2 transition after shared island-transition growth, prior group size and persistent colony identity were controlled (β = 0.0409; 100,000 within-season permutations; one-sided *p* = 0.00327). The association persisted at lag 3 but faded by lags 4–5, and a predeclared 4–5-year recruitment-echo contrast was negative. An independently sampled nest-level endpoint did not reproduce the primary association (mean chicks reaching crèche per monitored nest: β = 0.0296, *p* = 0.232), indicating that the informative variable is a specific late-season colony-wide state rather than generic reproductive success.

We then asked whether static breeding-island architecture captured transferable demographic information across Antarctica using three *Pygoscelis* species and 104 predictor-complete site × species units from 1980–2025. At the prespecified 2 km scale, area × habitat-complex interactions were negative in all three species, but the preregistered cross-species permutation test was non-confirmatory (median γ_AH = −0.318; 9,999 permutations; one-sided *p* = 0.0947). A simpler breeding-space buffering hypothesis was also unsupported, and radius variation did not pass the joint criterion for biological scale dependence. The two analyses are not a head-to-head comparison of predictive performance; rather, they reveal an evidentiary asymmetry. Local demographic history passed its frozen information test, whereas static island architecture did not yield a confirmed, scale-invariant transfer rule. More generally, ecological information can be valid at one hierarchical level without surviving aggregation into macroecological descriptors.

## Ecography

Ecological patterns are observed at multiple spatial and temporal grains, but information that is meaningful locally need not survive aggregation across scale. We tested this idea in Antarctic penguins by linking within-island demographic reorganization to an Antarctic-wide analysis of breeding-landscape structure. At Palmer Station, five neighbouring Adélie penguin populations shared a strong long-term decline while breeders became increasingly concentrated into fewer within-island colony-code groups. A relative late-season colony state carried short-lived temporal information about subsequent redistribution: the denominator-separated lag-2 coefficient was positive after shared island-transition growth, prior group size and persistent colony identity were controlled (β = 0.0409, *p* = 0.00327), remained positive at lag 3, and faded by lags 4–5. A predeclared delayed recruitment-echo contrast was negative. Importantly, an independent nest-level measure of mean chicks reaching crèche did not reproduce the primary association (β = 0.0296, *p* = 0.232), showing that the informative state is not interchangeable with generic productivity.

At the broader scale, we tested whether static breeding-space amount and habitat-complex heterogeneity explained site-specific demographic coupling across 104 predictor-complete Adélie, chinstrap and gentoo site × species units. The 2 km area × heterogeneity interaction was negative in all three species, but the preregistered cross-species test was non-confirmatory (median γ_AH = −0.318, *p* = 0.0947), and the apparent 1–5 km sign change failed the joint scale-dependence criterion. These analyses are inferentially independent and do not compare effect sizes directly. Together they show that dynamic demographic history can be detectable at a fine spatial grain while static landscape summaries remain sensitive to spatial support and fail to provide a confirmed scale-invariant representation at broader grains. The results highlight a general distinction among local explanatory structure, historical state information and transferable spatial structure.

## Journal of Animal Ecology

Population decline need not be spatially uniform, and the distribution of breeders can retain information about recent biological history even under shared environmental forcing. We investigated this process in Adélie penguins near Palmer Station and then tested whether the resulting local organization generalized through static landscape structure across Antarctic *Pygoscelis* populations. Five neighbouring Palmer populations shared a highly coherent long-term decline, yet breeders became progressively concentrated into fewer within-island colony-code groups. A late-season colony-wide state predicted subsequent relative redistribution after shared island-transition growth, prior group size and persistent colony identity were controlled. The denominator-separated primary lag-2 coefficient was β = 0.0409 (*p* = 0.00327), the association remained positive at lag 3 and faded at lags 4–5, and a predeclared 4–5-year recruitment-echo contrast was negative. An independent monitored-nest endpoint did not reproduce the primary association (β = 0.0296, *p* = 0.232), demonstrating that the predictive state cannot be generalized as ordinary reproductive success.

We next tested whether static breeding-space amount and habitat heterogeneity provided a broader demographic rule across 104 predictor-complete site × species units spanning Adélie, chinstrap and gentoo penguins. Although area × heterogeneity estimates were negative in all three species, the preregistered cross-species permutation test was non-confirmatory (median γ_AH = −0.318, *p* = 0.0947), and the result was sensitive to spatial radius. Thus the positive local result and the broad-scale non-confirmation define different limits of inference: recent colony state carries short-lived demographic history within a declining metapopulation, but that information is not captured by a confirmed scale-invariant landscape rule. The study therefore separates physical habitat, dynamic population state and individual behaviour as distinct levels of demographic organization.
