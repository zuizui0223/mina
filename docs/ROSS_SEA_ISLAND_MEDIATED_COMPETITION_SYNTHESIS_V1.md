# Islands as switches between competition release and dispersal in Adélie penguins — synthesis v1

**Status:** mechanistic synthesis for the independent Ross Sea follow-up. It does not alter the frozen Ecology submission.

## Core proposition

Island structure may determine **how density dependence is expressed spatially**.

When an occupied island has residual breeding capacity, extra breeders can be retained locally through infilling or within-island colony expansion.

When local capacity is constrained, the same demographic pressure can instead be expressed as emigration to another island.

Thus the relevant island-ecology process is not simply:

```
isolation -> dispersal
```

but:

```
local density / competition
        x
island breeding capacity
        ↓
stay and intensify
        vs
cross island boundary
```

## Beaufort Island is a direct natural experiment

LaRue et al. (2013) provide unusually direct evidence for this mechanism in the southern Ross Sea Adélie metapopulation.

For the main Beaufort Island colony:
- usable nesting habitat increased by **71%** from 1958 to 2010;
- population size increased by **84%**;
- the increase in habitat after glacial retreat was accompanied by reduced emigration of Beaufort-born birds to nearby Ross Island colonies after 2005.

The geographic distance between Beaufort and Ross Island did not change. What changed was the amount of usable breeding space on Beaufort.

That makes the causal interpretation unusually sharp:

> **changing island capacity altered inter-island movement.**

## A within-island expansion step is visible as well

The same study reports a newly founded disjunct subcolony on the northeast coast of Beaufort Island in 2004.

The public 1981–2024 Ross Sea aerial-census workbook contains a separate row named `Beaufort Island New` with:
- **460 pairs in 2004**;
- **491 in 2005**;
- **544 in 2008**;
- **957 in 2010**.

These values match the published description of the new Beaufort subcolony increasing from 460 to 957 pairs.

Therefore the recovery/expansion sequence is not only between islands. Beaufort shows a nested response:

```
habitat release on one island
        ↓
main colony can grow / infill
        ↓
new subcolony founded elsewhere on the same island
        ↓
emigration to another island decreases
```

This is exactly the hierarchy required by an island-mediated competition model.

## Ross Island provides the contrasting state

The southern Ross Sea metapopulation consists of:
- Cape Royds;
- Cape Bird;
- Cape Crozier;
on Ross Island, plus Beaufort Island.

Published work notes that unoccupied subcolony mounds with nesting pebbles indicate that the Ross Island colonies were not strongly space-limited during the study period, whereas Beaufort was uniquely constrained by glaciers/cliffs before habitat release.

Thus two nearby islands offered different terrestrial competitive landscapes even while sharing broad marine forcing.

## Mechanistic interpretation

A breeder's spatial decision can be represented as:

```
U(stay on island i)
  = habitat quality
  + social benefits
  - intraspecific crowding
  + philopatry

U(move to island j)
  = alternative habitat payoff
  - crossing / settlement cost
  - uncertainty
```

Increasing local capacity lowers the crowding term and therefore raises the payoff of staying.

The Beaufort natural experiment supports precisely this direction:
- more local breeding space;
- more local breeders;
- less export to Ross Island.

## Islands therefore act as demographic capacitors

A useful interpretation is that an island can temporarily **store population growth**.

When residual capacity is high:
> regional growth is absorbed locally.

As capacity is filled:
> competition rises and dispersal pressure increases.

This predicts a sequence:

```
growth
-> within-colony infilling
-> within-island expansion / new subcolony
-> inter-island dispersal
```

The thresholds need not be identical among islands because snow, glacier cover, topography, landing access and food-access geometry alter effective capacity.

## Connection to the Palmer/Signy decline results

The decline side appears asymmetric.

The five Palmer/Signy concentration trajectories are dominated by **differential attrition**, not active aggregation:
- 0/39 frozen components increased from first to last retained positive season;
- only 153 gross gains accompanied 7034 gross losses during joint N-down/E-down transitions.

Thus decline does not look like mass movement into a refuge.

A coherent island-mediated cycle is instead:

```
DECLINE
local conditions suppress some components
-> selective attrition
-> components disappear
-> surviving island/colony space becomes under-filled

RECOVERY
survivors initially absorb new breeders
-> within-island intensification
-> local capacity fills
-> competition rises
-> within-island expansion
-> eventually inter-island redistribution
```

This produces spatial path dependence without requiring active aggregation during decline.

## Interspecific competition

Island mediation may also apply to heterospecifics, but evidence is weaker and more local.

Where Adélie, gentoo or chinstrap breeding habitat overlaps, other species can reduce effective nesting capacity.

In the Ross Sea, published syntheses suggest that inter- and intraspecific competition may contribute at Cape Crozier and Beaufort, while Ross Island colonies also show substantial unused terrestrial breeding space.

Therefore the primary mechanism to test remains **intraspecific capacity-mediated redistribution**; interspecific nesting competition is a modifier of effective local capacity rather than the core universal rule.

## Strong next prediction

The independent Ross Sea follow-up should test whether the spatial level at which growth appears changes with local capacity:

1. first within an established colony;
2. then among subcolonies on the same island;
3. only later across island boundaries.

The Beaufort case already supplies a natural-history sequence consistent with levels 1–3 and direct band-resighting evidence for level 3.

A general multi-island test would ask whether the probability of crossing an island boundary rises when residual breeding capacity on the natal/current island is low, after controlling for distance and regional abundance.
