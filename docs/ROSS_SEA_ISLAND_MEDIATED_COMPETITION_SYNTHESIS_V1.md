# Island capacity may switch the spatial scale of Adélie redistribution — synthesis v2

**Status:** corrected mechanistic synthesis for the independent Ross Sea follow-up. It does not alter the frozen Ecology submission.

## Core proposition

The strongest current idea is not "growth intensifies before it expands."

Instead:

> **changes in usable breeding capacity may shift the spatial scale at which population redistribution occurs.**

An island with newly available breeding space can retain more breeders while those breeders spread among sites within that island. Reduced movement across an island boundary therefore does not imply increased concentration within the island.

## Beaufort supplies the key conjunction

LaRue et al. (2013) report an increase in usable Beaufort nesting habitat and a later decline in Beaufort-to-Ross-Island emigration/visitation.

The public census simultaneously shows that from 2004 to 2010 the new Beaufort subcolony:

- increased by 108%, versus 33.6% in the main colony;
- increased its share from 0.95% to 1.48%;
- received about 3.15 times the net gain expected under proportional growth;
- caused the two-unit effective number to rise from 1.0193 to 1.0300.

Thus the observed pattern is:

    capacity release
        ↓
    more growth retained on Beaufort
        ↓
    disproportionate growth of a smaller/new within-island unit
        ↓
    less export to Ross Island

The important contrast is **within-island spreading plus reduced between-island movement**.

## What this changes

The earlier "demographic capacitor" picture implied a serial sequence in which an established colony first fills and only later expands.

The data do not justify that sequence.

A better concept is a **hierarchical spatial gate**:

    local capacity state
          ↓
    how much redistribution is expressed
    within subcolonies / among colonies / across islands

This allows simultaneous infilling and within-island expansion.

## Decline and recovery are still asymmetric

Palmer/Signy decline is dominated by differential attrition:

- 0/39 frozen components increased from first to last retained positive season;
- gross positive gains were tiny relative to gross losses during joint N-down/E-down transitions.

So decline concentration is not evidence of active aggregation.

The revised cycle is:

    DECLINE
    local filtering
      -> unequal losses
      -> contraction / concentration

    RECOVERY
    absolute gains
      -> proportional or disproportionate redistribution among surviving units
      -> deconcentration when smaller units gain share

    CAPACITY / ISLAND BOUNDARY
      -> may determine whether redistribution is expressed within an island
         or as movement to another island

This is process asymmetry: the arithmetic generating contraction differs from the arithmetic generating recovery, even if an effective-number index eventually retraces part of its path.

## Ross Island is now an independent pre-effect test

Ross Island contains three major colony nodes:

1. Cape Royds
2. Cape Bird
3. Cape Crozier

The aerial workbook subdivides Bird into South/Middle/North and Crozier into West/East.

The primary Ross analysis therefore aggregates to the three biological colony nodes. The six census components are a secondary scale check.

Before the Ross counts are opened, the revised prediction is:

- decline phase: N decreases and three-colony E decreases;
- recovery phase: N increases and three-colony E increases.

The proportional reference is fixed starting composition. If all colonies change by the same proportion, E should remain unchanged.

The abundance–space kappa is secondary because both N and E can trend with year.

## What Ross can and cannot test

Ross Island can test whether numerical decline and recovery have opposite spatial directions **within one island**.

It cannot identify:

- individual dispersal;
- crossing of island boundaries;
- true colonisation;
- competition coefficients.

The Beaufort band/resighting evidence remains the direct movement component.

## General island-ecology hypothesis

The broader hypothesis to take beyond this case is:

> when effective breeding capacity changes, the probability that population redistribution crosses an island boundary should change relative to redistribution among breeding units on the same island.

A general test would need multiple islands with:

- usable breeding-capacity estimates through time;
- individual movement or defensible colonisation/reoccupation histories;
- distance among islands;
- abundance;
- search effort.

The current program now has one mechanistic case (Beaufort) and one independent within-island decline/recovery test waiting to be opened (Ross Island).

## Claim boundary

Current evidence supports a case-specific capacity–movement link and a revised, testable multiscale hypothesis.

It does **not** yet support the universal statement that island boundaries are mechanistic switches. That becomes the hypothesis to test, not the conclusion to assume.
