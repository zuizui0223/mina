## Working title

**Population recovery does not retrace spatial collapse in colonial seabirds**

Alternative:

**Population recovery does not retrace spatial collapse in colonial breeding systems**

## One hypothesis

> **The spatial pathway of population recovery is asymmetric: the same breeding sites are recovered at systematically higher surrounding population states than those associated with their loss.**

For the same SiteID:

\[
H=A_{\mathrm{recolonize}}-A_{\mathrm{abandon}}.
\]

Prediction:

\[
H>0.

## Competing model

### Reversible site-quality / buffer model

Stable place quality and current population size determine which breeding sites are occupied.

After pairing the same SiteID:

\[

\]

Population recovery should approximately retrace the occupancy pathway of decline.

### History-dependent recovery model

Current occupancy history changes the effective recovery threshold.

After a breeding aggregation disappears:

\[

Mechanisms can include social attraction, site fidelity, public information, Allee-type feedback or other biological memory, but the primary test does not distinguish among them.

## Introduction logic

### Paragraph 1 — island biogeography is about two directions

Island and metapopulation dynamics are built from extinction and colonization. These processes are often modeled separately, but ecological recovery is commonly interpreted as if increasing population state should reverse previous spatial loss.

The key question is whether loss and recovery are reciprocal at the same places.

### Paragraph 2 — theory already allows non-reciprocity

Dynamic island-biogeography theory already predicts hysteresis when colonization and extinction are constrained by different processes.

Allee-type seabird metapopulation models already predict delayed recolonization.

Therefore the theoretical possibility is not new.

### Paragraph 3 — seabird occupancy dynamics already show history and social information

Kittiwake studies already distinguish persistence, first colonization and recolonization and show effects of local density and conspecific reproductive information.

Buffer-effect work in guillemots already shows site-dependent occupancy across population increase, decline and recovery.

Therefore neither social site choice nor recolonization as a separate process is the novelty.

### Paragraph 4 — missing empirical test

What remains poorly tested is whether **the same breeding places show a reproducible loss–recovery asymmetry in the surrounding population states associated with their annual occupancy transitions**.

A direct test requires:

- repeated stable place identities;
- observed abandonment;
- later recolonization of the same place;
- an external population-state measure excluding the focal place;
- control for generic temporal alignment with the observed multivariate abundance trajectory.

### Paragraph 5 — Antarctic penguins provide the discovery

In Antarctic *Pygoscelis*:

- decline repeatedly concentrated reproduction beyond proportional thinning;
- independent Signy populations replicated the phenomenon;
- increasing regional networks did not necessarily restore effective breeding-site number.

These observations generate the prediction that recovery may not reverse collapse.

They do not constitute the confirmatory hysteresis test.

## Discussion if supported

### 1. Recovery is not the reverse movie of collapse

The principal result is an empirical asymmetry in the annual transition-state proxies associated with loss and later recovery of the same breeding places.

### 2. The result connects population dynamics to island history

Current population state is insufficient to reconstruct occupancy without knowing whether a breeding site is already occupied or has been abandoned.

### 3. Relation to dynamic island biogeography

The data provide a multi-species biological realization of a general theoretical prediction: colonization and extinction constraints can generate historical hysteresis.

### 4. Relation to coloniality

Social attraction and Allee-type feedback are plausible mechanisms because colonies themselves alter settlement payoff.

But mechanism is not identified.

### 5. Alternative mechanisms

Time-varying habitat deterioration, disturbance, predators, management and demographic composition can also contribute.

### 6. Conservation consequence

Numerical population recovery can overstate spatial recovery.

A population can return in number before it returns to previously used breeding space.

## Strongest allowed conclusion if supported

> **Across colonial seabirds, later recovery of the same breeding sites was associated with higher surrounding population states than their earlier loss, beyond structured temporal alignment with the observed MasterSite abundance trajectories. Population recovery therefore did not simply retrace the spatial pathway of collapse.**
