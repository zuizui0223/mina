# Integrated one-paper manuscript spine v1 — collapse and recovery thresholds

**Date:** 2026-10-05  
**Status:** prospective manuscript spine if and only if the frozen SMP hysteresis test passes both confirmatory gates.

## Working title

**Spatial recovery requires higher population thresholds than spatial collapse in colonial seabirds**

Alternative:

**Population recovery does not retrace spatial collapse in colonial seabirds**

Penguin-explicit alternative:

**Antarctic penguins reveal a spatial recovery barrier tested across colonial seabirds**

## One hypothesis

> **The population threshold for recolonization of an abandoned breeding site exceeds the threshold at which that same site was lost.**

For the same SiteID:

\[
H=A_{\mathrm{recolonize}}-A_{\mathrm{abandon}}>0.
\]

This is the only confirmatory hypothesis of the integrated paper.

## Discovery system — Antarctic penguins

The penguin analyses do not test \(H\) directly.

They establish the anomaly that motivates the hypothesis:

1. declining Palmer and Signy populations repeatedly concentrate beyond proportional thinning;
2. all three increasing regional MAPPPD networks end with lower effective breeding-site number;
3. therefore numerical recovery need not immediately reconstruct previous breeding-space organization.

These results are the **discovery half**, not one half of a pooled confirmatory test.

## Confirmatory system — SMP seabirds

The SMP analysis prospectively tests whether the same physical breeding sites are recovered at the same surrounding population state at which they were lost.

Stage A freezes stable place identity.

Stage B freezes explicit-zero abandonment/recolonization spells.

Stage C opens abundance once and calculates paired transition-state asymmetry.

Confirmatory support requires both:

1. positive species-balanced \(H\) under the species sign-flip test;
2. observed \(H\) exceeding the structured common-phase temporal null.

## If supported: central result

> **Across colonial seabirds, recolonization of abandoned breeding sites occurred at higher surrounding population states than the states at which those same sites were lost.**

This is the paper's empirical novelty.

## Island-ecology interpretation

The result implies that a breeding island or colony site has a state variable beyond current habitat and current regional abundance:

\[
\text{future occupancy}
=
f(\text{place},\text{population state},\text{occupancy history}).
\]

Extinction and recolonization are therefore not necessarily reciprocal transitions.

This is the link to dynamic island biogeography.

## Behavioral interpretation

Positive social feedback, colony familiarity, site fidelity, public information, or other biological memory can generate such non-reciprocity.

But these mechanisms are Discussion hypotheses only.

The paper must not state:

> Allee effects caused the hysteresis.

Instead:

> the observed asymmetry is consistent with processes that make an already occupied breeding site easier to retain than an equivalent empty site to re-establish.

## Relation to prior work

Not novel:

- colonization/extinction dynamics in seabirds;
- separating recolonization from first colonization;
- buffer effects;
- conspecific attraction;
- Allee effects;
- theoretical island-biogeographic hysteresis.

Candidate novelty:

> **prospective multi-species same-site paired measurement of collapse versus recovery population-state thresholds.**

## Discussion structure

1. Population recovery did not retrace spatial collapse.
2. Same-site design separates history from fixed place quality.
3. Relation to buffer effects and dynamic island biogeography.
4. Social/behavioral mechanisms remain unresolved.
5. Monitoring implication: abundance recovery can precede spatial recovery.
6. Conservation implication: restored population size need not imply restored colony network.

## Conservation statement

If supported:

> **Numerical population recovery can overstate spatial recovery because abandoned breeding sites may require a higher regional population state to be recolonized than the state at which they were lost.**

This is stronger and more useful than treating E alone as an early-warning indicator.
