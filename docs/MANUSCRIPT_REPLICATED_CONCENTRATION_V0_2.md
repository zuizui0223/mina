# Breeding-space contraction recurs across declining Adélie and chinstrap penguins

**Manuscript v0.2 — two-species replicated concentration synthesis**

## Abstract

Population decline is usually measured as loss of abundance, but colonial breeders can also change how the remaining population is distributed among breeding components. We asked whether declining penguin populations thin proportionally across established breeding units or instead contract onto a smaller effective set of units. In the Palmer Archipelago, West Antarctic Peninsula, effective colony number, N_eff = 1 / Σ p_j², declined by 19%, 51% and 83% in three Adélie penguin (*Pygoscelis adeliae*) island populations with unchanged census-unit rosters. Fixed-composition nulls preserved each observed population-total trajectory while holding relative breeding composition constant and adding Poisson or Gamma–Poisson count error; all three observed concentration trends remained more negative than expected under every frozen error model. We then tested the same endpoint prospectively in independent Signy Island records, South Orkney Islands. In a frozen 22-season Adélie panel, breeding pairs declined from 2,342 to 1,217 while N_eff declined from 3.08 to 1.94 (−37.2%; slope −0.044 yr⁻¹); no 20%-CV null replicate among 100,000 was as negative (plus-one p = 0.000010). A separately frozen cross-species test in chinstrap penguins (*P. antarcticus*) also supported concentration: breeding pairs declined from 1,642 to 581 and N_eff from 4.44 to 2.19 (−50.6%; slope −0.072 yr⁻¹); the 20%-CV test gave p = 0.000020. Post-hoc descriptive decomposition showed that the shared population-level outcome arose through different internal routes: the initially dominant breeding unit disappeared in all three Palmer populations, whereas the initially dominant Signy unit remained dominant and increased its share in both species. Independent Torgersen mapping similarly documents loss of historical subcolony footprints while retained footprints can fragment internally. Thus breeding-space contraction recurs across two *Pygoscelis* species and two Antarctic systems, but the identity of the breeding components that retain dominance is system-specific.

**Keywords:** Adélie penguin; chinstrap penguin; colonial breeding; population decline; spatial reorganization; breeding-space contraction; subcolonies; Antarctica

## Introduction

Population decline has a spatial form as well as a magnitude. Two populations can lose the same number of breeders while following different spatial trajectories. If breeding units decline in constant proportion, the population simply becomes thinner while preserving its relative distribution. Alternatively, decline can change the relative distribution itself, concentrating the remaining breeders into a smaller effective set of breeding units. Those trajectories are not interchangeable: they differ in how much breeding space remains occupied, where reproduction persists, and how local habitat and social geometry can affect the remnant population.

Colonial seabirds are particularly suited to this problem because their population structure is spatially nested. Individuals occupy nests; nests form aggregations; aggregations form subcolonies or monitoring units within larger breeding sites. In Adélie penguins, subcolony-scale nesting habitat and perimeter-to-area geometry are associated with reproductive success [@schmidt2021]. Mechanistic theory predicts that declining abundance can fragment nest aggregations at fine spatial scales through site fidelity, self-organization and edge-biased predation [@mcdowall2019]. At Torgersen Island, long-term spatial reconstruction shows that historical subcolonies have also disappeared non-randomly with respect to snow and terrain, while some surviving historical footprints contain smaller fragments [@cimino2025].

These results establish that breeding geometry matters, but they leave a distinct longitudinal question: **does a declining population lose breeding effort approximately in proportion across established colony components, or does the composition of breeding space itself change?** Merely observing that small colonies disappear first cannot answer this question. When total abundance falls, small components reach zero sooner even if their expected share is perfectly constant. A test for spatial reorganization therefore needs to preserve the observed abundance trajectory while asking whether the relative breeding distribution changes more than expected under proportional thinning and counting noise.

The Palmer Archipelago provides a discovery system. Five neighbouring Adélie island populations underwent a highly coherent long-term decline, but only Cormorant, Humble and Litchfield retained unchanged reported colony-code rosters over the synchronized census. In all three, effective colony number declined more rapidly than expected under a fixed-composition count-error null. This established repeated concentration within one regional system, but not geographic or taxonomic transferability.

Signy Island provides both. Long-term monitoring has documented declines of Adélie and chinstrap penguins and loss of monitored colonies [@dunn2016]. Before calculating concentration endpoints, we froze stable-roster panels and the same effective-number statistic, proportional-thinning null and error family used to interpret Palmer. Signy Adélie therefore tests whether the Palmer phenomenon transfers to a different Antarctic region, while Signy chinstrap provides a separately frozen cross-species test.

Our primary question is whether **population decline repeatedly reduces the effective number of breeding components beyond proportional thinning**. We then use a bounded post-hoc description to ask how concentration was assembled internally: did initially dominant units persist and absorb an increasing share, or did dominance itself turn over? This descriptive step has no inferential p-values and cannot alter the primary concentration decisions. Finally, published Torgersen mapping is used as physical spatial triangulation rather than as a merged response data set.

## Materials and Methods

### Palmer Adélie discovery populations

We used the public Palmer Station Antarctica Long Term Ecological Research Adélie breeding-pair census (DOI: 10.6073/pasta/89dd52217ca37e3a72a67f7a9bc3c82e). The synchronized island panel spans 1991–2017. Because changes in the set of census units can themselves create apparent changes in diversity or concentration, the primary within-island analysis was restricted to Cormorant, Humble and Litchfield, the three islands with unchanged reported colony-code rosters. Litchfield contributed through its final positive island census in 2006.

For each island-year, breeding pairs were summed over colony-code units. If n_j is the breeding-pair count in component j, N = Σ n_j, and p_j = n_j/N, effective colony number was

N_eff = 1 / Σ p_j².

The observed statistic was the ordinary-least-squares slope of N_eff against calendar year. Negative slopes indicate increasing concentration.

### Signy Adélie independent replication

The first external test used the NERC EDS UK Polar Data Centre data set *Population size and breeding success of Adelie penguins on Signy Island from 1978 to 2020* (DOI: 10.5285/daf2c4fd-c1e3-4e65-851f-d11f02c5b69d). Before any Signy N_eff value, abundance slope or concentration result was inspected, an outcome-blind support audit identified five canonical breeding units with numeric pair counts in 22 complete standardized seasons from 1996–2019; 1997 and 2010 were excluded because the relevant counts were not numerically complete.

The frozen units were A1+A60, A2, A3, A4 and A64. Literal A1, A60, A1 + A60 and A1+A60 labels were harmonized into the single canonical A1+A60 unit because the source changed from separate to pooled reporting. Constituent rows were summed only within seasons that passed the frozen completeness rule. No alternative roster or season set could be introduced after effect inspection.

A later, independently specified stricter-literal-roster analysis restricted the data to 1998–2009 and eight literal units. Because that analysis was frozen later, it is retained only as a robustness check rather than replacing the earlier prospective 22-season endpoint.

### Signy chinstrap cross-species replication

The cross-species test used the corresponding Signy chinstrap data set (DOI: 10.5285/d0633a9b-8c56-4ae5-88bf-6ec6ba9017b9). The test was frozen after the Palmer discovery and the Signy Adélie replication but before any chinstrap concentration effect was computed.

An outcome-blind support audit selected nine literal colony units—C15, C16, C17, C18, C46, C47, C79, C80 and C81—that spanned the monitoring window and were jointly complete in the same 22 primary seasons used above. The decline-eligibility gate required the summed breeding-pair abundance of these nine units to have a negative temporal slope. Conditional on that gate, the primary prediction was a negative effective-breeding-patch-number slope that remained more negative than expected under all frozen count-error nulls.

### Proportional-thinning null

For every population, we estimated one time-invariant vector of breeding-component shares from cumulative counts across the complete frozen panel. In each observed year, those shares were multiplied by the empirical population total. The latent null therefore retained the exact magnitude and temporal trajectory of population decline while removing directional redistribution among breeding components.

Counts were generated under three prespecified observation models: Poisson sampling, Gamma–Poisson sampling with 10% multiplicative CV and Gamma–Poisson sampling with 20% multiplicative CV. The 20% model is deliberately severe and is not presented as an empirical estimate of observer error. Positive observed population-years were conditioned on a nonzero simulated total.

For each error model we generated 100,000 realizations and calculated the N_eff slope. One-sided probabilities used the plus-one estimator,

p = (1 + number of null slopes ≤ the observed slope) / 100001.

Support required a negative observed slope and p ≤ 0.05 under all three frozen error models. No alternative concentration statistic, pair-count threshold, error CV, roster or time window could rescue a failed test.

### Post-hoc description of internal pathways

After all five concentration results were known, we froze a non-inferential descriptive decomposition. For each population we recorded (i) the identity and share of the largest breeding unit in the first eligible season and its share in the final season; (ii) the final dominant unit and its initial rank; and (iii) each unit's first-season share and OLS slope of log1p breeding-pair count against actual year. Within each population we report the Spearman rank correlation between initial share and the component-level log1p-count slope as a descriptive coefficient only, without a p-value or confidence interval.

This step tests no new hypothesis and cannot change the primary concentration classification. Its role is to prevent an unwarranted inference that falling N_eff necessarily means that the largest initial colony units are preferentially retained.

### Physical spatial triangulation

We used the published Torgersen Island reconstruction of Cimino et al. [@cimino2025] as independent physical evidence. That study reconstructed historical and current mapped subcolony footprints and related persistence to long-term snow and terrain. Because Palmer LTER colony codes are not publicly crosswalked one-to-one to those polygons, this layer is used only to establish that real breeding-space loss and internal fragmentation occur at physical spatial scales.

## Results

### Palmer Adélie populations concentrated beyond proportional thinning

Effective colony number fell on all three eligible Palmer islands. Cormorant declined from 3.535 to 2.859 (−19.1%; slope −0.03098 yr⁻¹); Humble from 4.625 to 2.285 (−50.6%; slope −0.08550 yr⁻¹); and Litchfield from 5.783 to 1.000 by its final positive census (−82.7%; slope −0.36808 yr⁻¹).

The trends remained unusual under the complete fixed error family. Under Gamma–Poisson CV20%, one-sided probabilities were p = 0.0380 for Cormorant and p = 0.000010 for both Humble and Litchfield; no simulation produced all three slopes simultaneously as negative as observed (joint p = 0.000010). Thus the decline could not be reduced to a fixed breeding composition becoming noisier as abundance fell.

### Signy Adélie independently replicated breeding-space contraction

In the prospectively frozen 22-season Signy Adélie panel, stable-roster breeding pairs declined from 2,342 in 1996 to 1,217 in 2019. Effective colony number declined from 3.081 to 1.936, a 37.2% reduction, with a slope of −0.04396 yr⁻¹.

The fixed-composition null was centred near zero slope. None of 100,000 Poisson, CV10% or CV20% realizations was at least as negative as observed; plus-one p = 0.000010 under each error model. Under CV20%, the 95% null interval was approximately −0.0176 to 0.0174 yr⁻¹, far inside the observed −0.044 slope.

The later strict literal-roster robustness analysis reached the same conclusion over 1998–2009: breeding pairs declined from 2,688 to 901 and N_eff from 3.61 to 2.54 (−29.7%), with p = 0.000130 even under CV20%. Thus the external replication was not dependent on the longer-panel canonicalization rule.

### Signy chinstrap penguins provided a prospective cross-species replication

The nine-unit chinstrap panel also passed its decline gate: breeding pairs declined from 1,642 in 1996 to 581 in 2019 (−64.6%). Effective breeding-patch number fell from 4.438 to 2.191 (−50.6%), with a slope of −0.07179 yr⁻¹.

The cross-species prediction was supported under all frozen nulls. No Poisson or CV10% replicate among 100,000 was as negative as observed (plus-one p = 0.000010 for each). Under CV20%, one of 100,000 null slopes was at least as negative, giving p = 0.000020. Breeding-space contraction therefore transferred not only across regions but from Adélie to chinstrap penguins.

### The same population-level contraction arose through different internal routes

The post-hoc descriptive decomposition rejected a simple universal narrative that initially large groups were always the refugia of decline.

At Palmer, the initially dominant colony-code unit disappeared by the final eligible census on all three islands. On Cormorant, the unit containing 45.4% of pairs in 1991 ended at zero; the final dominant unit had ranked fourth initially and increased from 11.6% to 43.5% of the remnant population. On Humble, the initial dominant held 33.6% and later disappeared, while the initially second-ranked unit rose from 26.6% to 58.8%. On Litchfield, the initial dominant (26.2%) disappeared, whereas the initially second-ranked unit accounted for all four remaining pairs in the final positive census. Descriptive correlations between initial share and subsequent log1p-count slope were negative on all three islands (rho = −0.64, −0.30 and −0.62).

Signy showed the opposite route. In Adélie penguins, A1+A60 remained dominant and increased from 47.5% to 69.4% of the five-unit population; the descriptive initial-share/slope rho was +0.90. In chinstrap penguins, C80 remained dominant and increased from 40.0% to 65.6%, with rho = +0.38. Thus Signy concentration was dominated by persistence of an initial core, whereas Palmer concentration involved turnover in which breeding component dominated.

### Torgersen mapping confirms hierarchical spatial reorganization

Independent mapping at Torgersen documented 23 historical active subcolony footprints but only five active footprints by 2022 [@cimino2025]. All historical south-aspect subcolonies were extinct. Larger subcolonies generally went extinct later, but size did not provide a universal protection: on south-facing terrain, larger subcolonies declined faster than smaller ones. The mapped evidence therefore illustrates the same scale distinction as the census synthesis: decline can remove whole breeding components at a coarse level while reorganizing or fragmenting the nests within retained components at a finer level.

## Discussion

### Breeding-space contraction recurred across regions and species

The central result is now broader than a Palmer-specific Adélie pattern. Five declining population units—three Palmer Adélie populations, Signy Adélie and Signy chinstrap—showed directional loss of effective breeding-component number beyond the concentration generated by their observed decline trajectories under a fixed latent composition and the same family of count-error models. The two Signy tests were frozen before the relevant concentration effects were computed, providing both an independent-system replication and a prospective cross-species extension.

Earlier studies had already established population decline, colony loss and spatial fragmentation in these systems [@dunn2016; @mcdowall2019; @cimino2025]. The added result here is distributional: **decline does not simply scale down a fixed breeding map**. The relative allocation of breeding effort among monitored components changes systematically enough to exceed the proportional-thinning expectation.

This distinction matters because abundance and spatial organization are not interchangeable state variables. Two populations with the same number of pairs can distribute reproduction across very different numbers of colony components. A reduction in N_eff therefore represents a second axis of population contraction: fewer effective places are carrying an increasing fraction of the remaining reproduction.

### Convergent contraction emerged through divergent internal routes

The post-hoc component decomposition provides a useful boundary on mechanism. At Signy, concentration looked like **core retention**: the initially dominant breeding unit remained dominant in both species and increased its share substantially. This pattern is compatible with a refuge-like interpretation in which some established breeding areas retain breeders more effectively than peripheral components.

Palmer did not follow that route. The initially dominant component disappeared in every eligible island, and the identity of the dominant remnant shifted. Concentration there was therefore **dominance turnover**, not simple retreat into the historically largest group. This result is especially important because falling effective colony number by itself cannot distinguish these pathways.

The contrast argues against treating initial colony size as a universal mechanism. Torgersen mapping reaches a similar conclusion from physical subcolonies: larger subcolonies tended to persist longer overall, yet on south-facing terrain larger subcolonies could decline faster [@cimino2025]. Local habitat, snow, geometry, history and social processes can therefore modify which components become refugia. The reproducible feature is the contraction of breeding-space organization; the identity of the winning component is contingent.

We treat the Palmer-versus-Signy route contrast as descriptive rather than inferential. Initial-share correlations are based on few breeding components and are vulnerable to starting-year idiosyncrasy and regression-to-the-mean effects. Their value is not to establish a new size-dependent law, but to show that the primary replicated result does not require one.

### Coarse contraction and fine fragmentation are complementary, not contradictory

The term contraction might appear inconsistent with the fragmentation predicted by Adélie spatial models [@mcdowall2019]. The difference is spatial level. Our effective-number statistic measures the distribution of breeders **among established monitoring components**. McDowall and Lynch model how individual nests and aggregations fragment **within breeding space** as abundance falls.

Torgersen makes the hierarchy visible in one real system. Many historical subcolony footprints have disappeared, while some surviving footprints now contain multiple smaller fragments [@cimino2025]. A declining population can therefore occupy fewer coarse breeding components while becoming more fragmented inside the components that remain. There is no requirement that aggregation change have the same sign at every spatial grain.

This scale dependence also offers a route for future synthesis with remote-sensing data. Satellite or drone products can measure occupied area and fragmentation, while long-term ground censuses retain the allocation of breeding pairs among named components. Combining them would allow direct estimation of whether component loss, within-component fragmentation and local habitat quality occur in a consistent sequence during decline.

### The replicated outcome does not identify one behavioural mechanism

Several processes could generate breeding-space contraction: persistent differences in snow and drainage, nest-site fidelity, changes in breeding participation, differential survival, recruitment, local movement, predation geometry or density dependence. Aggregate breeding-pair counts cannot separate these mechanisms.

A separate Palmer analysis found a short-lag association between relative reproductive performance and later within-island redistribution, but its frozen Signy replication was unsupported. We therefore do not use public information, win-stay/lose-switch or breeding dispersal to explain the replicated concentration result. Direct movement mechanisms require individual mark–resight or tracking data; the public Palmer sources examined so far do not provide a qualifying individual breeding-site transition table.

The strength of the present inference is consequently at the population-organization level: different individual and habitat processes can converge on the same observable outcome of reproduction being carried by fewer effective breeding components.

### Implications for monitoring declining colonial populations

Population totals remain indispensable, but they erase internal spatial change. Signy illustrates this clearly. Adélie breeding pairs fell by roughly half across the frozen panel while effective breeding components declined by more than one-third; chinstrap pairs fell by almost two-thirds while effective breeding components halved. At Palmer the contraction was even stronger in some populations, culminating in one effective breeding component immediately before Litchfield disappeared locally.

Component-resolved monitoring can therefore detect loss of spatial organization that cannot be reconstructed from total counts alone. This matters for conservation because the remaining spatial distribution determines which breeding surfaces, local environmental exposures and colony geometries continue to support reproduction. We do not show that maintaining more components improves population growth, but losing components changes the ecological state of a colony even when abundance is held fixed conceptually.

### Limitations and next tests

The primary synthesis contains five population units, but three Palmer populations share one regional environment and the two Signy species share one island. The cross-species result is therefore a meaningful extension, not a claim that all penguins or all colonies contract this way. Gentoo penguins provide an especially valuable future contrast because many Antarctic populations have increased rather than declined, but available Signy public data aggregate the relevant breeding distribution differently and were not opened as a post-result rescue comparison here.

Monitoring units are also not standardized habitat polygons. Palmer colony codes have no public one-to-one GIS crosswalk, Signy Adélie requires a frozen canonical A1+A60 unit, and component sizes differ physically. N_eff should therefore be read as the effective number of monitored breeding components rather than effective occupied area.

Finally, the count-error distributions are stylized sensitivities rather than empirically calibrated error models. Their purpose is to show that abundance-dependent stochasticity under fixed composition does not readily generate the observed trends. Replicate observer counts or spatially explicit detection models would tighten that boundary.

The most informative next study would link component-level census histories to mapped nesting polygons and individual movement. That design could test whether contraction preferentially removes specific habitat classes, whether dominant units change through movement or differential demographic performance, and whether the transition from coarse component loss to fine-scale fragmentation predicts subsequent population collapse.

## Conclusion

Declining penguin populations can lose not only breeders but breeding-space organization. Across three Palmer Adélie populations, a prospectively tested Signy Adélie population and a separately frozen Signy chinstrap population, effective breeding-component number declined beyond the expectation from proportional thinning under the observed abundance trajectory and frozen count-error models. The result therefore transfers across two Antarctic systems and two *Pygoscelis* species.

Yet the internal route was not universal. Signy populations concentrated by retaining their initially dominant breeding unit, whereas all three Palmer populations changed dominant unit and lost the component that had been largest initially. The general ecological result is thus **breeding-space contraction**, not a universal large-colony refuge. Together with mapped Torgersen subcolony loss and within-footprint fragmentation, the data indicate that population decline reorganizes colonial breeding space hierarchically: fewer coarse breeding components carry reproduction even as fine-scale spatial structure within those components can follow different trajectories.
