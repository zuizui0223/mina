# GEB cover-letter interest paragraph v0.5

Ecologists routinely infer broad-scale process from spatial descriptors, yet rarely test whether ecological information measured within a population remains informative after it is aggregated into landscape variables. We address that transfer problem using Antarctic penguins across nested spatial scales. At Palmer Station, Adélie penguin decline involved both within-island concentration and a short-lived late-season colony state that predicted subsequent redistribution after shared annual change, prior abundance and persistent colony identity were controlled. Crucially, an independently monitored nest-level reproductive metric did not reproduce the association, showing that the informative local state cannot be generalized as ordinary productivity. We then tested whether static breeding-space amount and habitat heterogeneity captured a transferable demographic rule across 104 Adélie, chinstrap and gentoo site × species units throughout Antarctica. Although the focal interaction was directionally concordant across species, the preregistered cross-species test was non-confirmatory and sensitive to spatial support. The contribution is therefore not a new public-information mechanism or a claim that habitat is irrelevant. Rather, the study establishes an empirical boundary on macroecological generalization: local demographic history can be measurable and ecologically meaningful without being recoverable as a confirmed scale-invariant rule in static landscape descriptors. We believe this distinction among local explanatory structure, dynamic historical information and transferable macroecological structure is broadly relevant to GEB readers.
