# Comparative synthesis: population recovery is not the reverse of spatial collapse

**Status:** post-result synthesis integrating frozen decline results, frozen Ross recovery, corrected Beaufort interpretation, and post-result Heard Island triangulation.

## Central question

Does numerical population recovery retrace the spatial pathway of population collapse?

The current evidence says no.

## Four empirical cases

| system | demographic direction | local-unit arithmetic | spatial redundancy | dominance identity | interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Palmer/Signy Adélie declines | N down | all 39 frozen components decline first-to-last; gains tiny relative to losses | concentration | survivors retain/gain relative share | differential attrition |
| Ross Island Adélie recovery | N up 3.70x | 3/3 colonies and 6/6 components increase | frozen endpoints E3 -10.7%, E6 -14.0%; most shift occurs 2001-2002 | Crozier remains dominant | early persistent-dominance amplification; no clear phase-long E trend |
| Heard Island King recovery | N up at every observed interval | North and South both increase at every observed interval | E non-monotonic; 1963-1988 endpoint down | North -> South | dominance-reversal amplification |
| Beaufort Adélie recovery | N up 34.3% | new subcolony grows 108%, main 33.6% | E slightly up | main remains dominant | weak spatial spreading after capacity release |

## Result 1 — decline concentration is attrition, not aggregation

Across the frozen Palmer/Signy decline components:

- 0/39 increased from first to last retained positive season;
- during joint N-down/E-down transitions, gross gains were about 153 versus gross losses about 7,034.

Thus concentration is generated by unequal disappearance/shrinkage rather than movement into a growing refuge.

## Result 2 — recovery concentration can occur without local decline

Ross Island 2001-2012:

- total N: 94,798 -> 351,119;
- Royds: +1,716;
- Bird: +49,379;
- Crozier: +205,226;
- E3: 1.729 -> 1.543.

At six-component resolution every component also increased.

Thus the frozen recovery interval begins with concentration generated by unequal positive growth. A post-result temporal audit shows that most of the E shift occurs in 2001-2002 and that E is broadly flat/fluctuating thereafter; this is not evidence for steady phase-long erosion.

## Result 3 — recovery concentration does not require persistence of the original dominant

Heard Island Spit Bay:

- 1963 North/South: 13/5;
- 1965: 36/9;
- 1969: 49/37;
- 1980: 82/400;
- 1988: 215/3,100.

Both subcolonies increased continuously across observed intervals.

But spatial redundancy followed:

    1.670 -> 1.471 -> 1.962 -> 1.393 -> 1.138.

The system first approached equality and then reconcentrated around a different dominant colony.

## Result 4 — recovery can also spread

Beaufort 2004-2010:

- main colony: +33.6%;
- new subcolony: +108.0%;
- new-subcolony share: 0.95% -> 1.48%;
- E: 1.019 -> 1.030;
- observed new-subcolony gain: ~3.15x proportional expectation.

Published individual resightings also indicate reduced export to Ross Island after nesting habitat availability increased.

This case prevents the paper from claiming that recovery generally concentrates.

## Exact unifying arithmetic

For instantaneous local growth rates:

    d log(E)/dt = 2(r_bar - r_D).

The sign of total growth does not determine the sign of spatial-redundancy change.

Over finite intervals:

    E1/E0 = (G_bar/G_D)^2.

But endpoint E does not reconstruct the path because dominance can reverse.

## Spatial collapse and recovery are not inverses

A naive inverse-path expectation would be:

    decline:
    nodes disappear -> E down

    recovery:
    nodes regrow -> E up.

The observations show at least three recovery paths instead:

    A. persistent amplification:
       all nodes grow, dominant grows fastest -> E down

    B. dominance reversal:
       subordinate catches up, overtakes, becomes dominant -> E may rise then fall

    C. spreading recovery:
       low-share/new nodes gain disproportionate growth -> E up.

Thus recovery has its own spatial dynamics.

## Relation to metapopulation theory

Existing spatial recovery theory already separates aggregate recovery from patch occupancy and local collapse.

The empirical addition here is an **abundance-distribution dimension among occupied patches**.

Even when:
- all monitored nodes persist;
- all monitored nodes increase;

the recovering system can lose effective spatial redundancy.

And even when endpoint N and E have the same signs, the identity of the dominant node can either persist or turn over.

## Conservation implication

A recovery assessment based only on total abundance can miss increasing dependence on one breeding node.

A recovery assessment based on occupancy can also miss it if every node remains occupied.

At minimum, monitoring should distinguish:

1. total abundance;
2. occupancy/persistence;
3. abundance-weighted spatial redundancy;
4. compositional/dominance turnover.

## Island-ecology implication

The island contribution is not that islands simply impose carrying capacities.

Colonial island populations are internally structured networks of breeding nodes.

During recovery, dynamic breeding capacity and local demographic return can redistribute abundance:
- within nodes;
- among nodes on the same island;
- across island boundaries.

The spatial path depends on where demographic opportunity lies relative to the current abundance distribution.

## Strongest general claim

> **Population recovery is not the spatial inverse of population collapse: concentration can be generated by unequal losses during decline or by unequal gains during recovery, and recovery can reorganize which breeding node is dominant even when every monitored node grows.**

## Claim boundary

- Ross is the frozen primary recovery test.
- Heard is independent species/island triangulation but was found post-result.
- Beaufort is a contrasting mechanistic case, not a replication.
- No universal predictor of recovery path has yet been prospectively established.
