# Regional decline, local collapse: breeding-colony network structure predicts demographic resilience across Antarctic penguin islands

## Abstract

Island populations can experience the same regional environment yet diverge sharply in local persistence because the processes that supply resources need not operate at the same spatial scale as the processes that constrain reproduction. Antarctic penguins provide an unusually clear case: they forage in a shared marine system but breed on discrete terrestrial patches. We used public Palmer Archipelago data to ask how regional and local structure combine to shape penguin island dynamics. An exploratory reanalysis of Palmer Penguins morphology first showed that apparent morphology–island association was dominated by species composition rather than a stable within-species island phenotype. We then used Antarctic Penguin Biogeography Project records to define local assembly endpoints and a complete Palmer LTER census of five Adélie penguin islands from 1991–2017 to quantify demographic synchrony. The five standardized log-abundance trajectories shared an exceptionally strong long-term component (PC1 = 96.4%), but annual growth was only moderately synchronized (median pairwise correlation = 0.373), and endpoints ranged from persistence at low abundance to local extinction at Litchfield Island. Prospectively bounded mechanism tests did not support a positive annual sea-ice-duration effect, a 3–7-year sea-ice-duration timescale rescue, or an October snowfall × snow-prone-habitat interaction. In contrast, effective colony number—an index of how breeders were distributed among within-island subcolonies—provided a small positive increment in held-out-year prediction of next-year island growth beyond current abundance, island identity, and secular time. This signal persisted after demographic momentum was added and strengthened when locally extinct Litchfield was excluded. It was not reproduced by simple active-colony count, and an externally specified >50-pair “large breeding group” hypothesis predicted in the opposite direction to its historical expectation. These results separate two ecological scales: regional processes impose a common long-term direction, whereas local vulnerability is expressed in the organization of terrestrial breeding networks. For marine-foraging island consumers, island ecology may therefore depend less on local food production than on the capacity of discrete breeding landscapes to maintain a distributed network of reproductive sites.

## Introduction

Island ecology is usually framed around spatial isolation, local resource supply, colonization, extinction, and interactions among organisms whose essential resources occur on the island itself. Highly mobile marine consumers violate part of that template. Seabirds can obtain almost all trophic resources from the surrounding ocean while remaining obligately tied to discrete terrestrial patches for reproduction. In such systems, regional environmental forcing and local island constraints are physically separated: marine conditions can affect populations across many breeding sites simultaneously, while snow, geomorphology, nesting space, and colony organization can determine whether a particular breeding patch persists. This separation makes marine-breeding islands useful natural experiments for distinguishing shared regional dynamics from local demographic vulnerability.

The Palmer Archipelago on the western Antarctic Peninsula is an especially informative system. Adélie penguins (Pygoscelis adeliae) have declined strongly around Palmer Station while gentoo penguins (P. papua) have expanded locally, and the three Pygoscelis species differ in their associations with sea ice and breeding environments. Yet coexistence and population change cannot be reduced to a single interaction axis. Adélie and gentoo penguins near Palmer overlap strongly in diet but partition core foraging space, and previous work did not find evidence that breeding-season prey competition explained their contrasting population trajectories (Pickett et al. 2018). Conversely, terrestrial breeding conditions can matter strongly: island geomorphology shapes snow accumulation, breeding phenology, and habitat quality, with documented demographic differences among neighboring islands exposed to broadly similar marine conditions (Fraser et al. 2013; Cimino et al. 2019). Penguins also transport marine nutrients onto land, making them ecosystem engineers as well as island occupants. The system is therefore best viewed as an externally subsidized breeding-island network rather than a conventional terrestrial food web.

A central inference problem follows from this structure. Strongly parallel long-term population trajectories across nearby islands can reveal a shared regional component without identifying the environmental mechanism that generated it. At the same time, persistent differences among islands can indicate local filtering without establishing whether the relevant process is snow, geomorphology, competition, colony history, or another state variable. These scales can be conflated if a common decline is attributed directly to a convenient climate index, or if local population size is treated as a sufficient description of breeding-habitat state. Distinguishing the scales requires synchronized island time series, prospectively specified mechanism tests, and explicit local-state metrics.

Here we developed a multi-scale analysis of Palmer penguin islands. We first used public Palmer Penguins morphology and isotope data as an exploratory entry point to ask whether apparent island phenotypes persist within species across years. We then used Antarctic Penguin Biogeography Project (APBP/MAPPPD) records to define a local Palmer breeding network and to contrast vacancy, persistence, and species replacement. Our primary analysis used the complete 1991–2017 Palmer LTER Adélie census for five neighboring islands to quantify long-term synchrony, annual synchrony, and island-specific decline. After freezing that result, we prospectively tested finite mechanism hypotheses involving preceding sea-ice duration, 3–7-year sea-ice duration, and annual snowfall × published snow-prone habitat. Finally, after those simple environmental formulations failed, we tested whether within-island breeding-colony organization predicted next-year demographic performance. We predicted that a more distributed breeding network, measured by effective colony number, would be associated with higher next-year growth after current abundance and secular time were controlled. We separately evaluated an externally specified historical hypothesis that retention of breeding groups larger than 50 pairs should improve demographic performance. This staged design allowed us to ask not merely whether Palmer populations declined, but at what ecological scale useful information about that decline resides.

## Methods

### Study system and evidence architecture

We treated the Palmer region as a hierarchy of marine-foraging consumers using discrete breeding patches. Analyses were separated into four roles: (1) exploratory phenotype evidence motivating species-turnover versus within-species differentiation; (2) local-network assembly endpoints from APBP/MAPPPD; (3) synchronized Adélie population dynamics from Palmer LTER; and (4) prospectively frozen mechanism and local-state tests. Each confirmatory or directional test was committed as a machine-readable contract before the corresponding outcome was opened, and terminal decisions were saved as result receipts. No mechanism family was retuned after a negative result.

### Exploratory Palmer Penguins phenotype analysis

We used the raw Palmer Penguins dataset associated with Gorman et al. (2014), pinned to commit 8957207b78d6ccd1b4654a9dd9c9041b657478ab. The dataset contains three Pygoscelis species sampled at Biscoe, Dream, and Torgersen breeding sites during 2007/08–2009/10, with bill dimensions, flipper length, body mass, sex, and blood stable isotopes. The original ODSP audit showed that morphology predicted island relative to a pooled island marginal, but the gain was dominated by species identity. In mina, we therefore treated this only as motivation and restricted within-species analysis to Adélie penguins, the species represented at all three sites.

For each trait, we compared models controlling sex, year, egg date, and clutch completion with models adding island and island × year terms. We also assessed whether island identity could transfer across years using leave-one-year-out nearest-centroid classification after training-fold sex residualization and scaling. These analyses were explicitly exploratory and were not used to infer local adaptation or individual plasticity.

### Palmer local breeding network and assembly endpoints

We used APBP/MAPPPD records (Che-Castaldo et al. 2023) to inventory breeding sites within 20 km of Palmer Station before opening abundance trajectories. Six discrete island sites were frozen as the primary local network: Torgersen, Litchfield, Humble, Christine, Cormorant, and Dream. Biscoe Point, a breeding site on Anvers Island, was retained separately as a non-island benchmark. The distinction matters because substring matching of “Biscoe” can otherwise admit unrelated Antarctic sites and because Biscoe Point should not be treated as a replicate small island.

For abundance summaries, numeric nest/breeding-pair counts were used independently by species; repeated site × species × year counts were summarized by their median and explicit absences could contribute zero. For community composition, every focal species known by APBP to breed at a site had to have a same-year nest count or explicit absence; non-breeders were structural zeros. These rules prevented unobserved species from being silently converted to zero.

### Five-island Adélie census and scale decomposition

The primary demographic dataset was the Palmer LTER “Adelie penguin area-wide breeding population census, 1991, present” (DOI 10.6073/pasta/89dd52217ca37e3a72a67f7a9bc3c82e). We used five islands with a complete synchronized record from 1991–2017: Christine (CHR), Cormorant (COR), Humble (HUM), Litchfield (LIT), and Torgersen (TOR). Colony-level breeding-pair counts were summed within island and year without interpolation.

We characterized common long-term structure using principal component analysis of island-standardized log1p abundance. Short-term synchrony was quantified as pairwise Pearson correlation of annual log1p growth, using adjacent calendar years only. To separate persistent spatial and temporal structure, we compared additive models of log1p abundance and calculated unique year, unique island, and residual fractions from sums of squares. We also reported island-specific endpoints and annual linear multiplicative trends. These quantities describe scale structure; the common temporal component was not assumed to be a causal sea-ice effect.

### Prospectively bounded environmental mechanism tests

After the synchrony result was frozen, we tested a finite set of mechanism hypotheses. First, annual island growth from census year t-1 to t was modeled against PAL DSR Grid sea-ice season duration for the completed preceding ice season. A baseline model contained island fixed effects; the regional model added standardized sea-ice duration, and a secondary model added a sea-ice × published suboptimal-habitat interaction using the Fraser et al. (2013) island habitat percentages. Predictive performance was evaluated by leave-one-calendar-year-out validation, holding all five islands out together. Support required both a positive held-out MSE improvement and a coefficient in the predeclared positive direction.

Because annual sea-ice duration failed the directional test, a separate pre-outcome contract tested whether a low-frequency relationship had been obscured by annual noise. Three-, five-, and seven-year trailing sea-ice-duration summaries were evaluated against explicit temporal baselines with overlap-purged validation. The five-year window was primary; three- and seven-year windows were fixed robustness checks. No additional smoothing windows were searched after the result.

A terrestrial weather lane independently tested whether October snowfall immediately before breeding interacted with published snow-prone habitat to explain island-specific deviations from the regional annual-growth signal. The predictor was the number of October days with numeric Precipitation Snow (cm) > 0 in the Palmer LTER daily weather record. The predeclared interaction direction was negative: snowfall should be more detrimental on islands with a higher percentage of suboptimal snow-retaining habitat. Prediction was again evaluated by held-out calendar year. No additional months were searched after the result.

### Within-island colony-network structure

After the environmental formulations above failed, we opened a biologically distinct local-state hypothesis based only on colony structure already present in the synchronized census. For each island-year with positive total abundance, colony-code counts were converted to shares p_j. We defined effective colony number as

N_eff = 1 / sum_j(p_j^2),

which increases when breeders are distributed more evenly among multiple subcolonies and decreases when breeding becomes concentrated.

The response was next-year island growth, log(1+N_t+1)-log(1+N_t). The baseline model contained island fixed effects, standardized current log1p abundance, and standardized year. The full model added standardized log1p effective colony number. All islands ending in the same calendar year were held out together. Support required both positive held-out MSE improvement and a positive full-data coefficient. Fixed sensitivities substituted active positive-colony count, added previous-year growth, and restricted the analysis to the four islands that remained extant.

### External >50-pair breeding-group validation

The positive effective-colony-number result was followed by a specificity test using an independently defined historical hypothesis. A Palmer LTER annual report described loss of “large” breeding groups, defined as groups exceeding 50 breeding pairs, as a candidate explanation for island differences. We therefore fixed 50 pairs without threshold search. The primary predictor was the log1p count of >50-pair colony groups at year t; a fixed sensitivity used the fraction of island breeders residing in such groups. Baseline covariates and leave-one-end-year-out validation matched the colony-network analysis. The historical directional prediction was positive.

## Results

### Apparent island phenotype was primarily compositional

In the exploratory Palmer Penguins analysis, morphology predicted island against a pooled reference (mean log-score gain 0.389), but species identity alone carried a larger gain (0.536), while the species-conditioned morphology increment was negative (-0.084). Within Adélie penguins, structural morphology classified held-out-year island identity with balanced accuracy 0.342 against a three-island reference of 0.333; isotopes gave 0.340. Pairwise island trait rankings frequently reversed among the three years. Thus the motivating island phenotype was not a stable within-species island signature.

### Local assembly endpoints were heterogeneous

APBP/MAPPPD records showed strong but nonuniform long-term change among Palmer breeding sites. Litchfield declined from 1,000 Adélie nests in 1955 to explicit zero by 2007–2008. Dream declined from 10,000 Adélie nests in 1984 to 1,887 in 2008 while sparse Chinstrap observations increased from 46 in 1978 to 250 in 1998, although same-year community coverage was insufficient for a confirmatory replacement trajectory. At the non-island Biscoe Point benchmark, Adélie declined from 3,020 nests in 1971 to 408 in 2025, whereas gentoo changed from explicit zero in 1984 to 5,405 nests in 2025. By 2025 the observed focal composition at Biscoe Point was 93.0% gentoo, producing a fixed-centroid assembly shift of +25.1 mm in community-weighted flipper length and +1.28 kg in body mass. These endpoints demonstrate that local decline can end in vacancy, persistence, or species replacement rather than a single regional succession pathway.

### A near-common long-term decline coexisted with local annual dynamics

All five LTER islands were complete for all 27 census years from 1991 through 2017. The first principal component of island-standardized log abundance explained 96.4% of variance, with similar positive loadings across islands. Nevertheless, median pairwise annual-growth correlation was only 0.373. Additive variance decomposition attributed 53.5% of total log-abundance variance uniquely to year, 33.0% uniquely to island, and 13.5% to residual variation.

Decline endpoints were severe but unequal. The fraction of 1991 breeding pairs remaining in 2017 was 1.9% on Christine, 3.4% on Cormorant, 6.3% on Humble, 0% on Litchfield, and 0.7% on Torgersen. Litchfield reached zero breeding pairs in 2007 while the other four islands persisted at low abundance through 2017. Thus the regional signal dominated long-term direction, but local state strongly affected the trajectory and endpoint of collapse.

### Simple sea-ice-duration and snowfall mechanisms failed prospective tests

Adding preceding sea-ice season duration to island baselines produced only a small held-out MSE improvement (+0.00112) and a regional coefficient of -0.050, opposite the predeclared positive direction; the year-cluster bootstrap interval included zero. Adding the sea-ice × static habitat interaction worsened prediction. The predeclared low-frequency rescue also failed: the primary five-year sea-ice duration coefficient was -0.016 and worsened purged out-of-window prediction by 0.00129 MSE; fixed three- and seven-year windows were also negative.

The independent terrestrial-weather test likewise failed. October snowfall × snow-prone habitat modestly increased in-sample R² (0.252 to 0.287) but had a positive interaction coefficient (+0.073), opposite the predeclared negative direction, and worsened leave-one-year-out RMSE from 0.308 to 0.325. These results reject the tested formulations, not the ecological importance of sea ice or breeding habitat.

### Distributed colony structure carried modest next-year information

Effective colony number was positively associated with next-year demographic performance after island identity, current abundance, and secular year were controlled. The full-data standardized coefficient was +0.117, and leave-one-year-out MSE improved from 0.07311 to 0.07208 (gain +0.00103). The signal persisted after previous-year growth was added (coefficient +0.118; gain +0.00093) and was stronger when restricted to the four islands that remained extant (coefficient +0.120; gain +0.00354).

This signal was specific to the distribution of breeders rather than a generic colony-count effect. Active positive-colony count had a positive coefficient (+0.164) but worsened held-out prediction (gain -0.00179). Reported colony-row counts were highly stable within islands across the census (Christine 13–14, Cormorant 7, Humble 9, Litchfield 9, Torgersen 16–17), arguing against wholesale changes in colony coding as an obvious explanation.

### The historical “large breeding groups” prediction did not explain the positive network signal

The externally fixed >50-pair group metric improved held-out prediction more strongly than effective colony number (MSE gain +0.00406), but its standardized coefficient was -0.221, opposite the historical positive-direction hypothesis. The fixed sensitivity based on the fraction of breeders in >50-pair groups likewise improved prediction (+0.00167) but had a negative coefficient (-0.094). We therefore rejected the directional hypothesis that retaining more large breeding groups predicts better next-year growth after current abundance and year are controlled. The contrast indicates that effective colony number and large-group retention are not interchangeable descriptions of colony state.

## Discussion

### Regional decline and local collapse occupy different ecological scales

The Palmer LTER record reveals an unusually strong separation of timescales. Over nearly three decades, five neighboring Adélie populations moved in almost the same long-term direction, yet their annual growth rates were only moderately synchronized and their endpoints differed sharply. This combination is exactly what would be expected when a regional process constrains the broader population state but local breeding patches determine how that pressure is translated into persistence or extinction. The result is not that islands are independent; rather, island identity remains consequential even inside an overwhelmingly shared regional decline.

This distinction matters for interpreting climate responses. The 96.4% common long-term component could easily be treated as evidence for a single dominant environmental driver, but our prospectively bounded tests show that trajectory synchrony does not identify mechanism. The predeclared positive annual sea-ice-duration effect failed directionally, fixed 3–7-year duration summaries did not rescue it, and the annual snowfall × snow-prone-habitat interaction failed both directionally and predictively. Adélie ecology is unquestionably linked to sea ice and breeding habitat: previous Palmer work shows sea-ice control of phenology and geomorphology-dependent snow effects on breeding (Fraser et al. 2013; Cimino et al. 2019). Our result is narrower but important: those relationships do not imply that simple sea-ice-duration or October-snowfall indices explain year-to-year population growth or the common long-term decline in the tested formulations.

### Colony-network erosion identifies a distinct local state

The positive result emerged not from another external climate covariate but from the internal organization of the breeding population. Effective colony number asks whether the same island total is spread across many comparably occupied subcolonies or concentrated in a few dominant groups. Conditional on current abundance and year, a more distributed network carried modest information about better next-year performance. The effect was small, but it survived the addition of demographic momentum and strengthened when locally extinct Litchfield was removed. That pattern is consistent with effective colony number indexing a local vulnerability state that total population size misses.

Importantly, the specificity tests prevent a simplistic interpretation. Merely counting active colonies did not improve held-out prediction. Nor did the historical hypothesis that more >50-pair groups should be protective survive directional validation: those metrics predicted in the opposite direction once current abundance and time were controlled. The result therefore cannot be summarized as “large colonies are good” or even “more colonies are good.” What transfers is closer to the evenness or distributedness of breeders among subcolonies. In network language, local resilience appears related to the configuration of occupancy rather than a single node-size threshold.

### Why externally subsidized islands are useful ecological systems

The Palmer system suggests a broader form of island ecology for highly mobile consumers. Adélie penguins do not depend on food produced within the breeding island. Their principal prey are marine, and sympatric Adélie and gentoo penguins can partition shared prey spatially rather than relying on distinct island food resources (Pickett et al. 2018). This externalization of trophic resources changes what “island quality” means. Geographic isolation and area remain relevant, but breeding-surface configuration, snow accumulation, access to the ocean, and the internal distribution of breeders can matter independently of local food production.

That separation also helps explain why regional and local signals can coexist so strongly. Shared ocean forcing can synchronize the long-term direction across islands, while terrestrial conditions determine whether breeding activity remains distributed, becomes concentrated, or disappears. Biscoe Point adds another boundary condition: regional decline need not end in vacancy, because sites accessible to expanding species can undergo functional replacement instead. The island network therefore contains multiple forms of reassembly—persistence at low abundance, local extinction, and replacement—within one regional marine setting.

### Limitations and next tests

The supported colony-network result remains predictive rather than causal. Colony codes are observational breeding groups, not independently mapped habitat patches, and effective colony number is derived from the same census as population growth. Stable colony-row counts reduce concern that the result is driven by gross changes in coding, but external spatial validation is necessary before using the language of habitat fragmentation. The most informative next step is therefore not another climate window or alternative colony-size threshold. It is to map colony codes to independent topographic and snow-retention information and ask whether loss of effective colony number corresponds to contraction from specific parts of the breeding landscape.

The morphology and APBP analyses also have intentionally limited roles. The Palmer Penguins reanalysis is exploratory and cannot establish local adaptation. MAPPPD provides useful long-term endpoints but lacks synchronized multi-species coverage for replicated community-weighted trait trajectories across all true islands. Biscoe Point is a non-island benchmark and should not be pooled as an island replicate. These boundaries leave the principal result centered on the synchronized five-island Adélie record.

### Conclusion

Neighboring Palmer Adélie populations experienced a nearly common long-term collapse, but the path to collapse was local. The tested annual and low-frequency sea-ice-duration metrics and an annual snowfall × habitat interaction did not explain that local variation in their predeclared forms. Instead, a small but repeatable predictive signal lay in the distribution of breeders among within-island colonies. For marine-foraging island consumers, regional environmental forcing may determine the direction of change while the organization of terrestrial breeding networks helps determine local resilience. Island biogeography in such systems is therefore not only about whether a species reaches an island, but about whether the breeding landscape can continue to support a distributed reproductive network once it arrives.
